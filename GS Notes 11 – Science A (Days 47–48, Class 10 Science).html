Unit 1 – Laws of Motion (PDF p. 9–23)

  • Mechanics = effect of force on bodies → Statics (bodies at rest under forces) + Dynamics (moving bodies). Dynamics → Kinematics (motion without cause) + Kinetics (motion with cause).
  • Aristotle: natural state of bodies = rest; "natural motion" (force independent) vs "violent motion" (force dependent); said heavier body falls faster (wrong).
  • Galileo: natural state = rest or uniform motion; bodies resist change (= inertia); in vacuum all bodies fall at the same rate.
  • Inertia = inherent property of a body to resist change in its state of rest or uniform motion. Depends on mass. Types:
    • Inertia of rest – coin on cardboard falls into tumbler when card flicked; shaking a tree drops fruits.
    • Inertia of motion – athlete runs before long jump; passengers lean forward on sudden brake.
    • Inertia of direction – leaning sideways on a sharp turn.
  • Linear momentum p = m × v; vector; SI unit kg m s⁻¹, CGS g cm s⁻¹.
  • Newton's 1st law: body continues in rest/uniform motion unless acted on by external force – defines force and inertia (law of inertia).
  • Force = push or pull; vector. Like parallel forces (same direction); unlike parallel forces (opposite direction, e.g. tug of war).
  • Resultant force = vector sum; resultant zero → balanced forces (equilibrium); non-zero → unbalanced. Equilibrant = force equal and opposite to resultant.
  • Moment of force / Torque (τ) = F × d (perpendicular distance); vector; SI N m. Door handle kept far from hinges.
  • Couple = two equal, unlike parallel forces at different points; no translation, only rotation. Moment of couple M = F × S; unit N m (SI), dyne cm (CGS). E.g. turning a tap, winding a screw, spinning a top.
  • Convention: anticlockwise moment = positive, clockwise = negative.
  • Applications of torque: gears (change speed of rotation, transmit power; used to change speed of a car), see-saw, steering wheel.
  • Principle of moments: at equilibrium, clockwise moments = anticlockwise moments; F₁d₁ = F₂d₂.
  • Newton's 2nd law ("law of force"): force ∝ rate of change of momentum; F = ma. Uniform circular motion → centripetal acceleration/force.
  • Units of force: SI newton (N) = 1 kg m s⁻²; CGS dyne = 1 g cm s⁻²; 1 N = 10⁵ dyne. Gravitational units: 1 kgf = 9.8 N; 1 gf = 980 dyne.
  • Impulse (J) = F × t = change in momentum; unit N s or kg m s⁻¹. Impulsive force = large force for very short time. E.g. shock absorbers/springs in vehicles; cricket fielder pulls hands back while catching.
  • Newton's 3rd law: every action has equal and opposite reaction; act on two different bodies. E.g. bird flying, swimming, gun recoil.
  • Conservation of linear momentum: no net external force → total momentum constant; m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂.
  • Rocket propulsion: based on conservation of linear momentum + Newton's 3rd law; mass decreases → velocity increases; velocity enough to escape Earth's pull = escape velocity.
  • Newton's universal law of gravitation: F = G m₁m₂ / r²; always attractive; independent of medium. G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻².
  • Acceleration due to gravity (g): mean 9.8 m s⁻² on Earth; unit m s⁻² (= N kg⁻¹). g = GM/R². Radius of Earth R = 6378 km (≈ 6400 km). Mass of Earth M = gR²/G = 5.972 × 10²⁴ kg.
  • Variation of g: maximum at poles, minimum at equator; decreases with height and with depth; zero at Earth's centre. g at height h=R (distance 2R from centre) = g/4.
  • Mass = quantity of matter; SI kg; same everywhere. Weight = mg; vector, towards Earth's centre; SI newton (also kgf); more at poles than equator.
  • g on Moon = 1.625 m s⁻² (≈ 0.1654 × Earth's). 60 kg person: 588 N on Earth, 97.5 N on Moon; mass same.
  • Apparent weight (R) in a lift: up with acceleration a → R = m(g+a) (> W); down → R = m(g−a) (< W); at rest → R = mg; free fall (a = g) → R = 0 = weightlessness.
  • Astronauts float not because gravity is absent – they and the space station are in free fall around Earth (weightlessness).
  • Uses of gravitation law: measure mass/radius of Earth, g; discover new stars and planets; "Wobble" of stars → mass of star; explains geotropism of roots; predict paths of astronomical bodies.

Unit 2 – Optics (PDF p. 24–39)

  • Light = form of energy travelling as waves; path = ray, group of rays = beam. Objects emitting own light = luminous (all stars incl. Sun). We see an object only when light reflected from it reaches our eyes.
  • Properties of light: travels in a straight line; needs no medium (travels in vacuum); speed in vacuum/air c = 3 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; c = νλ; different colours → different λ and ν; in visible light violet = lowest wavelength, red = highest wavelength; at an interface light is partly reflected, partly refracted.
  • Refraction (ஒளிவிலகல்) = deviation of light passing obliquely from one transparent medium to another; due to difference in velocity of light in media (more in rarer, less in denser).
  • 1st law of refraction: incident ray, refracted ray and normal lie in the same plane.
  • 2nd law (Snell's law): sin i / sin r = ratio of refractive indices (μ₂/μ₁).
  • Refractive index μ = c / v (speed in vacuum / speed in medium); high μ → low speed. Denser → rarer: ray bends away from normal; rarer → denser: towards normal.
  • Monochromatic source = single colour; composite source = white light (Sun, mercury vapour lamp).
  • Dispersion = splitting of white light into component colours on refraction (glass, water); band = spectrum = VIBGYOR. Cause: angle of refraction differs for each colour – smallest for red, highest for violet → refractive index depends on wavelength.
  • Scattering of light = redirection of a light beam in all directions on interacting with particles; particle = scatterer.
    • Elastic scattering – incident and scattered energy same; Inelastic – not same.
    • Types by nature/size of scatterer: Rayleigh, Mie, Tyndall, Raman.
  • Rayleigh scattering = by atoms/molecules of atmospheric gases. Law: amount of scattering S ∝ 1/λ⁴. Blue (short λ) scattered most → blue sky; at sunrise/sunset light travels longer path, blue scattered away → Sun appears red.
  • Mie scattering – scatterer diameter ≈ or > wavelength; elastic; independent of wavelength; by pollen, dust, smoke, water droplets in lower atmosphere → white clouds.
  • Tyndall scattering / Tyndall effect – scattering by colloidal particles; e.g. path of sunbeam visible in a dusty room.
  • Do you know – Colloid: microscopically small substance evenly dispersed in another; e.g. milk, ice cream, muddy water, smoke.
  • Raman scattering / Raman effect – monochromatic light through gas/liquid/transparent solid; scattered light has additional frequencies (change in wavelength/frequency). Lines with same frequency as incident = Rayleigh lines; different = Raman lines; lower frequency = Stokes lines; higher = Antistokes lines. (extra: C.V. Raman, Nobel Physics 1930; 28 Feb = National Science Day.)
  • Lens = transparent medium bounded by two spherical surfaces, or one plane + one spherical.
    • Convex (bi-convex) – thicker at centre; converging lens.
    • Concave (bi-concave) – thinner at centre; diverging lens.
    • Plano-convex, plano-concave – one face plane.
  • Ray rules: (1) ray through optical centre – undeviated; (2) ray parallel to principal axis – converges to (convex) / appears to diverge from (concave) principal focus; (3) ray through/towards focus – emerges parallel to axis.

  • Microscope = instrument to see tiny objects; simple and compound.
  • Simple microscope = one convex lens of short focal length; object within F → erect, virtual, enlarged image at D = 25 cm. Used by watch repairers, jewellers; to read small letters; observe flower parts, insects; finger prints in forensic science.

Unit 3 – Thermal Physics (PDF p. 40–49)

  • Sun = primary source of thermal energy. Thermal energy is the cause, temperature is the effect.
  • Temperature (வெப்பநிலை) = degree of hotness of a body; also = average kinetic energy of molecules; decides whether a body is in equilibrium with surroundings and the direction of heat flow. Scalar; SI unit kelvin (K); other units °C, °F.
  • Absolute (kelvin) scale / thermodynamic temperature – measured from absolute zero; 1 K = 1/273.16 of the thermodynamic temperature of the triple point of water. A difference of 1 °C = 1 K. 0 K = −273 °C.
  • Conversions (book): K = °C + 273; K = (F + 460) × 5/9.
  • Thermal equilibrium = no net flow of heat between bodies → they are at the same temperature. Hot + cold in contact → heat flows hot → cold until equal.
  • Thermal energy / heat = energy transferred between bodies due to temperature difference; produces sensation of warmth. Transfer = conduction, convection, radiation. Heat is scalar; SI unit joule (J). Hot → cold flow is called heating (sometimes "cooling").
  • Features of heat transfer: (1) always from higher to lower temperature; (2) mass does not change on heating/cooling; (3) heat gained = heat lost.
  • Calorie = heat to raise 1 g of water by 1 °C; kilocalorie = heat to raise 1 kg of water by 1 °C. (extra: 1 cal ≈ 4.186 J.)
  • Effects of heat: temperature rises (∝ heat supplied; depends on nature and mass); change of state (solid → liquid → gas); expansion.
  • Thermal expansion = increase in dimension due to rise in temperature; all states expand. Solids expand least (rigid); gases expand most; liquids in between. E.g. mercury in thermometer.

  • Boyle's law: at constant temperature, volume of a fixed mass of gas ∝ 1/pressure; PV = constant.
  • Charles's law (law of volume) – by French scientist Jacques Charles: at constant pressure, V ∝ T (V/T = constant).
  • Avogadro's law: at constant P and T, V ∝ n (number of atoms/molecules). Avogadro number NA = 6.023 × 10²³ per mol.
  • Real gases – molecules attract each other with definite intermolecular force; behave as ideal at very high temperature or low pressure.
  • Ideal (perfect) gas – molecules do not interact; in practice no gas is ideal; obeys Boyle's, Charles's and Avogadro's laws.
  • Combined gas law: PV/nT = constant. With n = μNA: constant = Boltzmann constant kB = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹.
  • Ideal gas equation / equation of state: PV = RT (per mole; generally PV = μRT); R = μNAkB = universal gas constant = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • Points from evaluation: temperature and heat are scalar; on heating/cooling change in mass = zero; Boyle's law graph of P vs 1/V = straight line.

Unit 4 – Electricity (PDF p. 50–66)

  • Electricity = flow of electric charges through a conductor; commonly, a form of energy.
  • Electric current (மின்னோட்டம்) I = rate of flow of charges; I = Q/t. SI unit ampere (A): 1 A = 1 coulomb flowing through a cross-section in 1 second (1 A = 1 C/1 s). E.g. 12 C in 5 s → 2.4 A.
  • Direction: electrons flow from − to + terminal; conventional current = direction of positive charge = opposite to electron flow (from + to − terminal in the external circuit).
  • Electric circuit = closed conducting loop of components through which electrons flow; current flows only when the circuit is closed (bulb glows because closing the switch completes the circuit).

  • Electric potential at a point = work done in moving a unit positive charge from infinity to that point against electric force.
  • Potential difference between two points = work done in moving unit positive charge from one point to the other; V = W/Q; V = VA − VB. Charges flow from higher to lower potential.
  • Volt (V) = SI unit of potential and p.d.; 1 V = 1 joule of work per coulomb (1 J/1 C). E.g. 100 J for 10 C → 10 V.
  • Ohm's law (German physicist Georg Simon Ohm): at constant temperature, steady current through a conductor ∝ potential difference across its ends; V = IR; V–I graph = straight line.
  • Resistance R = property of a material to oppose flow of charge; R = V/I. SI unit ohm (Ω): 1 Ω when 1 A flows under 1 V. (30 V, 2 A → 15 Ω.)
  • Resistivity ρ (specific resistance): R ∝ L, R ∝ 1/A → R = ρL/A; ρ = resistance of conductor of unit length and unit cross-section area; unit ohm metre (Ω m); constant for a material.
  • Nichrome – conductor with highest resistivity, 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m → used in heating elements.
  • Conductance G = 1/R; unit ohm⁻¹ = mho. Conductivity σ = 1/ρ; unit ohm⁻¹ m⁻¹ (mho m⁻¹).
  • Conductors (copper, aluminium): high conductivity, low resistivity. Insulators (glass, wood, rubber): low conductivity, high resistivity.
  • Table 4.2 resistivity (Ω m): Copper 1.62 × 10⁻⁸; Nickel 6.84 × 10⁻⁸; Chromium 12.9 × 10⁻⁸; glass and rubber – extremely high (insulators).
  • Resistors in series: same current through each; single loop; one break stops all (used in flashlights). Rs = R₁ + R₂ + R₃; n equal resistors → nR; Rs > highest individual resistance. (5 + 3 + 2 = 10 Ω on 10 V → 1 A.)
  • Resistors in parallel: same p.d. across each; current divides; 1/Rp = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃; n equal → R/n; Rp < lowest individual resistance. House wiring is parallel.
  • Series–parallel circuit: parallel groups joined in series (Rtotal = RP1 + RP2). Parallel–series: series groups joined in parallel (1/R = 1/RS1 + 1/RS2).

  • Wires: live – red insulation; neutral – black; earth – green. Main fuse is on the live wire.
  • Two circuits: 5 A rating – low power (tube lights, bulbs, fans); 15 A rating – high power (AC, refrigerator, iron, heater). All household circuits are in parallel (independent; equal voltage to each).

  • Earthing – green earth wire from metal body of appliance to metal tube/electrode buried in Earth; low-resistance path; protects from electric shock.
  • LED bulb – semiconductor device emitting visible light; colour depends on material; Gallium arsenide, gallium phosphide → red, green, yellow, orange. Uses: digital watch/calculator displays, traffic signals, street lights, decorative lights.
  • Seven segment display – shows numbers/text (digital meters, clocks, microwave ovens); 7 LEDs (a–g) in shape of digit 8; an 8th LED for the dot.
  • Merits of LED bulb: no filament → no heat loss, cooler than incandescent; much lower power than fluorescent; eco-friendly; many colours; cost- and energy-efficient; no mercury or toxic materials; helps overcome energy crisis.
  • LED TV = actually an LCD TV with LED backlight; LED arrays act as pixels; white LEDs in monochrome TV, RGB LEDs in colour TV. First LED TV screen: James P. Mitchell, 1977 (monochrome); first commercial LED TV: SONY, 2009. Advantages: brighter, thinner, less power, longer life, more reliable.

Unit 5 – Acoustics (PDF p. 67–81)

  • Acoustics = branch of physics dealing with production, transmission, reception, control and effects of sound (not just music/auditoria).
  • Sound is produced by vibrations; it is energy carried as waves; needs a material medium (solid, liquid, gas) – cannot travel in vacuum (no sound heard on the Moon – no air).
  • Sound waves are longitudinal: particles vibrate along the direction of propagation → high-pressure compressions and low-pressure rarefactions.

  • Particle velocity = velocity of vibrating particles; wave velocity = distance travelled by the wave per unit time; v = λ/T = nλ; SI unit m s⁻¹.

  • Reflection of sound = bouncing back of sound at interface of two media (soft repetition of voice in an empty room). Laws: incident wave, normal and reflected wave in the same plane; ∠i = ∠r.
  • Acoustical wonder – Golconda fort (Hyderabad, Telangana): "Clapping portico" – series of arches each smaller than the previous; sound under the dome is compressed and bounced back amplified to a great distance.
  • Reflection at denser boundary (rigid wall): compression reflected as compression (direction reversed). At rarer boundary: compression reflected as rarefaction.
  • More to know (for sound): medium in which sound travels faster = rarer (water is rarer than air for sound); slower = denser.
  • Plane surface – intensity unchanged. Convex surface – diverges, intensity decreases. Concave – converges to a point. Parabolic surfaces in halls to focus sound. Elliptical surface – sound from one focus always reaches the other focus → whispering halls.
  • Whispering gallery: St. Paul's Cathedral, London – elliptical walls; multiple reflections.
  • Echo = sound reproduced by reflection from rigid surfaces (walls, mountains). No echo in a small room (conditions not met).
  • Conditions for echo: persistence of hearing = 0.1 s → minimum gap 0.1 s; d = v × 0.1/2 = v/20; for v = 344 m s⁻¹, minimum distance = 17.2 m.
  • Applications of echo: animals communicate/locate objects; obstetric ultrasonography (images of foetus; safe – no harmful radiation); measuring speed of sound.
  • Echo method (source of pulses, tape, receiver, stopwatch): speed = 2d/t.
  • Applications of reflection: sound board – concave board with speaker at focus (auditoria); ear trumpet – hearing aid, wide end collects, narrow end into ear; megaphone – horn-shaped, multiple reflections concentrate voice.
  • Doppler effect – observed and explained by Christian Doppler (1803–1853), Austrian mathematician and physicist: when there is relative motion between source and listener, the frequency heard differs from the original (train whistle pitch rises while approaching, falls while receding). Causes: listener moves, source moves, both move, or medium moves.
  • Apparent frequency (medium at rest): n′ = n (v + vL)/(v − vS) when moving towards each other (n′ > n); n′ = n (v − vL)/(v + vS) when moving apart (n′ < n). Source → still listener: n′ = nv/(v − vS); source away: nv/(v + vS); listener → still source: n(v + vL)/v; listener away: n(v − vL)/v. Wind W along sound: v → v + W; against: v − W.
  • Examples: 90 Hz source approaching at v/10 → 100 Hz; 500 Hz at 30 m s⁻¹ (v = 330) → 550 Hz.
  • No Doppler effect when: both at rest; distance between them constant; moving in mutually perpendicular directions; source at centre of circle along which listener moves.
  • Applications of Doppler effect: police measure speed of vehicles (EM wave reflected); tracking satellites (radio frequency drops as satellite moves away); RADAR (RAdio Detection And Ranging) – speed and location of aircraft; SONAR – speed of marine animals and submarines.
  • Evaluation facts: reflection does not change speed, frequency or wavelength; echo minimum distance when v = 500 m s⁻¹ → 25 m; earthquake waves are infrasonic (true); sound cannot travel in vacuum.

Unit 6 – Nuclear Physics (PDF p. 82–98)

  • History of the atom: Democritus (Greek, 400 BCE) – matter made of tiny indestructible atoms; John Dalton 1803 – elements consist of identical atoms; J.J. Thomson – cathode rays = electrons; Goldstein – positive rays, named protons by Rutherford; James Chadwick 1932 – neutron; Ernest Rutherford 1911 (British) – mass concentrated in the nucleus. Other particles: photon, meson, positron, neutrino.
  • Discovery of radioactivity: Henri Becquerel (French), 1896 – uranium compound exposed a photographic plate in a drawer → uranium is radioactive.
  • Marie Curie (Polish) and Pierre Curie – two years later found radioactivity in pitchblende (ore of uranium); discovered radium.
  • Uranium – named after planet Uranus; discovered by Martin Klaproth (German chemist) in pitchblende.
  • Radioactivity (கதிரியக்கம்) = nuclear decay of unstable nuclei with emission of α, β, γ rays; such elements = radioactive elements.
  • Natural radioactivity = spontaneous emission without human intervention; elements with atomic number > 82 (uranium, radium). Only two with Z < 82: technetium (Tc, 43) and promethium (Pm, 61). 29 radioactive substances discovered so far (mostly rare earth and transition metals).
  • Artificial / induced / man-made radioactivity – discovered by Irene Curie and F. Joliot, 1934; light elements (boron, aluminium) bombarded with α particles. Parent nucleus → daughter nucleus; bombarding particle = projectile, emitted = ejected particle; notation X(P,E)Y, e.g. ₄Be⁹(α, n)₆C¹².

  • Alpha decay: ₉₂U²³⁸ → ₉₀Th²³⁴ + ₂He⁴. Beta decay: ₁₅P³² → ₁₆S³² + ₋₁e⁰. Gamma decay: only energy level changes; Z and A same. Product element is identified by its atomic number, not mass number.
  • Nuclear fission (அணுக்கரு பிளவு) – Otto Hahn and F. Strassman (German), 1939: heavy nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei + few neutrons + large energy. ₉₂U²³⁵ + ₀n¹ → ₅₆Ba¹⁴¹ + ₃₆Kr⁹² + 3₀n¹ + Q. Energy per fission ≈ 3.2 × 10⁻¹¹ J ≈ 200 MeV.
  • Fissile (fissionable) materials: U-235, Pu-239, Pu-241. Natural uranium = 99.28 % U-238 + 0.72 % U-235; U-238 is non-fissionable.
  • Fertile materials (convertible to fissile): U-238, Th-232, Pu-240.
  • Chain reaction – self-propagating; neutrons multiply in geometric progression (1 → 3 → 9 → 27). Controlled (one neutron kept, extra absorbed) → nuclear reactor. Uncontrolled → atom bomb.
  • Critical mass (mc) = minimum mass of fissile material to sustain chain reaction (depends on nature, density, size). Less = subcritical, more = supercritical. 2–3 neutrons per fission; losses by leakage and absorption.
  • Atom bomb – uncontrolled chain reaction; two subcritical pieces joined by conventional explosive → supercritical → explosion with heat, light, γ radiation. Used 1945, Hiroshima and Nagasaki (World War II).
  • Hiroshima bomb = "Little Boy" – gun-type, uranium core. Nagasaki bomb = "Fat Man" – explosion type, plutonium core.
  • Nuclear fusion (அணுக்கரு இணைவு) – two light nuclei combine into a heavier one; ₁H² + ₁H² → ₂He⁴ + Q (₁H² = deuterium); energy ≈ 3.84 × 10⁻¹² J per fusion.
  • Electron volt: 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; 1 MeV = 10⁶ eV.
  • Mass defect – product mass less than parent masses; converted to energy by Einstein's mass–energy equivalence (1905): E = mc². (2 kg mass defect → 1.8 × 10¹⁷ J.)
  • Conditions for fusion: temperature 10⁷ to 10⁹ K and high pressure (to overcome repulsion of positive nuclei) → called thermonuclear reaction.
  • Stellar energy – fusion of hydrogen in cores of Sun and stars = source of their light and heat. Sun fuses about 620 million metric tons of hydrogen per second, radiates 3.8 × 10²⁶ J/s; reaching Earth ≈ 1.4 kJ per unit area per unit time. Proton–proton chain = fusion.
  • Hydrogen bomb – based on fusion; has an inbuilt atom bomb to create the temperature/pressure; far more energy than atom bomb.

Unit 7 – Atoms and Molecules (PDF p. 99–113)

  • Atom idea: first proposed by Greek philosophers (5th century BCE) – philosophical. First scientific atomic theory: John Dalton; later corrected by J.J. Thomson, Rutherford, Niels Bohr, Schrödinger → modern atomic theory.
  • Postulates of modern atomic theory:
    • Atom is divisible (electron, proton, neutron).
    • Atoms of same element may have different masses – isotopes (₁₇Cl³⁵, ₁₇Cl³⁷).
    • Atoms of different elements may have same mass – isobars (₁₈Ar⁴⁰, ₂₀Ca⁴⁰).
    • Atoms can be transmuted – not indestructible (artificial transmutation).
    • Atoms may not combine in simple whole-number ratio (glucose C₆H₁₂O₆ = 6:12:6; sucrose C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ = 12:22:11).
    • Atom = smallest particle taking part in a chemical reaction.
    • Mass of atom can be converted to energy (E = mc²).
  • Isotones = different elements with same number of neutrons (₆C¹³, ₇N¹⁴).
  • Mass number = protons + neutrons (electrons have negligible mass).
  • Atomic mass unit (amu) = 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom (6 p + 6 n); modern symbol U (unified atomic mass). Proton or neutron ≈ 1 amu.
  • Relative atomic mass (Ar) = average mass of isotopes of the element ÷ 1/12 mass of one C-12 atom; also called standard atomic weight; no unit. Standard history: hydrogen → oxygen → carbon-12 (now). Modern method: mass spectrometry (C-12 standard).
  • Gram atomic mass = atomic mass in grams: H 1 g, C 12 g, N 14 g, O 16 g.
  • Average atomic mass = weighted average of masses of naturally occurring isotopes × fractional abundance. Atomic masses in the periodic table are average atomic masses (also called atomic weight).
  • Carbon = 12.011 amu: C-12 98.90 % + C-13 1.10 %. Oxygen (most abundant element in Earth's crust and human body) – 3 isotopes, O-16 99.757 % → 15.999 amu. Boron: B-10 20 % + B-11 80 % → 10.8 amu.
  • Table 7.2 atomic masses (amu): H 1.008; He 4.003; Li 6.941; Be 9.012; B 10.811.
  • Molecule = two or more atoms held by chemical bonds. Except noble gases, atoms exist in combined form. All compounds are molecules, but all molecules are not compounds (e.g. O₂).
  • Homoatomic molecule = same kind of atoms; heteroatomic = different elements (a compound). Atomicity = number of atoms in a molecule: monoatomic, diatomic, triatomic, polyatomic (>3).
  • Examples: O₂ – homodiatomic; O₃ (ozone) – homotriatomic (oxygen's two allotropes: O₂, O₃); diatomic elements – H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂; HCl – heterodiatomic; H₂O – heterotriatomic; CO₂ – triatomic; P₄, S₈ – polyatomic.
  • Relative molecular mass = mass of one molecule ÷ 1/12 mass of C-12 atom; no unit; = sum of atomic masses. Gram molecular mass: H₂O 18 g; CO₂ 44 g; NH₃ 17 g; HCl 36.5 g; H₂SO₄ 98 g; Ca₃(PO₄)₂ 308 g (book, P = 30); glucose 180.

  • Mole (mol) (SI) = amount of substance with as many entities as atoms in exactly 12 g (0.012 kg) of C-12.
  • Avogadro's number NA = 6.023 × 10²³ – named after Italian scientist Amedeo Avogadro.
  • Mole concept = using mole as counting unit to express mass and volume of particles. 1 mole of atoms = gram atomic mass (1 mol O atoms = 16 g); 1 mole of molecules = gram molecular mass (1 mol O₂ = 32 g).
  • Molar volume = 22.4 litre (22400 ml) for 1 mole of any gas at STP (273.15 K, 1 atm).
  • Number of moles = mass/atomic (or molecular) mass = number of particles/6.023 × 10²³ = volume at STP/22.4 L. E.g. 46 g Na = 2 mol; 5.6 L O₂ = 0.25 mol; 14 g N₂ → 11.2 L; 4.4 g CO₂ → 2.24 L.
  • Percentage composition = mass of each element in 100 g of compound = (mass of element/molecular mass) × 100. H₂O: H 11.11 %, O 88.89 %; CH₄: C 75 %, H 25 %; S in H₂SO₄ = 32.65 %. Used to find empirical and molecular formula.
  • Avogadro's hypothesis (1811): "equal volumes of all gases under similar conditions of temperature and pressure contain equal number of molecules"; V ∝ n. H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl (1 vol + 1 vol → 2 vol) shows molecules can be subdivided.
  • Applications of Avogadro's law: explains Gay-Lussac's law; determines atomicity of gases; derives molecular formula of gases; relates molecular mass and vapour density; gives gram molar volume (22.4 L at STP).
  • Vapour density (V.D.) = mass of a volume of gas ÷ mass of equal volume of hydrogen (same T, P). Relative molecular mass = 2 × vapour density.
  • Atomicity = molecular mass/atomic mass.
  • Evaluation facts: 1 amu = 1/12 mass of C-12 atom; mass of 1 mole of N atoms = 14 g; smallest mass among choices = 1 atom of He; CO₂ is triatomic.

Unit 9 – Solutions (PDF p. 132–144)

  • Solution (கரைசல்) = homogeneous mixture of two or more substances (salt + water); sand + water = heterogeneous (separable by filtration). All solutions are mixtures, but not all mixtures are solutions.
  • Everyday solutions: sea water (dissolved salts), air (N₂, O₂, CO₂…), blood, lymph, urine, fruit juice, aerated drinks, tea, coffee; plants absorb nutrient solutions from soil. Smoke from burning wood = mixture of solid carbon + CO₂, CO.
  • Solute = component in lesser amount; solvent = larger amount (dissolving medium). Dissolution = uniform distribution of solute in solvent.
  • Binary solution = one solute + one solvent (CuSO₄ in water); ternary = three components (salt + sugar in water).

  • Water = "universal solvent". Aqueous solution – water is solvent (salt, sugar, CuSO₄ in water). Non-aqueous – alcohols, benzene, ethers, carbon disulphide, acetone as solvent; e.g. sulphur in CS₂ (insoluble in water), iodine in CCl₄.

  • Concentrated vs dilute – qualitative comparison of unsaturated solutions of same solute/solvent (sweeter tea = concentrated); judged by colour, density.
  • Solubility = grams of solute dissolving in 100 g of solvent to form saturated solution at given T and P; = (mass of solute/mass of solvent) × 100. Solubility of NaCl = 36 g at 25 °C; CaCO₃ = 0.0013 g/100 g water (nearly insoluble).
  • Factors affecting solubility: nature of solute and solvent, temperature, pressure.
    • "Like dissolves like" – polar (common salt) in polar (water); non-polar (fat) in non-polar (ether).
    • Temperature – solids in liquids: generally increases (more sugar in warm water); endothermic dissolution → solubility rises with temperature; exothermic → falls.
    • Temperature – gases in liquids: solubility decreases with temperature – bubbles when water is heated (O₂ escapes); aquatic animals thrive in cold regions (more dissolved oxygen).
    • Pressure – affects only gas in liquid; higher pressure → more soluble (carbonated soft drinks, aqueous ammonia cleaners, formalin = aqueous formaldehyde).
  • More to know – Henry's law: solubility of a gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the pressure of the gas over the solution at a definite temperature.
  • Concentration = amount of solute in given amount of solution/solvent.
  • Mass percentage (w/w) = mass of solute/(mass of solute + solvent) × 100; used for solid in liquid; independent of temperature. 5 % sugar = 5 g sugar + 95 g water. Used for ointments, antacids, soaps.
  • Volume percentage (v/v) = volume of solute/volume of solution × 100; both liquids; decreases with temperature (liquid expands). 10 % ethanol = 10 ml in 100 ml solution. Used for syrups, mouthwash, antiseptic solutions, household disinfectants.
  • Hydration – ions attract water molecules chemically in a fixed ratio. Water of crystallisation = number of water molecules in crystal; such salts = hydrated salts. Heating → lose water, become amorphous/lose colour.

Unit 10 – Types of Chemical Reactions (PDF p. 145–162)

  • Chemical reaction = breaking of old bonds and forming new bonds; new substances formed (petrol burns → CO₂ + H₂O; digestion, photosynthesis, combustion, rusting are chemical changes). Bond breaking absorbs energy; bond formation releases energy.
  • Word equation → chemical equation (reactants left, products right, arrow = direction) → balanced equation (law of conservation of matter: atoms equal on both sides), e.g. CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
  • More to know – state symbols: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous; 2K(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq) + H₂(g).
  • (a) Combination (synthesis/composition) reaction: A + B → AB; H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl.
    • Element + element: S + O₂ → SO₂ (non-metals); 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl (metal + non-metal).
    • Compound + element: PCl₃ + Cl₂ → PCl₅.
    • Compound + compound: SiO₂ + CaO → CaSiO₃.
    • Most combination reactions are exothermic.
  • White-washing: slaked lime Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ (air) → CaCO₃ (shiny finish after 2–3 days); marble is also CaCO₃.
  • (b) Decomposition reaction – single compound splits into simpler substances; needs energy (endothermic):
    • Thermal decomposition / thermolysis: 2HgO → 2Hg + O₂; CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂.
    • Electrolytic decomposition / electrolysis: 2NaCl → 2Na + Cl₂.
    • Photo decomposition / photolysis: 2AgBr → 2Ag + Br₂ (yellow → grey). All photodecomposition reactions are endothermic.

  • (c) Single displacement: element replaces another in a compound; Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂; Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu (copper deposits on iron nail). F₂ displaces Cl from NaCl, but Cl₂ cannot displace F (F more active). More active element displaces less active.

  • (d) Double displacement / metathesis – ions of two compounds interchange (like charges only); one product must be a precipitate or water.
    • Precipitation: Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2KI → PbI₂ (yellow precipitate) + 2KNO₃.
    • Neutralisation: acid + base → salt + water; NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O; HNO₃ + NH₄OH → NH₄NO₃ + H₂O. Neutralisation prevents tooth decay.
  • (e) Combustion – reactant combines rapidly with oxygen → oxides + heat; exothermic oxidation; with flame = burning. LPG = propane, butane, propylene; C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O + heat.

Irreversible – one direction only (burning wood; C + O₂ → CO₂). Physical changes (freezing/melting) are easily reversed. Most chemical reactions are irreversible.

  • Rate of reaction = change in amount/concentration of a reactant or product per unit time; Rate = −d[A]/dt = +d[B]/dt. Important for yield and slowing food spoilage.
  • Factors affecting rate:
    • Nature of reactants – Na reacts faster with HCl (strong acid) than acetic acid.
    • Concentration – granulated zinc faster with 2M HCl than 1M.
    • Temperature – higher temperature faster; food spoils slower in refrigerator.
    • Pressure – for gases, higher pressure faster.
    • Catalyst – increases rate without being consumed; MnO₂ speeds decomposition of potassium chlorate → KCl + O₂.
    • Surface area – powdered CaCO₃ reacts faster than marble chips.
  • Chemical equilibrium – in a reversible reaction, rate of forward = rate of backward; no net change in amounts. Possible only in a closed system. E.g. CaCO₃ ⇌ CaO + CO₂ in closed vessel.
  • Physical equilibrium – water ⇌ vapour in closed vessel (evaporation rate = condensation rate); volumes of phases constant.
  • Characteristics: forward rate = backward rate; observable properties (pressure, concentration, colour, density, viscosity) constant; dynamic equilibrium. Soda bottle: dissolved CO₂ ⇌ gaseous CO₂; opened bottle goes "flat".

Unit 11 – Carbon and its Compounds (PDF p. 163–180)

  • Carbon compounds are in food, medicines, clothes, fuels, paint, cosmetics; more than 5 million carbon compounds – more than any other element.
  • Unique nature of carbon: catenation (self-linking), tetravalency, multiple bonding; bonds are covalent; such compounds = organic compounds.
  • Characteristics of organic compounds: high molecular weight, complex structure; mostly insoluble in water, soluble in organic solvents (ether, CCl₄, toluene); highly inflammable; less reactive, slower reactions than inorganic; covalent; lower m.p./b.p.; show isomerism (one molecular formula, several compounds); volatile; can be made in laboratory.
  • Classification by carbon chain:
    • Acyclic / open chain – saturated (all single bonds, propane CH₃CH₂CH₃) or unsaturated (double/triple bond, propene CH₃CH=CH₂).
    • Cyclic – carbocyclic (homocyclic): alicyclic (cyclobutane) and aromatic (benzene – alternate double bonds); heterocyclic (ring with O, N, S – furan, pyridine).
  • Hydrocarbons = only C and H; parent organic compounds.
    • Alkanes – single bonds, saturated, CₙH₂ₙ₊₂; simplest methane CH₄.
    • Alkenes – C=C, unsaturated, CₙH₂ₙ; simplest ethylene (ethene) C₂H₄.
    • Alkynes – C≡C, CₙH₂ₙ₋₂; simplest acetylene (ethyne) C₂H₂.

  • Manufacture of ethanol – fermentation of molasses (by-product of sugar industry; dark syrup with ~30 % sucrose):
    • Dilute to 8–10 % sugar; add nitrogen source (ammonium sulphate/phosphate) if needed; add yeast, keep at 303 K for a few days.
    • Enzyme invertase: sucrose → glucose + fructose; enzyme zymase: glucose → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂.
    • Fermented liquid = "wash" (15–18 % alcohol) → fractional distillation → rectified spirit (95.5 % ethanol + 4.5 % water) → reflux over quicklime → absolute alcohol (100 %).
  • More to know: yeast = single-celled fungi; fermentation = conversion of complex organic molecules into simpler ones by enzymes (e.g. curdling of milk).

  • Ethanol – chemical:
    • Dehydration with conc. H₂SO₄ at 443 K → ethene.
    • With sodium → sodium ethoxide + H₂.
    • Oxidation with alkaline KMnO₄ or acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ → ethanoic acid; K₂Cr₂O₇ turns orange → green (test for alcohol).
    • Esterification: ethanol + ethanoic acid (conc. H₂SO₄) → ethyl ethanoate (ester, fruity odour).
    • Dehydrogenation over heated copper at 573 K → acetaldehyde.
    • Combustion: C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O (highly inflammable).
  • Uses of ethanol: medical wipes, antiseptic, sterilising wounds; anti-freeze in automobile radiators; hand sanitizers; solvent for drugs, oils, perfumes, dyes; food flavour (vanilla extract).
  • Ethanol mixtures: methylated spirit = 95 % ethanol + 5 % methanol; rectified spirit = 95.5 % ethanol + 4.5 % water; power alcohol = petrol + ethanol; denatured spirit = ethanol + pyridine.
  • Ethanoic acid (acetic acid) CH₃COOH, molecular formula C₂H₄O₂ – made by oxidising ethanol (alkaline KMnO₄/acidified K₂Cr₂O₇).
  • Physical: colourless, unpleasant odour, sour; miscible with water; b.p. higher than corresponding alcohols, aldehydes, ketones; pure acid freezes into ice-like flakes → glacial acetic acid.
  • Chemical: with Na, Zn → ethanoate + H₂; with Na₂CO₃/NaHCO₃ → CO₂ (brisk effervescence); with NaOH → sodium ethanoate + water; decarboxylation – sodium ethanoate + soda lime (3 NaOH : 1 CaO) → methane + Na₂CO₃.
  • Uses of ethanoic acid: vinegar (dilute) – food additive, flavouring, preservative; plastics; dyes, pigments, paints; fabric printing; lab reagent; coagulating rubber from latex; pharmaceuticals.
  • Organic compounds in daily life: hydrocarbons – LPG, petrol, kerosene, tyres, plastic; alcohols – solvent, antiseptic; aldehydes – formaldehyde as disinfectant; ketones – solvent, stain remover; ethers – anaesthetic, pain killer; esters – in all cooking oils and lipids.
  • Soaps and detergents contain surfactants (break surface tension).
  • Soap = sodium or potassium salts of long-chain fatty (carboxylic) acids; raw materials: fat + alkali (NaOH; KOH gives more water-soluble soap). Hard soap – with caustic soda (NaOH), for washing. Soft soap – with potassium salts, for bathing.
  • Kettle process (oldest): saponification – oil + 10 % alkali boiled with steam in iron kettle (hydrolysis); salting out – common salt added, soap rises as curdy mass.

  • Factors affecting photosynthesis: internal – pigments, leaf age, carbohydrate accumulation, hormones; external – light, CO₂, temperature, water, mineral elements.
  • Info bit: artificial photosynthesis produces renewable energy from sunlight; C.N.R. Rao (Bharat Ratna 2013) works on artificial photosynthesis for hydrogen fuel.
  • Mitochondria – first discovered by Kolliker (1857) in striated muscles; produce ATP → "Power house of the cell" / ATP factory; size 0.5–2.0 µm; 60–70 % protein, 25–30 % lipids, 5–7 % RNA, small DNA.
    • Outer membrane – smooth, freely permeable; porin channels. Inner membrane – semi-permeable; 80 % proteins; each membrane 60–70 Å thick.
    • Cristae – finger-like folds of inner membrane (more surface area). Oxysomes (F₁ particles) – tennis-racket shaped; ATP synthesis.
    • Matrix – Krebs-cycle enzymes, 70 S ribosomes, tRNA, mitochondrial DNA.
    • Functions: cell respiration; maintain calcium ion concentration; regulate metabolism.
  • External respiration = gas exchange with environment (physical). Cellular respiration = oxidation of food in cells (biochemical).
  • Aerobic respiration: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ATP. Stages:
    • Glycolysis – glucose (6C) → 2 pyruvic acid (3C); in cytoplasm; first step of both aerobic and anaerobic.
    • Krebs cycle / TCA (tricarboxylic acid) cycle – in mitochondrial matrix.
    • Electron transport chain – on inner mitochondrial membrane; NADH + H⁺ and FADH₂ oxidised; ADP → ATP = oxidative phosphorylation; O₂ = final electron acceptor, reduced to water.
  • Anaerobic respiration – without oxygen: glucose → ethanol + CO₂ (yeast) or lactic acid (bacteria).
  • Respiratory quotient (RQ) = volume of CO₂ liberated / volume of O₂ consumed.

Rabbit

  • Habit: gentle, timid; leaping; live in burrows; worldwide; herbivorous (grass, turnips, carrots, lettuce); gregarious. Pygmy rabbit listed threatened in Washington 1990; Columbia Basin pygmy rabbit federally endangered March 2003 (habitat loss).
  • Morphology: ~45 cm, ~2.25 kg; fur; head, neck, trunk, tail. Head – mouth, nostrils, eyes, vibrissae (whiskers), large movable pinnae. Trunk = thorax + abdomen; females 4–5 pairs of teats; pentadactyl limbs with claws, forelimbs shorter. Short tail signals danger.
  • Integument gives hairs, claws, nails, sweat, sebaceous and mammary glands (modified skin glands); sweat and sebaceous glands regulate temperature.
  • Coelom: thoracic and abdominal cavities separated by the diaphragm – characteristic feature of mammals; diaphragm movement brings breathing.
  • Digestive system: mouth → buccal cavity (tongue, teeth) → pharynx → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine → caecum (bacteria digest cellulose) → large intestine (colon, rectum) → anus. Glands: salivary, gastric, liver, pancreas, intestinal.
  • Dentition: diphyodont (milk + permanent teeth); heterodont (different types: I, C, PM, M). Dental formula of rabbit: I 2/1, C 0/0, PM 3/2, M 3/3 = 2033/1023; canines absent; gap between incisors and premolars = diastema (helps chewing in herbivores).
  • Respiration: pair of spongy lungs with double pleura; nostrils → pharynx → glottis → larynx (voice box, 4 cartilage plates, vocal cords) → trachea (cartilage rings) → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli. Epiglottis stops food entering trachea. Inspiration active, expiration passive.
  • Heart: pear-shaped, in pericardium; 4 chambers (2 auricles, 2 ventricles); right AV aperture – tricuspid valve; left – bicuspid (mitral) valve; pulmonary artery and aorta – 3 semilunar valves. Right auricle gets deoxygenated blood via 2 precavals + 1 postcaval; left auricle gets oxygenated blood via pulmonary veins; right ventricle → pulmonary trunk → lungs; left ventricle → systemic arch (aorta).
  • Nervous system: CNS (brain + spinal cord); PNS – 12 pairs cranial + 37 pairs spinal nerves; ANS – sympathetic + parasympathetic. Brain membranes: duramater (outer), arachnoid (middle), piamater (inner). Forebrain (olfactory lobes, cerebral hemispheres joined by corpus callosum, diencephalon); midbrain (optic lobes); hindbrain (cerebellum, pons varolii, medulla oblongata).
  • Excretory: dark red bean-shaped kidneys of nephrons; excrete urea; ureters → urinary bladder → urethra.
  • Reproduction: sexual dimorphism, separate sexes. Male – pair of ovoid testes in scrotal sacs (seminiferous tubules → epididymis → vas deferens → urethra → penis); accessory glands: prostate, Cowper's, perineal. Female – ovaries behind kidneys; oviduct (fallopian tube) → uterus → vagina → vestibule (urinogenital canal) → vulva; Cowper's and perineal glands.

Unit 14 – Transportation in Plants and Circulation in Animals (PDF p. 208–225)

  • Xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries food. Translocation = bulk movement of substances through vascular tissue. Animal transport = circulatory system (blood, lymph, heart, vessels).
  • Diffusion – movement of molecules from higher to lower concentration without energy (passive).
  • Active transport – uses energy (ATP); membrane proteins ("pumps") move molecules from low to high concentration ("uphill").
  • Osmosis – movement of solvent/water from higher to lower (water) concentration through a semi-permeable membrane; passive. Demonstrated with thistle funnel + sucrose solution.
  • Plasmolysis – plant cell in hypertonic solution loses water; protoplasm shrinks from cell wall.
  • Imbibition – solid plant material absorbs water and swells (dry seeds, raisins/grapes); helps seedlings emerge.
  • Root hairs – thin, slender extensions of epidermal cells (unicellular); increase absorbing surface. Water: root hair → cortex (osmosis) → xylem → stem, leaves.
  • Apoplast pathway – through cell walls and intercellular spaces (no membrane crossing). Symplast pathway – through plasma membrane, cytoplasm, plasmodesmata; slower.
  • Transpiration = evaporation of water from aerial parts, mainly through stomata (open by day, closed at night). Guard cells turgid → stoma opens; flaccid → closes. Loss of water creates transpiration pull.
  • Factors: external – temperature, light, humidity, wind; internal – number/distribution of stomata, % open, water status, canopy.
  • Significance of transpiration: transpiration pull; water for photosynthesis; mineral transport; cools leaves; keeps cells turgid.
  • Root pressure – active pumping of ions into xylem draws water; pushes water up to some extent (base of stem). Guttation (More to know) – water drops on grass leaf tips in humid mornings due to root pressure, through hydathodes.
  • Mineral uptake – mostly active absorption (ions charged; soil concentration lower than root) using ATP; then carried by transpiration pull.
  • Remobilisation – from old to young leaves (deciduous plants): P, S, N, K easily mobilised; calcium not.
  • Phloem – sieve tubes with sieve plates; carries sucrose from source (leaf) to sink (use/storage); bidirectional. Xylem flow is unidirectional (upward).
  • Pressure flow hypothesis – sucrose actively loaded into companion cells and sieve tubes → hypertonic → water enters from xylem → sap flows to low-pressure sink → sucrose unloaded.
  • Ascent of sap = upward movement of water and minerals: osmosis into root hairs → root pressure → capillary action → cohesion (attraction between water molecules) + adhesion (water to xylem walls) form continuous column → transpiration pull (main force; works even in tallest trees).
  • Blood = red fluid connective tissue; plasma (slightly alkaline, ~55 %; proteins, glucose, urea, enzymes, hormones, vitamins, minerals) + formed elements.
  • RBC (erythrocytes) – most abundant cells; formed in bone marrow; red due to haemoglobin; mature mammalian RBC has no nucleus or organelles; biconcave disc; life span ~120 days; carries O₂. No nucleus → more surface for O₂; no mitochondria → all O₂ delivered; no ER → flexible in capillaries.
  • WBC (leucocytes) – colourless, nucleated, no haemoglobin; in bone marrow, spleen, thymus, lymph nodes; amoeboid.

  • Human heart – between lungs, tilted left, above diaphragm; cardiac muscle; in double-walled pericardium with pericardial fluid. Wall = endocardium, myocardium, epicardium.

  • Heartbeat = one systole (contraction) + diastole (relaxation); normal 72–75 per minute.
  • Neurogenic heart (impulse from nerve ganglion) – annelids, most arthropods; myogenic (modified muscle) – molluscs, vertebrates. Human heart is myogenic.
  • SA node (sino-atrial) – in right atrium wall near superior vena cava – pacemaker. Impulse → atria → AV node → AV bundle (Bundle of His, discovered by His, 1893) → Purkinje fibres → ventricles.
  • Pulse – expansion of artery with each beat (felt at wrist); normal 70–90 per min.
  • Cardiac cycle ≈ 0.8 s: atrial systole 0.1 s, ventricular systole 0.3 s, ventricular diastole 0.4 s.
  • Heart sounds: LUBB – longer, closure of tricuspid and bicuspid valves at start of ventricular systole; DUPP – shorter, closure of semilunar valves at end of ventricular systole.
  • Blood pressure = force of blood on artery walls; highest in arteries, lowest in veins. Systolic (peak, ventricular contraction) / diastolic (lowest) = 120/80 mm Hg normal. Persistent high = hypertension (risk of heart attack, stroke); low = hypotension.
  • Stethoscope – hears sounds of internal organs (chest). Sphygmomanometer – measures BP of brachial artery at rest; manometric and digital types.

Unit 15 – Nervous System (PDF p. 226–236)

  • Stimulus = change in environment detected by receptors; response = reaction (withdrawing hand from hot object, closing eyes in bright light). Effector = part that responds (muscle, gland).
  • Coordination = organs working together systematically; maintains physiological balance = homeostasis.
  • Nervous system = nervous tissue: neurons, neuroglia, nerve fibres.
  • Neuron (நரம்பு செல்) = structural and functional unit of nervous system; longest cell in human body (over 100 mm); conducts electrical impulses. Neurons cannot divide.
  • Neuroglia (glial cells) – non-excitable supporting cells; do not conduct impulses. Nerve fibres – long processes of neurons; bundled into nerves.
  • Structure of neuron:
    • Cyton (cell body / perikaryon) – nucleus, neuroplasm, Nissl's granules, mitochondria, ribosomes, lysosomes, ER, neurofibrils.
    • Dendrites – branched; carry impulses towards cyton.
    • Axon – single long process; carries impulses away from cyton; ends in synaptic knobs; membrane = axolemma, cytoplasm = axoplasm; covered by myelin sheath (insulator, faster conduction) and neurilemma (Schwann cells); gaps = nodes of Ranvier, between = internode.
  • Synapse – junction between synaptic knob of one neuron and dendrite of next; impulse passes by chemicals = neurotransmitters; main one = acetylcholine.

  • Meninges (protect brain): duramater – outermost, tough fibrous; arachnoid – middle, thin, web-like ("spider"); piamater – innermost, delicate, rich blood supply. Meningitis = inflammation of meninges (mostly viral/bacterial).
  • Brain = forebrain + midbrain + hindbrain.
  • Cerebrum – largest part, about two-thirds of brain; two hemispheres split by median cleft, joined by corpus callosum; outer cerebral cortex = grey matter, inner medulla = white matter; folds = gyri, grooves = sulci; lobes – frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital. Functions: thinking, intelligence, consciousness, memory, imagination, reasoning, will power.
  • Diencephalon: thalamus – relay centre for sensory and motor signals; hypothalamus – controls hunger, thirst, sleep, sweating, sexual desire, anger, fear, water balance, BP; thermoregulatory centre; controls anterior pituitary – link between nervous and endocrine systems.
  • Midbrain – corpora quadrigemina (4 rounded bodies) – visual and auditory reflexes.
  • Hindbrain:
    • Cerebellum – second largest; two hemispheres + vermis; coordinates voluntary movements, maintains balance/posture.
    • Pons (Latin "bridge") – connects cerebellar lobes; relays signals; controls respiration and sleep cycle.
    • Medulla oblongata – posterior-most, joins spinal cord; cardiac, respiratory, vasomotor centres; regulates vomiting and salivation.
  • Box: human brain is nearly 60 % fat; Essential Fatty Acids (EFAs) cannot be synthesised – from fish, green leafy vegetables, almond, walnut.
  • Spinal cord – in neural canal of vertebral column; covered by meninges; from medulla to first lumbar vertebra; ends as filum terminale; central canal with CSF; grey matter H-shaped (inside), white matter outside; dorsal (afferent) root + ventral (efferent) root → spinal nerve. Conducts impulses and controls reflex actions.
  • More to know – EEG (Electroencephalogram): records electrical impulses of brain; detects seizures, epilepsy, brain tumours, head injuries.
  • Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) – lymph-like fluid around brain and in central canal; shock absorber; supplies nutrients; removes wastes; keeps cranial pressure constant.
  • Reflex action = automatic response without consciousness. Simple (basic) – inborn, unlearned: blinking, sneezing, coughing, yawning. Acquired (conditioned) – learned by practice: playing harmonium on seeing a note. Most are controlled by spinal cord = spinal reflexes.
  • Reflex arc (touching hot pan): receptor (thermoreceptor) → sensory neuron → spinal cord → relay neuron → motor neuron → effector (arm muscle).
  • PNS: 12 pairs of cranial nerves (sensory e.g. optic nerve; motor – eyeball rotation, iris muscles, tear gland) and 31 pairs of spinal nerves (dorsal sensory root – impulse towards cord; ventral motor root – away).
  • ANS (visceral nervous system) – controls involuntary functions of visceral organs through two antagonistic parts: sympathetic and parasympathetic.

Unit 16 – Plant and Animal Hormones (PDF p. 237–250)

  • Hormone – from Greek "hormon" = to excite. Plant hormones = phytohormones – organic molecules at extremely low concentration controlling morphological, physiological and biochemical responses.
  • Five major plant hormones: growth promoters – auxins, cytokinins, gibberellins; growth inhibitors – abscisic acid (ABA), ethylene.
  • Auxins (Gk auxein = to grow) – first plant hormones discovered; term by Kogl and Haagen-Smit (1931); produced at tips of stems and roots, move to zone of elongation.
    • Charles Darwin (1880) – curvature of canary grass (Phalaris canariensis) coleoptiles; an "influence" from the tip, later identified as auxin by Went.
    • Frits Warmolt Went (1903–1990), Dutch – experiments on Avena coleoptiles with agar blocks; named the chemical "auxin".
    • Natural auxins: IAA (indole-3-acetic acid), PAA (phenyl acetic acid), IAN (indole-3-acetonitrile). Synthetic: 2,4-D (2,4-dichlorophenoxy acetic acid), IBA, indole-3-propionic acid, NAA, 2,4,5-T.
    • Effects: stem/coleoptile elongation; root formation at low concentration (inhibits at high); apical dominance (apical bud suppresses lateral buds); parthenocarpy – seedless fruits without fertilisation (watermelon, grapes, lime); prevents abscission layer.
  • Cytokinins (cytos = cell, kinesis = division) – promote cell division; first isolated from herring fish sperm; zeatin from Zea mays; abundant in liquid endosperm of coconut.
    • Effects: cell division (with auxin); cell enlargement; with auxin forms organs from callus in tissue culture (morphogenesis); lateral bud growth despite apical bud; delays ageing = Richmond Lang effect.
  • Gibberellins – most abundant plant hormones. Kurosawa (1926) – Bakanae / foolish seedling disease of rice caused by fungus Gibberella fujikuroi; active substance = gibberellic acid.
    • Effects: extraordinary internode elongation (corn, pea); bolting – sudden shoot elongation + flowering in rosette plants; male flowers in monoecious plants (cucurbits); breaks dormancy of potato tubers; better than auxins at parthenocarpy (tomato).
  • Abscisic acid (ABA) – growth inhibitor; regulates abscission and dormancy; "stress hormone"; found in chloroplasts.
    • Effects: promotes abscission (shedding of leaves, flowers, fruits); stomatal closure in drought; senescence (chlorophyll loss); bud dormancy before winter (birch); inhibits lateral buds in tomato.
  • Ethylene – gaseous hormone; growth inhibitor; fruit ripening (max during ripening of apple, banana, melon; ripens tomato, apple, mango, banana); inhibits stem/root elongation in dicots; hastens senescence; abscission zone formation (premature shedding); breaks dormancy of buds, seeds, storage organs. Activity: a ripe banana in a bag ripens unripe tomatoes (ethylene).
  • Endocrine (ductless) glands – secrete hormones in minute amounts into blood → act on target organs. Exocrine glands have ducts – salivary, mammary, sweat glands.
  • Endocrinology – study of endocrine glands; Thomas Addison = Father of Endocrinology. Term "hormone" introduced by English physiologists W.M. Bayliss and E.H. Starling (1909); first hormone discovered = secretin.
  • Human endocrine glands: pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid, pancreas (islets of Langerhans), adrenal (cortex + medulla), gonads (testes, ovary), thymus.
  • Pituitary (hypophysis) – pea-shaped; at base of brain attached to hypothalamus by pituitary stalk; anterior lobe (adenohypophysis) + posterior lobe (neurohypophysis); intermediate lobe absent in humans; controls other glands → "Master gland".
  • More to know – Melatonin: from pineal gland; "time messenger" – signals night-time; light at night (short wavelength) lowers it → sleep disturbance.

  • Parathyroid – four small oval glands on back of thyroid lobes; chief cells secrete parathormone; acts on bone, kidney, intestine.

Unit 17 – Reproduction in Plants and Animals (PDF p. 251–268)

  • Reproduction = producing more of own kind; ensures continuity – self-perpetuation. Sexual reproduction = fusion of male and female gametes carrying genes on chromosomes.
  • Plants reproduce by vegetative, asexual and sexual methods.
  • Vegetative reproduction – new plants from somatic parts; only mitosis, no gamete fusion; offspring genetically similar.
    • Leaves – Bryophyllum (plantlets at leaf notches).
    • Stems – strawberry (weak aerial stems root at ground).
    • Roots – tuberous roots of asparagus, sweet potato.
    • Bulbils – modified flower buds – Agave.
    • Fragmentation – filamentous alga Spirogyra.
    • Fission – Amoeba (one cell → two).
    • Budding – yeast.
    • Regeneration – Hydra, Planaria.
  • Asexual reproduction – single parent, no gametes; mitosis only, no meiosis; offspring exact copies. Commonest = spore formation (fungi, bacteria): sporangium on fungal hypha – e.g. Rhizopus (bread mould).
  • Flower = modified shoot of limited growth; 4 whorls on thalamus: calyx (sepals), corolla (petals) – accessory/non-essential; androecium (stamens) and gynoecium/pistil (carpels) – essential whorls.
  • Stamen = filament + anther (pollen in pollen sacs). Pollen grain: outer hard exine with germ pores; inner thin intine (cellulose + pectin); mature pollen = vegetative cell + generative cell (divides by mitosis → 2 male gametes).
  • Gynoecium = ovary, style, stigma; ovary contains ovules.
  • Ovule: nucellus enclosed by two integuments with opening micropyle; stalk funiculus; base chalaza. Embryo sac – 7 cells, 8 nuclei: egg apparatus at micropylar end (1 egg + 2 synergids), 3 antipodal cells at chalazal end, 2 polar nuclei at centre (secondary nucleus).
  • Pollination (மகரந்தச் சேர்க்கை) = transfer of pollen from anther to stigma; leads to fertilisation, fruits, seeds; cross pollination gives new varieties.
  • Self-pollination (autogamy) – same flower or another flower of same plant (Hibiscus). Advantages: possible in bisexual flowers; no agent needed; no pollen wastage. Disadvantages: fewer seeds; minute endosperm, weak plants; no new varieties.
  • Cross pollination – to flower of another plant of same species (apple, grapes, plum). Advantages: better plants, new varieties, more viable seeds. Disadvantages: distance barrier; pollen wastage; unwanted characters; depends on agents.

Unit 18 – Genetics (PDF p. 269–281)

  • "Like begets like"; inheritable traits – eye colour, hair colour, nose shape, earlobe type.
  • Genetics (மரபியல்) = study of genes, genetic variation and heredity. Heredity = transmission of characters to next generation; variation = differences among individuals of same species/same parents.
  • Gregor Johann Mendel (1822–1884) – Austrian monk, Father of Genetics; born 1822 in a farming family in Silesia (Czechoslovakia); entered Augustinian monastery at Brunn at 18; trained at University of Vienna; returned 1854. Experiments on garden pea for ~7 years (1856–1863), nearly 10,000 plants of 34 varieties; chose 7 pairs of contrasting characters.

  • Why pea? naturally self-pollinating (pure lines); short life span (annual); easy to cross-pollinate; distinct contrasting characters; bisexual flowers.
  • Monohybrid cross (one pair of characters): pure tall (TT) × pure dwarf (tt) → F1 all tall (Tt, monohybrids) → selfing → F2 tall : dwarf = 3 : 1 (Mendel got 787 tall, 277 dwarf). Phenotypic ratio 3:1; genotypic ratio 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt = 1:2:1.
  • Phenotype = external expression of a trait; genotype = genetic constitution. Mendel's "factors" = genes. Homozygous = alike factors (TT, tt); heterozygous = unlike (Tt).
  • Alleles = two factors of a contrasting pair (one from each parent); their phenotypic expressions = allelomorphs. Expressed one = dominant; masked = recessive. Each gamete gets only one factor.
  • Dihybrid cross (two pairs): round-yellow (RRYY) × wrinkled-green (rryy) → F1 all round-yellow (RrYy) → F2 9 round-yellow : 3 round-green : 3 wrinkled-yellow : 1 wrinkled-green = 9:3:3:1 (dihybrid ratio). New combinations: round-green and wrinkled-yellow.
  • Mendel's laws:
    • Law of dominance: when two homozygous individuals with contrasting characters are crossed, the characters appearing in F1 are dominant, those not appearing are recessive.
    • Law of segregation / purity of gametes: the two factors of an allelic pair stay together without mixing in the F1 hybrid and separate during gamete formation, so only one enters each gamete.
    • Law of independent assortment: when two or more pairs of characters are inherited together, factors of one pair assort independently of the other (basis of 9:3:3:1).
  • More to know: T.H. Morgan – Nobel Prize for role of chromosomes in heredity (book prints 1993; actual year 1933 – extra).
  • Chromosomes – thread-like structures in nucleus; term coined by Waldeyer (1888); condensed chromatin with DNA; carriers of heredity. Gene = DNA segment for a character; position on chromosome = locus.
  • Structure of chromosome: two sister chromatids joined at centromere; chromatid = coiled chromonema with bead-like chromomeres; made of DNA, RNA, histone and non-histone proteins, metal ions.
    • Primary constriction = centromere – where spindle fibres attach.
    • Secondary constriction = nucleolar organiser (forms nucleolus).
    • Telomere = chromosome end; prevents joining; gives stability; shortens with each division – "ageing clock".
    • Satellite = knob-like appendage; such chromosomes = sat-chromosomes.
  • By centromere position: telocentric (terminal; rod), acrocentric (near one end; short + long arm; rod), submetacentric (near centre; unequal arms; J or L shape), metacentric (centre; equal arms; V shape).
  • Autosomes – somatic characters (equal in both sexes). Allosomes = sex chromosomes (hetero-chromosomes) X, Y; male XY, female XX.
  • Human karyotype: 23 pairs (46) – 22 pairs autosomes + 1 pair sex chromosomes. Body cells diploid (2n); gametes haploid (n). Karyotype = number, size, shape of chromosomes; idiogram = its diagram (metaphase chromosomes in decreasing length, sex chromosomes last).
  • DNA structure – double helix model by James Watson and Francis Crick, using X-ray diffraction data of Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins; Nobel Prize (Medicine) 1962 to Watson, Crick, Wilkins.
  • DNA = polynucleotide; nucleotide = deoxyribose sugar + nitrogenous base + phosphate. Purines – adenine, guanine; pyrimidines – cytosine, thymine. Nucleoside = base + sugar; nucleotide = nucleoside + phosphate.
  • Watson–Crick model: two antiparallel polynucleotide chains; sugar-phosphate backbone, bases inside; purine always pairs with pyrimidine – A = T (two hydrogen bonds), C ≡ G (three hydrogen bonds) = complementary base pairing; one turn = 34 Å (3.4 nm) with 10 base pairs; nucleotides joined by phosphodiester bonds.
  • More to know – Chargaff's rule: in DNA, adenine = thymine and guanine = cytosine.
  • DNA replication: origin of replication → replication fork; helicase separates strands; topoisomerase removes twists; RNA primer (by primase); DNA polymerase adds nucleotides; leading strand continuous, lagging strand in short Okazaki fragments joined by DNA ligase; ends at terminus.
  • Significance of DNA: transmits hereditary information; information for protein formation; controls development and life activities.
  • Sex determination in humans: eggs all 22+X → females homogametic; sperms 22+X or 22+Y in equal numbers → males heterogametic. X-sperm + egg → 44+XX female; Y-sperm + egg → 44+XY male. Father's sperm decides the sex of the child; mother is not responsible.
  • Mutation – term by Hugo de Vries (1901) from evening primrose Oenothera lamarckiana; inheritable sudden change in DNA. Types: chromosomal and gene mutation.
  • Chromosomal mutation: structural – deletion, duplication, inversion, translocation; numerical (ploidy):
    • Euploidy – whole extra sets: triploidy (3n) – usually sterile (triploid oyster); tetraploidy (4n) – larger fruits and flowers in plants.
    • Aneuploidy – loss/gain of one or more chromosomes: monosomy (2n−1), trisomy (2n+1), nullisomy (2n−2).
  • Down's syndrome – identified by Langdon Down, 1866; trisomy 21 (extra chromosome 21 → 47 chromosomes); mental retardation, delayed development, behavioural problems, weak muscle tone, vision/hearing defects.
  • Gene (point) mutation – change in nucleotide sequence (substitution, deletion, insertion, inversion of bases) → abnormal protein. Sickle cell anaemia – single-gene mutation altering haemoglobin protein → sickle-shaped RBC.

  • Big Bang theory – universe began with an explosion about 15 billion years ago (book); galaxies of stars, gas, dust; solar system formed when gas clouds collapsed by gravity (Milky Way galaxy). Earth formed ~4.5 billion years ago; life appeared ~500 million years after Earth formed.

  • Evidences of evolution – all organisms evolved from common ancestors.
  • Homologous organs – common ancestry and development, different functions: forelimbs of human (hand), cat (front leg), whale (flipper), bat (wing) – same bones (humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals, phalanges).
  • Analogous organs – similar function, different origin/structure: wings of bat, bird, insect.
  • Vestigial organs – degenerate, non-functional (functional in relatives); in man: vermiform appendix, nictitating membrane, caudal vertebra, coccyx.
  • Atavism – reappearance of ancestral characters: rudimentary tail in newborns, thick body hair.
  • Embryology – embryos of fish to mammals similar in early stages (gill slits, tail). Biogenetic law / recapitulation theory – Ernst Haeckel: "Ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny".
  • Palaeontology – study of fossils; Leonardo da Vinci = Father of Palaeontology; fossils show gradual evolution from simple to complex.
  • Archaeopteryx – oldest known fossil bird, Jurassic period; connecting link between reptiles and birds: bird-like feathered wings; reptile-like long tail, clawed digits, conical teeth.
  • Evolution = gradual change in organisms over time; formation of new species through natural selection over generations.
  • Lamarckism – Jean Baptiste Lamarck (1744–1829), French naturalist; published in "Philosophie Zoologique" (1809); "theory of inheritance of acquired characters" / "use and disuse theory".
    • Principles: (i) internal vital force (organs increase in size); (ii) environment and new needs → new adaptive parts; (iii) use and disuse – used organs develop, unused degenerate; (iv) inheritance of acquired characters.
    • Examples: giraffe's long neck and forelimbs (use); degenerated wings of kiwi (disuse).
  • Darwinism / theory of natural selection – Charles Darwin (1809–1882), born England; via Prof. J.S. Henslow joined the 5-year voyage of H.M.S. Beagle (book: 1831–1835) around South America, Galapagos and Pacific islands; worked 20 more years; published "Origin of Species" (1859).
    • Principles: overproduction (geometric increase) → struggle for existence – intraspecific (same species), interspecific (different species), environmental (heat, cold, drought, flood) → variations (favourable useful) → survival of the fittest / natural selection → origin of species by gradual accumulation of favourable variations.
  • Variation = differences among individuals of a species/same parents; arises from gene recombination in meiosis and fertilisation; raw material of evolution.
    • Somatic variation – body cells, environmental, not inherited.
    • Germinal variation – germ cells, inherited: continuous (fluctuating) – small, gradual (skin colour, height, weight, eye colour); discontinuous – sudden, by mutation, no intermediates (short-legged Ancon sheep, six or more fingers in humans) – basis of De Vries' mutation theory.
  • Mutation arises from DNA replication errors, UV rays, chemicals; mutation leads to variation.
  • Palaeobotany (paleon = old) – study of plant remains of geological past; plant fossils mostly disarticulated parts. Importance: phylogeny and evolution; historical view of plant kingdom; classification; descriptive and comparative anatomy.
  • Kaspar Maria von Sternberg (1761–1838) – Father of Palaeobotany; founded Bohemian National Museum, Prague. Birbal Sahni (1891–1949) – Father of Indian Palaeobotany; worked on Palaeozoic ferns and fossil plants of Indian Gondwana formations.
  • Fossilisation methods:
    • Petrifaction – minerals like silica replace tissue; preserves hard and soft parts; most bones and wood.
    • Mold and cast – hollow impression (mold) filled by minerals (cast); shape only, not internal structure.
    • Preservation – whole organism in ice or amber (tree sap).
    • Compression – hard parts settle on sea bed under sediment.
    • Infiltration / replacement – silica, CaCO₃, MgCO₃ replace hard parts.
    • (Carbonization also listed.)
  • Living fossils – living organisms resembling fossil ancestors – Ginkgo biloba.
  • Age of fossils – by radioactive elements (carbon, uranium, lead, potassium). Radiocarbon (C-14) dating – W.F. Libby (1956): C-14 intake stops at death; measuring remaining C-14 gives time since death.
  • More to know – geologic time scale: chronological dating relating rock strata to time.
  • Thiruvakkarai fossil wood park (Villupuram district, TN) – tree trunks buried by river ~2 million years ago, replaced by silica; annual rings, nodes, colours still visible.
  • Ethnobotany – study of a region's plants and their traditional uses; term coined by J.W. Harshberger (1895); distinct branch in 20th century. Relevance: nutrition, health care, faith, cottage industries, biodiversity conservation. Tribals use bark, roots, leaves, flowers, seeds, oils, resins, gums for diarrhoea, fever, headache, diabetes, jaundice, snakebite, leprosy.
  • Astrobiology / exobiology – search for extraterrestrial life; origin, evolution, distribution of life in universe. Habitable zone: planet must have right mass to hold an atmosphere and orbit at the right distance for liquid water = "Goldilocks zone"; Earth is the only planet in it in our solar system; Mars may once have been habitable. Organisms of extreme environments = extremophiles (e.g. microbes in sandstone/granite of McMurdo Dry Valleys, Antarctica).
  • NASA Mars 2020 – astrobiology mission to study an ancient Martian environment and possible past life/biosignatures.

  • Outbreeding – unrelated animals; hybrids stronger. Mule = male donkey × female horse – stronger, more intelligent, hard-working, disease-resistant, but sterile.
  • Info bits: White Leghorn × Plymouth Rock → hybrid fowl (more eggs); Brown Swiss × Sahiwal → Karan Swiss cow (2–3 times more milk).
  • Heterosis (hybrid vigour) = superiority of hybrid: more milk, more eggs, better meat, faster growth.
  • Genetic engineering / recombinant DNA (rDNA) technology – transferring genes between organisms to make rDNA. Tools: plasmid (small circular double-stranded DNA in bacterial cytoplasm, replicates independently – vector); restriction enzymes ("molecular scissors", cut at palindromic restriction sites); DNA ligase (joins fragments).
  • Gene cloning steps: isolate DNA fragment (restriction enzyme) → insert into vector (plasmid) = rDNA → transfer into bacterial host (transformation) → select and multiply → express gene. Produces enzymes, hormones, vaccines.
  • Dolly – first cloned (female) sheep; Dr. Ian Wilmut, Roslin Institute, Scotland, July 1996; somatic cell nuclear transfer; lived 6.5 years, died 2003 (lung disease).
  • rDNA products: insulin (diabetes); human growth hormone; blood clotting factors (haemophilia); tissue plasminogen activator (dissolves clots, prevents heart attack); vaccines – hepatitis B, rabies. Eli Lilly and Company (USA), 1979 – first commercial human insulin by rDNA.
  • Gene therapy – replacing defective gene with functional gene; first successful in 1990. Somatic (body cells; not inherited – only type done so far) vs germ line (egg/sperm).
  • Stem cells – undifferentiated cells (body has over 200 specialised cell types); properties: self-renewal and differentiation; potency = number of possible fates. Embryonic stem cells – from inner cell mass of blastocyst; can form any cell. Adult (somatic) stem cells – from amniotic fluid, umbilical cord, bone marrow. Stem-cell therapy – e.g. neuronal stem cells for Parkinson's and Alzheimer's.

Unit 21 – Health and Diseases (PDF p. 308–322)

  • Lifestyle risk factors: smoking, alcohol, drugs, high-fat/cholesterol diets, junk food, low physical activity → chronic diseases: diabetes, obesity, heart disease, cancer, AIDS. Prevented by nutritious diet, exercise, avoiding drugs, alcohol, smoking.
  • Abuse = cruel, violent, harmful treatment: physical, emotional/psychological, verbal, child and sexual abuse; within or outside family.
  • Child abuse – all forms of physical/emotional ill-treatment, sexual abuse, exploitation harming a child's health and development. Physical abuse – threatening, beating, kicking, hitting.
  • Sexual abuse/harassment – power and dominance; inappropriate or forced sexual contact; more common at work places; verbal remarks, gestures, looks most common; causes psychological distress, illness, eating disorders.
  • Child sexual abuse – children are soft targets; abusers often known to child; lure with chocolates and toys; signs – genital injury, abdominal pain, frequent urinary infection, behavioural problems.
  • Protection of abused child: Child Helpline (1098) – social worker gives food, shelter, protection; counselling; family support; medical care; free legal counsel; rehabilitation (back to school); community awareness.
  • More to know – POCSO Act, 2012 (Protection of Children from Sexual Offences) – championed by Ministry of Women and Child Development; protects from sexual assault, sexual harassment, pornography; Special Courts for speedy trial; child traffickers punishable.
  • More to know – NCPCR (National Commission for Protection of Child Rights) – set up March 2007 under the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005; protects all children up to 18 years; universality and inviolability of child rights.
  • Prevention tips for children: avoid strangers; don't be alone with unknown persons; careful in transport; no gifts/money/chocolates without parents' knowledge; don't allow anyone to touch.
  • Addiction = physical and mental dependency on alcohol, smoking, drugs.
  • Drug abuse – habitual use → drug addict. Addictive drug modifies physical, biological, psychological, social behaviour by stimulating/depressing body and mind (acts on CNS). Psychotropic drugs = act on brain, alter behaviour, consciousness, thinking, perception = mood-altering drugs.
  • Drug dependence – physical and mental (needed for normal well-being) and psychological (feel it reduces stress). WHO (1984) recommended the term "drug dependence" instead of drug addiction/abuse.
  • Key dates/laws: International Day against Drug Abuse and Illicit Trafficking – 26 June; Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (NDPS) Act – 1985.
  • Behavioural changes in drug users: falling academic performance, absenteeism; poor hygiene, isolation, depression, fatigue, aggression; strained relations; changed food/sleep habits; weight and appetite swings; seeking easy money; prone to AIDS, hepatitis B.
  • De-addiction: detoxification (gradual withdrawal; medicines for withdrawal symptoms) → psychotherapy (individual/group counselling) → counselling family → rehabilitation (vocational training).
  • Tobacco – from Nicotiana tabacum and Nicotiana rustica (dried, cured leaves); addiction due to nicotine – an alkaloid, stimulant, highly poisonous; nicotine stimulates adrenaline secretion. Used by smoking (cigar, cigarette, bidi, pipe, hukka), chewing (with pan), snuffing (through nose).
  • Effects of smoking/tobacco:
    • Benzopyrene and polycyclic hydrocarbons – carcinogenic → lung cancer.
    • Inflamed throat and bronchi → bronchitis, pulmonary tuberculosis.
    • Inflamed alveoli → less gas-exchange area → emphysema.
    • Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin → less O₂ → hypoxia.
    • Raised BP → heart disease; more gastric secretion → gastric and duodenal ulcers.
    • Tobacco chewing → oral (mouth) cancer.
  • Info bits: WHO directive – all cigarette ads/packs carry "Smoking is injurious to Health". More to know: Anti-Tobacco Act passed 1 May 2004; by 2030 tobacco expected to be the single biggest cause of death (10 million deaths/year); 31 May = World No Tobacco Day.
  • Alcohol abuse – dependence = alcoholism, addict = alcoholic. Alcohol depresses nervous system (sedative, analgesic); acts first on central nervous system.
    • Harmful effects: nerve cell damage; lack of coordination; blurred vision → road accidents; dilation of blood vessels (heart); fatty liver → cirrhosis (fibrous tissue); loss of control, death. Info bit: flax seeds, guava, tomato, spinach lower blood sugar.
  • Obesity – excess body fat with abnormal weight gain; multifactorial chronic disease; calorie intake > energy spent. BMI = weight (kg) / height² (m²).
  • More to know: every 7 excess calories → 1 g fat; fat > 20–25 % of body weight; 10 % above standard weight = overweight; 20 % above = obese.
  • Obesity causes: genetic, inactivity, overeating, endocrine factors; raises risk of hypertension, diabetes, gall bladder disease, coronary heart disease, arthritis. Control: low-calorie, normal-protein, high-fibre diet; exercise; meditation, yoga.
  • Cardiovascular disease (CVD); coronary heart disease (CHD) – most common; cholesterol deposition in vessels (fatty streak → fibrous plaque) → atherosclerosis → ischemia (deficient blood supply to heart muscle) → myocardial infarction (death of heart muscle tissue).
  • More to know: desirable blood cholesterol for Indians < 200 mg/dl; risk rises from 200 to 300 mg/dl.
  • Risk factors: hypercholesterolemia and hypertension (can damage brain, kidney → stroke). Causes: heredity, saturated fat/cholesterol diet, obesity, age, smoking, stress, sedentary life, alcohol. Symptoms: breathlessness, headache, tiredness, dizziness, chest pain, leg swelling, GI disturbance. HDL = "good" cholesterol (lowers risk); LDL = "bad" (raises risk).
  • Prevention: fewer calories, low saturated fat, cholesterol, carbohydrate and salt; more PUFA, fibre, fruits, vegetables; exercise, walking, yoga; no alcohol, no smoking.
  • Cancer – ~4 million deaths/year worldwide; over 1 million patients in India; word from Latin "crab"; study = oncology (oncos = tumour). Uncontrolled cell division forming tumour/neoplasm; spread to distant parts = metastasis (lungs, bones, liver, skin, brain).
  • More to know: World Cancer Day – 4 February; National Cancer Awareness Day – 7 November. Benign tumours stay confined; malignant grow rapidly and invade (metastasis is linked to malignant tumours).
  • Types: carcinoma – epithelial and glandular tissue (skin, lung, stomach, brain) – ~85 % of tumours; sarcoma – connective and muscle tissue (bone, cartilage, tendon, fat, muscle) – 1 %; leukaemia – excess WBC in bone marrow and lymph nodes – "blood cancer"; commonest cancer also in children under 15. (Lymphoma affects lymph nodes and spleen – evaluation.)
  • Carcinogens: physical – heavy smoking (lung, oral, throat, larynx cancer), betel and tobacco chewing (oral), excess sunlight (skin); chemical – nicotine, caffeine, coal/oil combustion products, pesticides, asbestos, nickel, some dyes, artificial sweeteners; radiation – X-rays, γ-rays, UV; biological – oncogenic viruses. Cancer-causing genes = oncogenes.
  • Treatment: surgery; radiation therapy (cancer cells more sensitive as they divide rapidly); chemotherapy (anticancer drugs); immunotherapy (interferons as biological response modifiers). Prevention: avoid tobacco, industrial pollutants, excess radiation; early detection.
  • AIDS (Acquired Immuno Deficiency Syndrome) – caused by HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus); attacks lymphocytes; immune failure → opportunistic infections.
  • Dr. Suniti Solomon – pioneer of HIV research in India; first voluntary testing and counselling centre and AIDS research group in Chennai (1980s); her team documented India's first HIV infection in 1985 (first Indian AIDS patient, Chennai).
  • HIV found in urine, tears, saliva, breast milk, vaginal secretions. Transmission: sexual contact; contaminated needles/syringes (IV drug users); infected blood transfusion; mother to child through placenta. Not by touch, physical contact or patient's clothes.
  • Symptoms: immunodeficiency; swollen lymph nodes, brain damage, memory loss, appetite and weight loss, fever, chronic diarrhoea, cough, lethargy, pharyngitis, nausea, headache. Diagnosis: ELISA (Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay) or Western Blot. Treatment: anti-retroviral drugs, immunostimulation.
  • Prevention: screen blood before transfusion; disposable needles/syringes; safe sex, condoms; awareness; do not isolate HIV patients.
  • More to know: "Don't die of ignorance"; NACO (National AIDS Control Organization) and NGOs educate people; 1 December = World AIDS Day.
  • Evaluation terms: study of cause of disease = etiology; reduced response to same drug dose on repeated use = tolerance; insulin resistance occurs in Type-2.

Unit 22 – Environmental Management (PDF p. 323–336)

  • Environmental management = environment's structure, function, quality and maintenance, incl. conservation of living and non-living components.
  • Renewable resources – renewed along with use (forests, crops, wildlife, groundwater, wind, solar). Non-renewable – exhausted by use, not easily replaced (mineral ores, coal, petroleum).
  • Conservation = proper use, preservation and management of nature and resources (biological, economic, recreational value); judicious balance between exploitation and replenishment; sustainable world – efficient energy use, save water, avoid plastics/non-biodegradables.
  • Forests – wood, food, fodder, fibre, medicine; carbon sink; regulate climate, increase rainfall, reduce global warming, prevent floods and landslides, protect wildlife, water catchments, ecological balance.
  • Deforestation – causes: intensive agriculture, urbanisation, dams, roads, buildings, industries, hydroelectric projects, forest fires. India loses about 1.5 million hectares of forest every year. Effects: floods, drought, soil erosion, loss of wildlife, extinction, imbalance of biogeochemical cycles, climate change, desertification, decrease in rainfall.
  • More to know – Chipko movement (1973) – non-violent; "chipko" = embrace – villagers hugged trees; began in Chamoli district, Uttar Pradesh (now Uttarakhand); victory in 1980 – 15-year ban on tree felling in Himalayan forests.
  • Forest area: 752.3 lakh ha reserved forests, 215.1 lakh ha protected forests.
  • Forest conservation: afforestation (Van Mahotsav); social forestry (public participation, common land for firewood, fodder, timber; helps tribals); laws – National Forest Policy (1952 and 1988), Forest Conservation Act, 1980.
  • Wildlife = undomesticated animals in natural habitats; maintain biodiversity; tourism revenue. Global wildlife fell by 52 % between 1970 and 2014.
  • Aims of wildlife management: limit exploitation; prevent extinction; protect threatened/endangered species; study ecological relationships; ban hunting and poaching; set up national parks, sanctuaries, protected areas, biosphere reserves.
  • Wildlife Protection Act, 1972 – bans killing/hunting of specified animals; sanctuaries, national parks, closed areas; schemes for endangered species; Central Zoo Authority; regulates trade in wildlife products.
  • Jim Corbett National Park – first national park, 1936, Uttarakhand. 15 biosphere reserves in India (book); Nilgiris = biosphere reserve in Tamil Nadu. National park – conserves entire wildlife (plants + animals); sanctuary – reserved exclusively for animals.
  • Organisations: Indian Board for Wildlife (IBWL); WWF; World Conservation Union; IUCN; CITES (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species); BNHS (Bombay Natural History Society); Wildlife Preservation Society of India, Dehradun.
  • Rathika Ramasamy – from Venkatachalapuram, Theni district, TN; first Indian woman wildlife photographer of international repute; bird photography; book "The best of wildlife moments" (Nov 2014).
  • Info bits – projects: Project Tiger 1973; Project Elephant 1992; Crocodile Conservation Project 1976; Sea Turtle Conservation Project 1999; Indian Rhino Vision 2020 – 3000 one-horned rhinos in Assam.
  • Soil erosion – removal of top soil (humus, minerals) by wind and water; agents: high wind, flowing water, landslides, deforestation, farming, mining, overgrazing. Control: keep vegetation cover, control grazing, crop rotation, store runoff, reforestation, terracing, contour ploughing, shelter belts of trees against wind.
  • Non-renewable (exhaustible) energy: coal, petroleum, natural gas, nuclear – conventional sources = 90 % of world commercial energy, nuclear 10 %. Renewable (inexhaustible) – non-conventional: biofuel, biomass, geothermal, hydro, tidal, solar, wave, wind.
  • Fossil fuels – formed by anaerobic decomposition of buried organisms over millions of years under heat and pressure → hydrocarbons (petroleum, coal, natural gas).
  • India – third largest consumer of crude oil (after USA and China). Coal → thermal power plants; petroleum (crude oil) refined → petrol, diesel; kerosene, LPG for cooking.
  • Conserving coal and petroleum: save electricity; cycle short distances; pressure cooker, solar cooker/heater; fuel-efficient engines.
  • Case study – Taj Mahal (Agra, UP): white marble yellowed by sulphur and nitrogen oxides from Mathura oil refinery (Indian Oil Corporation); Govt set emission standards around it.
  • Solar energy – only about 47 % of solar energy reaching the atmosphere reaches Earth's surface.
    • Solar cell (photovoltaic) – made of silicon; sunlight → electricity, no pollution; for remote areas; used in street lights, traffic signals, pumps, battery charging, satellites and space probes, radio/TV in remote areas, calculators, toys, watches.
    • Solar panel = many cells connected; costly to make.
    • Solar cooker – insulated box painted (black) inside, thick glass cover, plane mirror reflector.
    • Solar thermal power plant – concentrates rays to make steam → turbine. A 100-litre solar heater saves up to 1500 units of electricity a year.
    • Advantages: abundant, free, renewable, no pollution.
  • Biogas ("gobar gas") – ~75 % methane + H₂S, CO₂, H₂; from anaerobic decomposition of cow dung and plant wastes. Uses: cooking, motors, pump sets, electricity. Advantages: smokeless; disposes of wastes and sewage; slurry is manure rich in N and P; safe; reduces greenhouse gases.
  • Shale gas – in fine-grained sedimentary shale rock; extracted by hydraulic fracturing; concerns – groundwater contamination, soil fertility, needs millions of gallons of water. More to know: six basins for shale gas – Cambay (Gujarat), Assam-Arakan (NE), Gondwana (Central India), Krishna-Godavari onshore, Cauvery onshore, Indo-Gangetic.
  • Wind energy – kinetic energy of wind → windmill/turbine → generator; electricity, water pumps, flour mills. World's largest and tallest wind turbine – Hawaii; one turbine powers 300 homes. Free, eco-friendly, low maintenance.
  • Water energy – Earth surface ~71 % water; hydropower / hydroelectricity – kinetic energy of flowing/falling water (hilly areas); no pollution. (Activity: Tehri Dam, Sardar Sarovar Dam.)
  • Tidal energy – from ocean tides (gravitational effects); turbines in tidal streams; no pollution, fuel or waste; tides predictable; water denser than air → works at lower speeds than wind.
  • Rainwater harvesting – collecting and storing rainwater; main purpose – recharge groundwater. Methods: rooftop harvesting, recharge pits; rural – eris (interconnected village tanks; traditional TN system), ooranis (small ponds for domestic use). Advantages: checks groundwater depletion, meets demand, reduces floods and erosion, clean drinking water.
  • More to know – Kallanai (Grand Anicut) – fourth oldest dam in the world, built by Karikala Chola, 2nd century CE, on the Kaveri, ~20 km from Tiruchirappalli; still in use.
  • Electrical energy – generators run by turbines (steam, water, wind). Saving: CFL and LED bulbs, switch off appliances and chargers, solar water heaters instead of geysers, minimise AC use.
  • E-waste – spoiled/outdated electrical and electronic devices; toxic lead, cadmium, chromium, mercury; recoverable iron, copper, silicon, aluminium, gold; only 5 % recycled. Sources – computers, mobiles, TVs, appliances, cartridges, batteries. Composition: computer components 66 %, telecom 12 %, biomedical 7 %, other 6 %, electronic 5 %.
  • More to know – health effects: lead – nervous system, child brain development; chromium – asthmatic bronchitis; cadmium – kidney, liver, neural damage; mercury – brain and respiratory damage; burning PVC → dioxin (reproductive, developmental, immune harm).
  • Sewage – untreated wastewater = leading polluter of Indian water. Sources: households, dye/textile, leather, sugar/breweries, paper/pulp industries. Treatment: pre-screening → aeration (microbial degradation with air) → sedimentation (sludge) → sludge removal → disinfection (chlorination, UV) → water recycling.
  • Solid waste – municipal, hospital, industrial, e-waste. Disposal: segregation (biodegradable/non-biodegradable); sanitary landfill (low-lying areas, compacted; stabilises in 2–12 months); incineration (non-biodegradable/medical waste at high temperature); composting (microbes or earthworms → humus).
  • Recycling: old paper → paper; coconut shells, jute, cotton stalk, bagasse → paper and hardboard; paddy husk → fodder; cow dung → biogas + manure. 4R approach: Reduce, Reuse, Recovery, Recycle.
  • Evaluation facts: tidal energy is renewable (non-conventional); coal, petroleum, natural gas = fossil fuels; global warming raises sea level, melts glaciers, sinks islands.
