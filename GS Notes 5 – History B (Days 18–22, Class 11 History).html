Unit 1 · Early India: From the Beginnings to the Indus Civilisation (PDF p. 5–20)

Sources

  • No written evidence for this period; Harappan script still undeciphered. Sources = archaeological sites, sediments, fossils, stone/bone tools, rock paintings.
  • Faunal (animal) and floral (plant) evidence – charred seeds, pollens, phytoliths (plant stones). mt-DNA (mitochondrial DNA) studies → prehistoric migrations.
  • Language families of India: Indo-Aryan, Dravidian, Austro-Asiatic, Tibeto-Burman.

1.1 Pre-historic India

  • Pre-historic = period before script = Stone Age. Human ancestors evolved in Africa; first to migrate out of Africa = Homo erectus.
  • Human ancestors present in India between 2 million and 1 million years ago.
  • Cultures named after material (Painted Grey Ware), region (Indus) or first site found (Acheulian, Harappan).
  • Sequence: Palaeolithic (Lower / Middle / Upper) → Mesolithic → Neolithic (plant & animal domestication). Classified by stratigraphic, chronological and lithic (stone tool) evidence.

  • Only hominin fossil of India: Hathnora near Hoshangabad (MP) – cranium called "Narmada human" (Archaic Homo sapiens).
  • Narmada valley animal fossils: Elephas namadicus (giant tusked elephant), Stegodon ganesa, Bos namadicus (wild cattle), Equus namadicus (extinct horse-like). Athirampakkam: teeth of Equus, water buffalo → open wet landscape near Chennai.

  • Anatomically modern humans emerged c. 3,00,000 years ago; modern humans (from sub-Saharan Africa) reached Asia c. 60,000 years ago. Middle Stone Age = Homo sapiens in Africa, Neanderthals in Europe; no fossil in India.
  • Ostrich eggshell beads: Patne (dated 25,000 BP) and Bhimbetka; incised shells/beads at Jwalapuram (AP), Patne, Batadomba-Lena & Fa Hien Cave (Sri Lanka).
  • Green paintings of Bhimbetka = Upper Palaeolithic. Earliest shrine in India: Baghor (UP) – sandstone block in rubble circle.

Mesolithic culture

  • Sites: Paisra (Bihar), Langhnaj (Gujarat), Baghor II, Chopani Mando, Sarai Nahar Rai, Mahadaha, Damdama (UP), Sankanakallu (AP), Kibbanahalli (Kar); rock shelters Adamgarh, Bhimbetka; coastal: Mumbai, teri sites in Thoothukudi district (TN), Vishakapatnam.
  • Teri = coastal landscape of sand dunes (Pleistocene). Didwana (W. Rajasthan) fresh-water lakes 10,000–3500 BP. Sri Lanka microliths c. 28,500 BP.
  • Economy: hunting (cattle, gaur, nilgai, sambar, chital…), fishing; domestic animal bones at Kanewal, Loteshwar, Ratanpur, Adamgarh, Bhimbetka; camel bones – Kanewal.
  • Huts: circular huts with postholes – Chopani Mando, Damdama (UP), Bagor, Tilwara (Raj).
  • Burials: Mahadaha, Damdama, Sarai Nahar Rai (UP); at Mahadaha a man and woman buried together; one burial with ivory pendant.
  • Art: rock paintings in MP – Bhimbetka (near Bhopal), Raisen, Pachmarhi, South Mirzapur (UP); engraved chert core at Chandravati (Raj); haematite.

1.2 Hunter-gatherers of the historical period

  • Microlith-using hunter-gatherers continued alongside later cultures; recorded in Edgar Thurston's Castes and Tribes of Southern India. Calling them "primitive" is incorrect.
  • When the Indus Civilisation peaked, Tamil Nadu had microlithic hunter-gatherers; Andhra–Karnataka had Neolithic agro-pastoralists.

1.3 Early Neolithic cultures and beginning of agriculture

  • Agriculture emerged 10,000–5000 BCE (Fertile Crescent, Indus, Ganges, China) → surplus, villages, pottery = Neolithic revolution.
  • Wild vs domestic: domestic plants' seeds become smaller; domestic animals lose ferocity.

1.4 The Indus Civilisation

  • First urbanisation of India; also called Harappan Civilisation (Harappa first site identified). Area ≈ 1.5 million sq km.
  • Phases: Early Harappan 3000–2600 BCE; Mature 2600–1900 BCE; Late 1900–1700 BCE.
  • Charles Masson visited Harappa 1826; Alexander Burnes visited Amri 1831. Harappa damaged for Lahore–Multan railway. Seal reached Alexander Cunningham (first surveyor of ASI) – visited 1853, 1856, 1875. Sir John Marshall (DG, ASI) started research; Mortimer Wheeler excavated in 1940s.
  • Boundaries: Sutkagen-dor (W, Pak–Iran border), Shortugai (N, Afghanistan), Alamgirpur (E, UP), Daimabad (S, Maharashtra).
  • Major cities: Harappa (Punjab, Pak), Mohenjo-Daro (Sindh, Pak), Dholavira, Lothal, Surkotada (Gujarat), Kalibangan (Rajasthan), Banawali, Rakhigarhi (Haryana).
  • Town planning: fortification, grid pattern, drains of burnt brick, houses of mud brick, multi-storey, central courtyard; Mohenjo-Daro on a platform – citadel + lower town; warehouse.
  • Great Bath (Mohenjo-Daro): tank in courtyard, stairs on N & S, bricks laid with gypsum mortar, ritual bathing.
  • Agriculture: wheat, barley, lentil, chickpea, sesame, millets; double cropping; ploughs – ploughed field at Kalibangan; canal & well irrigation.
  • Animals: sheep, goat, fowl; knew buffalo, pig, elephant; horse not known. Cattle = Zebu.
  • Craft centres: Shell – Nageshwar & Balakot; Lapis lazuli – Shortughai; Carnelian – Lothal; Steatite – S. Rajasthan; Copper – Rajasthan & Oman.
  • Pottery: red slip, black paintings (pipal leaves, fish-scale, intersecting circles). Bronze Age; no iron. Chert blades of Rohri chert (Pakistan).
  • Textiles: knew cotton and silk; "priest" wearing shawl with flowers; dancing girl with bangles up to upper arm.
  • Trade: Mesopotamian cuneiform texts call Indus region "Meluhha"; Harappan jar found in Oman; seals in Oman, Bahrain, Iraq, Iran.
  • Weights: cubical chert weights, binary ratio 1:2:4:8:16:32; the 16-unit weight = 13.63 g; scale – 1 inch ≈ 1.75 cm.
  • Script: ~5,000 texts; longest ≈ 26 signs; some scholars – Dravidian. Seals of steatite, copper, terracotta, ivory.
  • Art: steatite "Priest king", copper/bronze "dancing girl" (both Mohenjo-Daro); toy carts, rattles, marbles.
  • Religion: nature, pipal tree, mother goddess; fire altars at Kalibangan; burial (and some cremation) with pottery, mirrors → belief in afterlife.
  • Polity: uniform pottery, seals, weights, bricks → central authority; Harappa/Mohenjo-Daro possibly city-states.
  • Indus & Tamil civilisation: graffiti on megalithic pots of South India and place names cited for link; not proved. Arikamedu, Keezhadi, Uraiyur belong to the second urbanisation – different from Indus cities.
  • Contemporary cultures: Kerala & Sri Lanka – hunting-gathering; Karnataka–Andhra – Neolithic; Deccan & W. India – Chalcolithic → "cultural mosaic".
  • Decline from c. 1900 BCE: climate change, decline of Mesopotamian trade, drought/drying rivers, invasions, floods, shift of river course; people moved south and east.

Unit 2 · Early India: The Chalcolithic, Megalithic, Iron Age and Vedic Cultures (PDF p. 21–36)

  • Period c. 2000–600 BCE (after Indus decline to Mahajanapadas). Sources: archaeology + Vedic literature (orally transmitted). Early Vedic ≈ some Chalcolithic cultures; Later Vedic ≈ Painted Grey Ware (Iron Age, N. India).

Literature of the Vedic Age

  • Veda from vid = to know. Four Vedas: Rig (oldest), Yajur, Sama, Atharva. Earliest written manuscripts: 10th–11th century CE.
  • Rig Veda: 1500–1000 BCE; 10 books – Books 2–7 earliest; 1, 8, 9, 10 later.
  • Structure: Samhitas (hymn collections) → Brahmanas (commentaries on rituals) → Aranyakas (forest texts) → Upanishads (philosophy).
  • Sama Veda – musical notes; Yajur – rituals & hymns; Atharva – charms and magic spells.
  • Zend Avesta – Persian text of Zoroastrianism; linguistic similarity with Vedas → indirect evidence Aryan home was outside India.

2.1 Pre-Aryan, Late Harappan and Chalcolithic cultures

  • Chalcolithic = copper + stone; rural, pastoral-farming; black-and-red ware; silos (storage pits); houses on stone foundations, bamboo-frame walls.
  • South India: no full-fledged Chalcolithic; black-on-red ware, copper flat axes; millets, pulses, horse gram.
  • Ochre Coloured Pottery (OCP): 2600–1200 BCE, Indo-Gangetic plain; red slip that comes off on touch; also called "copper hoard culture"; rice, barley, legumes; seen as "impoverished Harappan" by some.

2.2 Iron Age in North India

  • Coincides with Painted Grey Ware (PGW), 1100–800 BCE; 1000+ sites, mainly Ganga–Yamuna valley; correlates with Kuru-Panchala kingdom. Followed by Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW) – Mahajanapada & Mauryan period.
  • Iron Age in North = PGW; in South = Megalithic burials.

2.3 Megalithic / Iron Age in Tamil Nadu

  • Megalith = circular tomb of big stone slabs over a burial. Urn burial (e.g. Adichanallur) grouped as megalithic because grave goods are identical.
  • Black ware peculiar to TN burial sites (found in burials, not habitations).
  • Megalithic burials end c. 3rd–2nd c. CE. Brahmi writing akin to Ashokan Brahmi found at Kodumanal (Erode).
  • Four hero stones with Tamil-Brahmi (3rd–2nd c. BCE) in upper Vaigai valley confirm Sangam hero-stone tradition (cattle raids) → some Sangam poems ≈ early 1st c. BCE. Hero stone = extension of menhir tradition. (Photo: Tamil-Brahmi hero stone – Puliman Kombai.)
  • Menhirs (upright stones) and dolmens (slab tombs) in TN (photos: Dolmen – Mallachandram; Menhir – Kumarikalpalayam, Tiruppur).
  • Vadamalkunda (Krishnagiri) – black-and-red ware, human remains, iron objects. Singaripalayam near Kundadam (Tiruppur) – menhir; settlement on River Uppar.

The Aryans (2.5 Aryans and Rig Vedic society)

  • "Aryan" ≠ race; = original speakers of Indo-Aryan languages. Rig Veda has ~300 Munda and Dravidian words.
  • Aryans: domesticated horse, spoked-wheel chariots, fire cult, soma; came as migrants from Central Asia (Eastern Europe–Central Asia, north of Black Sea); Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex 1900–1500 BCE.
  • Inscriptions with Indo-European names: Iraq 2200 BCE; Anatolian 1900–1700; Kassite 1600; Mittani 1400 BCE (Syria); Bogaz Koi 1400 BCE (Vedic-like god names).
  • Rig Veda: asva (horse) 215 times, vrishabha (bull) 170 times; tiger & rhinoceros not mentioned; no urban life → Aryans not Harappans. Genetic marker M17.

2.6 Rig Vedic (Early Vedic) culture – 1500–1000 BCE

  • Geography: E. Afghanistan, Pakistan, Punjab, fringes of W. UP.
  • Dasas/Dasyus – non-Aryans; Simyu, Kikata; Sambara (son of Kulitara, 90 forts) defeated by Divodasa of Bharata clan.
  • Battle of Ten Kings (Dasarajna): on Paurushni (Ravi); Sudas of Bharatas (priest Vasishta) defeated 10 chiefs (5 Aryan); Purus defeated → Purus + Bharatas = Kurus; Kurus + Panchalas → Upper Ganga. India = Bharata Varsha after Bharatas.
  • Indra = Purandara (destroyer of settlements/forts).
  • Social: varna = colour; Arya varna vs Dasa varna. Purusha Sukta – Brahmana (mouth), Kshatriya (arms), Vaisya (thighs), Sudra (feet). Panis – itinerant/caravan traders.
  • Jana (tribe) occurs 21 times, janapada never; vis (common people) 170 times; family = griha, head grihapati, wife sapatni.
  • Women: attended assemblies, sacrifices; polyandry, widow remarriage known; marriage at 16–17; little child marriage.
  • Agriculture: field = kshetra; ploughing = krishi; plough = langla/sura; furrow = sita; barley = yavam, wheat = godhuma.
  • Pastoralism: cattle = wealth; war = gavishthi (search for cows); no private property in land.
  • Crafts: ayas = copper/bronze; smith = karmara; carpenter = takshan; yarn = siri.
  • Trade: Panis; pan = barter; nishka = gold/silver ornament used in barter; dana & dakshina = redistribution. Sea = samudra, boat = nau (boats with 100 oars).
  • Polity: tribal; chief = rajan (hereditary, maybe elected by samiti); also gopa/gopati (chief of cattle). Assemblies: sabha (elders), samiti (people), vidhata (tribe), gana; women attended sabha & vidhata. Purohita (priest-adviser), Senani (army chief), Vrajapati (controlled territory), Gramani (head of village & fighting unit). Bali = voluntary contribution. No tax officers, no justice references. Trasadasyu (Purus) gifted 50 women.
  • Religion: naturalistic, polytheistic. Indra most important; Agni – intermediary; Varuna – water, upholder of natural order; Surya; Ushas – dawn; Aditi, Prithvi, Sinivali; Soma – plants; Maruts – strength; few references to Rudra/Siva.

2.7 Later Vedic culture – 1000 to 700–600 BCE

  • Texts: Yajur, Sama, Atharva + Brahmanas, Aranyakas, Upanishads. Associated with PGW.
  • Eastward expansion: Punjab → Ganga-Yamuna doab (Kuru-Panchala); c. 1000 BCE to Kosala (E. UP) and Videha (N. Bihar). Aitareya Brahmana: Pundras (Bengal) and Andhras outside Aryan fold; Atharva Veda: Anga & Magadha people seen as enemies. Munda words entered.
  • Iron = syama-ayas / krishna-ayas (dark metal); now dated c. 1200 BCE or earlier.
  • Janapada term in Brahmanas (c. 800 BCE); nagara = commercial quarters; Hastinapura, Kausambi = proto-urban.
  • Polity: king's power rose; vidhata disappeared, sabha & samiti continued; Samrat/Samrajya; sacrifices vajapeya (chariot race), rajasuya, asvamedha (horse let loose); rashtra (territory), rajya (sovereignty); bali → tribute. Romila Thapar: "lineage to state". Kuru-Panchala capital Hastinapur; after flood Kurus moved to Kausambi.
  • Society: Panchavimsa Brahmana places Kshatriya first; Satapatha Brahmana places Brahmana first. Dvija (twice-born), upanayana limited to upper varnas; Sudras & women denied upanayana and Gayatri mantra. Rathakaras (chariot makers) could wear sacred thread. Gotra (= cowpen) – same-gotra marriage barred. Polygyny; married man with wife = yajamana. Ashramas: brahmacharya, grihasta, vanaprastha mentioned; sanyasa not yet.
  • Women: status declined; excluded from rituals; daughters seen as trouble.
  • Economy: Satapatha Brahmana – royal ploughing rituals; Balarama with plough; rice in doab; potter = kulala; wool = urna sutra; dakshina of 20 camels, 100 gold necklaces, 300 horses, 10,000 cows; no coins (barter) – coins after c. 600 BCE.
  • Religion: Agni & Indra declined; Prajapati chief deity; Rudra (= Siva) important – names Pasunampathi, Sarva, Bhava, Bahikas (Satapatha Brahmana); Vishnu = protector (no incarnations yet).
  • Upanishads ("sit nearby") = Vedanta; atman, Brahman, karma. "Satyameva Jayate" – Mundaka Upanishad. Dara Shukoh translated Upanishads into Persian in 1657.
  • Music instruments: lute, flute, drum; glass beads late Vedic.

Unit 3 · Rise of Territorial Kingdoms and New Religious Sects (PDF p. 37–49)

  • Sources: epics, dharmasastras, Tripitakas, Jatakas, Jaina texts, Greek accounts (Arrian).
  • Views on iron: D.D. Kosambi – Aryans moved east for S. Bihar iron ore → Magadha's dominance; R.S. Sharma – iron axes/ploughs expanded cultivation; Makkhan Lal – a myth (Gangetic plain forested even in 16th–17th c.); A. Ghosh & Niharranjan Ray – forests cleared by fire.

3.1 Developments in the Gangetic plain – Second urbanisation

  • Wet rice cultivation + iron → surplus → crafts, trade → second urbanisation (first = Harappan).
  • Types of towns: political (Rajagriha, Shravasti, Kaushambi, Champa); trade (Ujjain, Taxila); holy (Vaishali).
  • Urban features: NBPW = luxury ware, "urban hallmark"; moats, fortification; drains, ring wells, soak-pits.

3.2 Janapadas to Mahajanapadas

  • Janapada = "place where the tribe sets its foot". Loyalty shifted from jana to janapada. State elements: territory, people, government, sovereignty.
  • 16 Mahajanapadas (Puranic, Buddhist, Jaina traditions): Gandhara, Kamboja, Asmaka (Assaka), Vatsa, Avanti, Shurasena, Chedi, Malla, Kuru, Panchala, Matsya, Vajji (Vrijji), Anga, Kasi, Kosala, Magadha. (Asmaka is the only one south of the Vindhyas – map.)
  • Gana-sanghas (oligarchies, clan-based, collective decisions): Vrijjis best known, capital Vaishali (Mithila).
  • Monarchies: Vedic orthodoxy; hereditary, primogeniture; councils parishad, sabha (advisory). Taxes: Bali (on cultivable land area), Bhaga (share of produce), Kara, Shulka.
  • Rich landowners = grihapatis; labourers = dasas/karmakaras; small landowners = kassakas/krishakas. A new category below shudras = untouchables.

3.3 Emergence of heterodox thinkers

  • Causes: rigid Vedic religion; new territorial states; Vedic religion not yet deep-rooted; new merchants (sethis) sought status; Kshatriyas denied ashramas.
  • Dr S. Radhakrishnan quoted on the revolt against ceremonial religion.
  • Samannaphala Sutta: Ajatashatru met six teachers – Purana Kassapa, Makkhali Gosala, Ajita Kesakambalin, Pakudha Kachchayana, Sanjaya Belatthiputta, Nigantha Nataputta (Mahavira) – before Buddha.

3.4 Ajivikas

  • Founder (Buddhist records): Nanda Vaccha → Kisa Samkicca → Makkhali Gosala (3rd and greatest).
  • Gosala met Mahavira at Nalanda; friends for 6 years. At Sravasti patronised by potter woman Halahala; Sravasti = Ajivika HQ. Naked ascetics.
  • Doctrine: niyati (fate) – everything predetermined; six inevitable factors – gain/loss, joy/sorrow, life/death.
  • Purana Kassapa – actions have no merit/demerit; Pakudha Kacchayana – world of seven eternal substances.
  • Ajita Kesakambalin ("of the hair blanket") – materialist: four elements, no life after death.
  • Ajivikas in Tamil land: referred to in Manimekalai, Nilakesi, Sivagnanasiddhiyar; Nilakesi meets Buddha and Puranan (Ajivika leader); Cholas levied a tax on Ajivikas.
  • Lokayata / Carvaka – materialism; founders Carvaka & Ajita Kesakambalin; scepticism; rejected Vedas.
  • Rivalry: Jaina Bhagavatisutra and Buddhagosa ridicule Gosala; Jataka compares heretics to fire-flies.

3.5 Jainism

  • Earlier called Nirgranthas (free from bonds); Mahavira = Jina (conqueror). Founder per tradition: Risabha (1st Tirthankara); Yajur Veda names Risabha, Ajitanatha, Aristanemi.
  • Mahavira: born c. 540 BCE, Kundagrama (near Vaishali); father Siddhartha (Jnatrika clan); mother Trishala (Lichchavi, sister of Chetaka). Left home at 30; 12 yrs wandering; 6 yrs with Gosala; enlightenment at 42 (13th year); preached 30 yrs; died c. 468 BCE at 72, Pavapuri (near Rajgriha), by fasting.
  • Schism c. 79/82 CE (≈500 yrs after Mahavira) due to Magadha famine: Bhadrabahu → south → Digambaras (sky-clad); Sthulabhadra → Svetambaras (white-clad).
  • Councils: Pataliputra (Sthulabhadra) – canon of 12 angas; Valabhi (Gujarat), 5th c. CE – added 12 upangas. Earliest texts: Acharangasutra, Sutrakritanga, Kalpasutra; language Ardha-Magadhi.
  • Tenets: non-violence central; denies God; Vedas rejected; dualism – jiva (soul) and ajiva (matter); only monks attain liberation.
  • Triratnas: Right faith (samyag-darshana), Right knowledge (samyag-jnana), Right conduct (samyag-mahavrata).
  • Pancha-mahavrata: ahimsa, asteya (not steal), satya, brahmacharya, aparigraha (no property).
  • Jains took to trade and money-lending; women admitted to order but cannot attain salvation as women.
  • Jainism in Tamil Nadu: from c. 3rd c. CE; rock shelters around Madurai; Kopperuncholan's death by fasting (Purananuru) ≈ sallekhana. Jaina Tamil works: Naladiyar, Palamoli, Jivaka Chinthamani, Yapperunkalam Karikai, Neelakesi. Jaina Dravida Sangha at Madurai c. 470 CE by Vajranandi (disciple of Boojya Padha). Temple at Tiruparuthikunram near Kanchipuram (ceiling paintings) – area called Jaina Kanchi.
  • Decline: no royal patronage, split, lack of missionary zeal, factionalism, severity, rival Buddhism.

3.6 Buddhism

  • Revived in India in mid-20th c. by Dr B.R. Ambedkar.

Unit 4 · Emergence of State and Empire (PDF p. 50–66)

Sources

  • Buddhist & Jain texts, Brahmanas; Mahavamsa (Pali chronicle, Sri Lanka); Greek accounts after Alexander.
  • James Prinsep deciphered Brahmi of Sanchi inscriptions in 1837. Edicts begin "devanampiya piyadassi" (beloved of the gods, of pleasing looks); identified as Ashoka in 1915 (edict naming "devanampiya Ashoka").
  • Junagadh rock inscription (near Girnar, Gujarat) of Rudradaman (130–150 CE) – mentions Pushyagupta, provincial governor (rashtriya) of Chandragupta → empire reached Gujarat.
  • Mudrarakshasa by Visakhadatta (Gupta period, after 4th c. CE) – Chandragupta's accession and Chanakya's strategies.

4.1 Rise of Magadha – Haryanka dynasty

  • Kasi first powerful, then Kosala; struggle among Magadha, Kosala, Vrijji, Avanti → Magadha = first Indian empire.
  • Bimbisara (Haryanka; contemporary of Buddha): sister married to Prasenajit of Kosala → Kasi as dowry; married Lichchhavi and Madra princesses; friendly with Avanti; annexed Anga.
  • Ajatashatru killed his father; war with Kosala (Prasenajit took back Kasi) → Kosala annexed; defeated Lichchhavis and Mallas; died 461 BCE. Capital Rajgriha.
  • Shishunaga (viceroy of Benaras) founded next dynasty; ruled 50 yrs.

4.2 Nandas – first empire builders

  • Nandas ruled Magadha from 362 BCE; first ruler Mahapadma Nanda (murdered last Shishunaga); succeeded by 8 sons = navanandas. Exterminated kshatriya clans.
  • Hathigumpha (elephant cave) inscription, Udayagiri near Bhubaneshwar – aqueduct built by King Nanda 300 years earlier. Last Nanda = Dhana Nanda; Nandas unpopular.

4.3 Persian and Macedonian invasions

  • Gandhara part of Achaemenid (Persian) Empire. Cyrus invaded c. 530 BCE, destroyed Kapisha. Herodotus: Gandhara = 20th and richest satrapy. Darius I inscriptions list "Gadara, Haravati, Maka".
  • Taxila (Takshashila, Pakistan): centre of learning; excavated by Sir John Marshall (1940s); Panini compiled Ashtadhyayi here.
  • Impact of Persian contact: Kharosthi script (from Aramaic, written right to left; used by Ashoka in Gandhara); Persian sigloi (silver coin); word karsa (coin) Persian; Ashokan edicts use lipi (Iranian dipi); bell-shaped capitals (Sarnath lion, Rampurva bull) and Pataliputra pillared hall resemble Achaemenid ones.
  • "Hindu" first appears in Darius I's inscription at Persepolis (Sindhu → Hindu → Greek Indu → India).
  • Bogaz Koi inscription (1380 BCE) – Hittite–Mitanni treaty naming Indara, Uruvna (Varuna), Mitira, Nasatiya (Ashvins). Indologist Thomas Burrow – only phonetic change.

4.4 Alexander's invasion (327–325 BCE)

  • During Dhana Nanda's reign. Ambhi of Taxila surrendered (326 BCE). Porus (between Jhelum and Beas) fought the Battle of Hydaspes – captured, throne restored. Soldiers refused to go further; Alexander died of fever in Babylon.
  • Impact: Greek satrapies; four trade routes opened; Greek settlements – Alexandria (near Kabul), Boukephala (near Peshawar), Alexandria (Sindh); helped Chandragupta.

4.6 Mauryan state and polity

  • Centralised but practically decentralised. Arthasastra = prescriptive text (dated a few centuries later). Salaries: chief minister, purohita, army commander 48,000 panas; soldier 500 panas.
  • Provinces (governed by princes): Suvarnagiri (near Kurnool), Ujjain (Avanti), Taxila (NW), Tosali (Odisha); capital region Pataliputra ruled directly. Secretaries = mahamatriyas.
  • Samaharta – collector-general (≈ finance minister). District – sthanika; 5–10 villages – gopa; town – nagarika; village – gramani + elders.
  • Land revenue 1/6 of produce in theory, often 1/4. State monopolies: forests, mines, salt.
  • Courts: Dharmasthiya (civil – marriage, inheritance) and Kantakasodhana ("removal of thorns" – criminal, police-like); each 3 judges + 3 amatyas.
  • Ashoka's dharmic state: yuktas, rajjukas, pradesikas to tour every 5 years (Major Rock Edict 3); "all men are my children" (Kalinga Rock Edict 1); be informed promptly (MRE 6); tolerance of all religions (MRE 7 & 12); hospitals for humans and animals (MRE 2).

4.7 Economy and society

  • Greeks: two crops a year; Megasthenes – sugarcane "reed that produced honey", cotton "trees on which wool grew".
  • Textile regions (Arthasastra): Kasi, Vanga, Kamarupa, Madurai; silk called "Chinese silk". Polished Chunar stone for Ashoka's pillars.
  • Guilds: seni/sreni headed by pamukha and jettha; disputes settled by mahasetthi. Caravan leader = maha-sarthavaha.
  • Exports to Egypt: indigo, ivory, tortoiseshell, pearls, perfumes. Currency: silver pana; punch-marked coins; Bindusara karshapana.
  • Urbanisation: Kaushambi, Bhita, Vaishali, Rajagriha; Sangam poems describe Madurai, Kanchipuram, Poompuhar; Sanchi sculptures show cities.
  • Pataliputra: confluence of Ganga and Son; parallelogram, 14 km × 2 km; wooden wall, 64 gates, 570 watch towers; moat; administered by corporation of 30 members.
  • Literature: Panini (c. 500 BCE); Katyayana (contemporary of Nandas, commentary on Panini). Buddhist/Jain texts in Pali.
  • Decline: weak successors; empire split; invasions (Indo-Greeks, Sakas, Kushanas); last ruler Brihadratha killed c. 185 BCE by commander Pushyamitra Sunga → Sunga dynasty.

5.1 South India during Mauryan times

  • Ashokan edicts (c. 270–230 BCE): Rock Edict II lists Cholas, Pandyas, Keralaputras, Satiyaputra as neighbours; Ashoka provided medical treatment for humans and animals there. Mauryan empire included N. Karnataka & Andhra; Tamil kingdoms independent.

5.2 South India under the Satavahanas

  • Emerged 1st c. BCE; started in Telangana → Godavari basin with capital Prathistan (Paithan, Maharashtra) → coastal Andhra. Pliny: 30 walled towns in Andhra country.
  • Gautamiputra Satakarni – greatest; defeated Shaka ruler Nahapana, restruck his coins; mother Gautami Balashri's Nashik inscription calls him conqueror of Shakas, Pahlavas, Yavanas; performed asvamedha.
  • Vasishthiputra Pulumayi expanded frontiers; Yagnashri Satakarni – coins with ship motif.
  • Hala – Gaha Sattasai: 700 love poems in Maharashtri Prakrit (themes like Sangam poetry).
  • Declined 3rd c. CE → Ikshvakus, then Pallavas (Andhra) and Kadambas (N. Karnataka).
  • Importance: land grants to Buddhists & Brahmins (Naneghat inscription – tax exemption to Buddhist monks); first big Deccan state; rock-cut caves on Konkan trade routes; Indo-Roman trade.

5.5 Tamil polity

  • Three levels: Kizhar (village/small territory heads, later nadu), Velir (hill & forest chiefs between muvendar territories – Athiyaman, Pari, Ay, Evvi, Irungo; patrons of poets), Vendar (kings of fertile lands).
  • Debate: state vs pre-state chiefdoms (no stratification, no clear territory, destructive war, no taxation). Counter: differentiation in Marutham; muvendar territories clear (Greco-Roman texts); Puratthinai war theme; taxes at highways and Kaviripattinam port; Chera got resources from hills & Musiri; trade (late 1st c.   • Vendar emerged from Iron Age chiefs (c. 1100–300 BCE). Titles: Kadungo, Imayavaramban, Vanavaramban, Peruvazhuthi; court = avaiyam.

5.6 Society and economy

  • War captives in cult centres; slaves; women in production and poetry.
  • Crafts at Arikamedu, Uraiyur, Kanchipuram, Kaviripattinam, Madurai, Korkai, Pattanam (Kerala). Maduraikanchi – day markets and night markets.
  • Manimekalai: Magadha artisans, Maratha mechanics, Malva smiths, Yavana carpenters worked with Tamil craftsmen.
  • Trade terms in Tamil-Brahmi: vanikan, chattan, nigama; salt merchants = umanar (bullock carts with families); chattu = itinerant merchants. Barter common; Roman coins circulated as bullion; Roman amphora, glass; Roman coin hoards in Coimbatore region.

Keeladi excavations (TN State Archaeology Dept)

  • On the banks of Vaigai → urbanisation in TN c. 6th c. BCE, like Gangetic plains.
  • Six carbon samples (4th season, 2018) sent to Beta Analytic Lab, Miami, Florida – AMS dating: sample at 353 cm depth = 580 BCE. Tamil-Brahmi potsherds of 580 BCE → high literacy by 6th c. BCE.
  • Bricks ratio 1:4:6; 180 spindle whorls, 20 bone tips, loom weights, copper needle (weaving).
  • Deccan College, Pune tested 70 bone samples: cow/ox (Bos indicus), buffalo, sheep, goat, nilgai, blackbuck, wild boar, peacock.
  • Agate & carnelian beads (raw material via Maharashtra & Gujarat); red ware like Arretine ware of Rome (2nd c. BCE); rouletted ware.
  • Terracotta: human (13), animal (3) figurines, ~600 toys/gamesmen, 28 ear ornaments; dice, hopscotch; "sillu" = rounded potsherd game pieces; 7 gold ornaments; 4,000+ beads; iron nails, knives. Similarities between Keeladi graffiti and Indus signs.

Porunai (Thamirabarani) – "Cradle of Tamil Civilisation"

  • Porunai = only perennial river in TN; flows through Tirunelveli and Thoothukudi.
  • Adichanallur: first excavated 1876 by Dr Jagor; Alexander Rea 1899–1905 (artefacts in Madras Government Museum); ASI 2004; TN State Archaeology 2019–2021.
  • Korkai: TN excavation 1968; ongoing 2020–21; conch/shell-bangle industry.
  • Sivakalai (2019, near Adichanallur): husked paddy in urn dated (Beta Analytic, Miami) to 1155 BCE → Thamirabarani civilisation ≈ 3200 years old.
  • Finds: urns 3–12 ft deep; black-and-red ware; 500+ graffiti and Tamili (Tamil-Brahmi) potsherds; gold diadems; high-tin bronze vessels; female figurines like Indus; 32 iron weapons; water/sewage structure with 29 brick courses and terracotta pipes; foreign ceramics (trade before 6th c. BCE).

5.7 Ideology and religion

  • Buddhism reached S. India & Sri Lanka in Ashoka's time; legend of Chandragupta Maurya's move to Karnataka. Satavahanas, Sangam kings, Ikshvakus supported Vedic sacrifices; varnasrama not yet rooted in Tamil region.
  • Buddhist centres: Amaravati, Nagarjunakonda (Krishna–Godavari delta); in TN only Kaviripattinam, Kanchipuram. Jainism more influential in TN (Tamil-Brahmi cave shelters); Jains contributed much to post-Sangam literature.

5.8 Age of Kalabhras – post-Sangam (c. 300–600 CE)

  • Earlier called "interregnum / dark age" – no longer accepted: Tirukkural, 18 minor works, Silappathikaram and Manimekalai belong to this period; Jainism & Buddhism influential.
  • Pulangurichi inscriptions (Sivagangai, mid-5th c.) name kings Chendan and Kurran (possibly Kalabhras).
  • Kalabhras uprooted by Pandyas c. third quarter of 6th c. CE → Pallavas (north TN) and Pandyas (south).

6.1 Indo-Greek relations

  • Seleucus Nicator (Alexander's general) ruled from Phrygia (Turkey) to Indus after 311 BCE; defeated by Chandragupta c. 305 BCE → peace treaty: land up to Indus ceded, 500 war elephants, marriage agreement; Megasthenes = first Greek ambassador to Pataliputra.
  • Bindusara: ambassadors from Ptolemy II of Egypt; correspondence with Antiochus of Syria.
  • Ashoka's Rock Edict 13 names five yona kings: Antiochus II Theos (Syria), Ptolemy II Philadelphus (Egypt), Antigonus Gonatas (Macedonia), Magas (Cyrene), Alexander (Corinth).
  • Yavana/yona from Persian yauna (Greeks) – later all people from E. Mediterranean.
  • Exports to West: ivory, tortoise shell, pearls, indigo, spikenard/nard (fragrant oil, Gangetic region), malabathrum (cinnamon leaf), rare woods.
  • Indo-Greek kings: Diodotus (Bactria) revolted against Antiochus II; Euthydemus (212 BCE); Antiochus III defeated Indian king Subhagasena; Demetrius (c. 200 BCE); Demetrius II = first known Indo-Greek king (c. 175 BCE) – bilingual square coins (Greek obverse, Kharoshti reverse). Yavana raid on Ayodhya (Saketa); land ceded to Pushyamitra Sunga. Bactria lost c. 165 BCE; 30+ kings known from coins. Indo-Greek hallmark = exquisite portrait coinage.
  • Menander (c. 165/145–130 BCE): best known; coins from Kabul–Indus to W. UP; attacked Pataliputra; Kalinga's Kharavela (Hathigumpha) could not stop him; coins call him "king" and "soter" (saviour); hero of Milindapanha with monk Nagasena; became Buddhist.
  • Antialcidas (c. 110 BCE): envoy Heliodorus to King Bhagabhadra erected Garuda-dhvaja pillar at Vidisha (MP) in honour of Vasudeva (Krishna).

6.2 Sakas, Parthians and Kushanas

  • Chinese Great Wall pushed Yueh-chi west → they pushed Sakas (Scythians) → Parthian Mithradates pushed Sakas to NW India.
  • Sakas: first ruler Maues/Moga (c. 80 BCE, Gandhara); Azes ended Indo-Greek rule, extended to Mathura. Governors = kshatrapas/satraps (mahakshatrapas). Rudradaman (130–150 CE) – Junagadh inscription; defeated Satavahanas.
  • Parthians (Pahlavas): Gondophernes conquered Kabul (c. 43 CE); records in Peshawar district.
  • Kushanas (Yueh-chi): Kujula Kadphises (conquered Afghanistan) → Wima Kadphises (to Mathura).
  • Kanishka: 78 to 101/102 CE (78 CE = start of Saka era; reign start debated 78–144 CE); 4th Buddhist Council; Mahayana; missions to China. Coins: "king of kings", "Caesar"; Roman weight standard; statue near Mathura in tunic & boots. Rock of Hunza (Karakoram highway, completed 1979) names two Kadphises and "Kusanadevaputra Maharaja Kaniska". Conquered Magadha, Kashmir, Khotan. Later kings: Huvishka, Vasishka, Vasudeva.
  • Art & literature: Gandhara art (Indo-Greek; Buddha with half-closed eyes; from 1st c. CE); Mathura red sandstone; rock caves from Ajanta to Kanheri (Mumbai). Kanishka patronised Asvaghosha, Parsva, Vasumitra, Nagarjuna. Asvaghosha – Buddhacharita; first Sanskrit play Sariputraprakarana (9 acts). Bhasa's plays rediscovered ~100 yrs ago in South India. Manusmriti, Kamasutra (Vatsyayana), Arthasastra took final shape by 2nd c. CE.

6.3 The Tamil kingdoms

  • Satavahanas in Deccan (1st c. CE) – provincial governors autonomous. Tamil region: muvendar – Pandyas (Madurai), Cholas (Uraiyur, now suburb of Tiruchi), Cheras (Vanji = Karur) + velir chiefs. Ashoka's 2nd rock edict names them.
  • Sources: Sangam literature (3rd c. BCE–3rd c. CE), Silappadikaram, Manimekalai; Periplus Maris Erythraei (1st c. CE) – most reliable for ports.

Contours of international trade

  • Rome became empire in 27 BCE (Augustus) → demand for spices, textiles.
  • Hippalus, Egyptian sailor, discovered monsoon winds (1st c. CE) → ended Arab monopoly; ships rose from ~20 a year to almost one a day.

6.4 Trade between Tamizhagam and Rome

  • Arikamedu near Pondicherry – Indo-Roman trading station.
  • Before 1st c. CE Roman ships stayed on west coast; traders crossed Palghat pass. Beryl mines: Kodumanal (Erode), Padiyur (Tiruppur), Vaniyampadi (Vellore); iron & steel at Chennimalai (Erode). Early Roman coins concentrated in Coimbatore, Erode, Salem, Karur.
  • West coast ports: Naura (Cannanore), Tyndis/Tondi (Ponnani), Musiri/Muziris – traditionally Kodungallur, now located at Pattanam. Muziris papyrus – agreement between merchants of Muziris and Alexandria. Musiri: rice for fish (barter) and pepper for gold.
  • Exports to Rome: pepper, pearls, ivory, silk cloth, spikenard, malabathrum, sapphire, beryl, diamonds, tortoiseshell, fine cotton textiles. Imports: coin, topaz, antimony, coral, crude glass, copper, tin, lead, wine.
  • Trade peaked under Tiberius; drain of 55 million sesterces a year; Vespasian curbed luxury imports → only cotton and pepper. Chinese silk shipped via Coromandel ports.
  • Yavanas: lived in separate quarters at Puhar (most important Coromandel port); yavana guards with swords at Madurai fort; yavana metal-workers and carpenters ("hard-eyed").
  • Money: Roman coins + local imitations; Chera coins in Amaravati river bed; salt merchants bartered.

7.4 Administration

  • Titles: maharajadhiraja, parama-bhattaraka, parameshvara; parama-bhagavata (worshipper of Vasudeva Krishna); Samudragupta compared to Purusha.
  • Kumaramatya – high officer (6 Vaishali seals); amatya (Bita seals). Harisena was Kumaramatya, Sandhivigrahika, Mahadandanayaka; son of Dhruvabhuti.
  • Sandhivigrahika – minister for peace and war; dandanayaka/mahadandanayaka – judicial/military; mahashvapati – cavalry commander. Posts hereditary.
  • Provinces = deshas/bhuktis under uparikas (title maharaja in Damodarpur plates); districts = visayas under vishayapatis. Eran pillar (Budhagupta) – Surashmichandra as lokpala between Kalindi and Narmada.
  • Below district: vithi, bhumi, pathaka, peta; ayuktakas; village – gramika, gramadhyaksha. Damodarpur plate (Budhagupta) – ashtakula-adhikarana (board of 8) under mahattara. Sanchi – panch-mandali.
  • Army: baladhikrita, mahabaladhikrita; "senapati" absent in Gupta records; ranabhandagaradhikarana (military store); dandapashika (police); mahapratihara (palace guards); khadyatapakita (royal kitchen); spies = dutakas.

7.5 Economic condition

  • Akshapataladhikrita – keeper of royal records. Revenue terms in inscriptions: klipta, bali, udranga, uparikara; vesti (vishti) = forced labour.
  • Paharpur copper plate – king sole owner of land; ustapala kept land records.
  • Land types: Kshetra (cultivable), Khila (waste), Aprahata (jungle), Vasti (habitable), Gapata Saraha (pasture).
  • Irrigation: Narada Smriti – dykes bardhya (flood protection) and khara (irrigation); drains jalanirgamah (Amarasimha); Sudarsana lake at foot of Girnar.
  • Peasants reduced to serfs; tenants-at-will.

7.6 Cultural florescence

  • Nagara and Dravida styles evolved. Rock-cut caves: Ajanta, Ellora, Bagh, Udayagiri. Structural temple types (5); shikhara = Nagara feature. Stupas: Samat (UP), Ratnagiri (Odisha), Mirpur Khas (Sind).
  • Sculpture: erect Buddha of Sarnath; great Varaha at Udayagiri; copper Buddha 18 ft at Nalanda; Sultanganj Buddha 7.5 ft.
  • Painting: Ajanta, Bagh, Badami – Ajanta murals are not true frescoes (painted after plaster set); Madhyadesa school. Pottery: "red ware" (Ahichchhatra, Rajgarh, Hastinapur, Bashar).
  • Buddhist literature: Arya Deva, Arya Asanga; Vasubandhu – first Buddhist work on logic; disciple Dignaga. Jaina: Vimala – Jaina Ramayana; Siddasena Divakara – Jaina logic.
  • Kalidasa: Sakunthalam, Malavikagnimitram, Vikramaurvashiyam. Sudraka – Mrichchhakatika; Visakhadatta – Mudrarakshasa, Devichandraguptam. Elite spoke Sanskrit, commoners Prakrit in dramas.
  • Prakrits: Suraseni (Mathura), Ardha Magadhi (Awadh, Bundelkhand), Magadhi (Bihar). Sanskrit = official language.
  • Grammar: Panini (Ashtadhyayi), Patanjali (Mahabhashya); Amarakosa (Sanskrit thesaurus) by Amarasimha; Chandravyakaranam by Chandragomin (Bengal).
  • Puranas got present form; 18 major Puranas; Mahabharata & Ramayana given final shape.
  • Nalanda: ~95 km SE of Patna near Bihar Sharif; 5th c. to c. 1200 CE; UNESCO site; Sailendra king built a monastery; destroyed by Bakhtiyar Khalji c. 1200; excavations from 1915 – 11 monasteries, 6 brick temples on 12 ha.
  • Science: zero & decimal; Aryabhatta (Surya Siddhanta, solar eclipse, earth rotates on axis; Aryabhattiyam); Varahamihira – Brihat Samhita, Panch Siddhantika, Brihat Jataka; Brahmagupta – Brahmasphuta-siddhanta, Khandakhadyaka; Navanitakam (medical recipes); Hastyayurveda (veterinary) by Palakapya.

7.7 Decline

  • Vakataka Narendrasena attacked Malwa, Mekala, Kosala (under Budhagupta); Harishena took Malwa, Gujarat. Huns in 6th c. took Malwa, Gujarat, Punjab, Gandhara. New powers: Yasodharman (Malwa), Maukharis (UP), Maitrakas (Saurashtra).
  • Feudalism (R.S. Sharma): land grants, transfer of fiscal/judicial rights, forced labour, decline of trade & coinage, officials paid by land revenue, obligations of samantas.

Unit 8 · Harsha and Rise of Regional Kingdoms (PDF p. 108–120)

I. Harsha (606–647 CE)

  • Post-Gupta states: Maitrakas (Saurashtra, capital Valabhi), Maukharis (Agra, Awadh), Vakatakas; Pushyabhutis of Thaneswar.
  • Sources: Bana's Harshacharita (first formal biography of a king), Hiuen Tsang's Si-Yu-Ki; Madhuban & Banskhera copper plates, Sonpat copper seal, Nalanda clay seals, Aihole inscription.
  • Pushyabhuti – founder of Vardhana dynasty. Prabhakaravardhana (580–605) fought Gurjaras and Huns; daughter Rajyasri married Maukhari Grahavarman of Kanauj. Rajyavardhana (605–606) murdered by Sasanka, Gauda king of Bengal.
  • Kanauj magnates (minister Poni) invited Harsha; titles Rajputra, Siladitya; capital moved Thanesar → Kanauj. Rescued Rajyasri in Vindhyas (from Deva Gupta of Malwa); she converted him to Buddhism.
  • Allied with Kamarupa (Assam) against Sasanka; Gauda subdued only after Sasanka's death. Maitraka Dhruvabhatta married Harsha's daughter. Defeated by Pulikesin II (Chalukya) – who took title "Parameswara".
  • Ruled 41 years; real sway Ganga–Yamuna. China: T'ang emperor Tai Tsung sent embassies 643 and 647.
  • Administration: Mantri Parishad. Officials: Avanti (foreign relations & war), Simhananda (C-in-C), Kuntala (cavalry), Skandagupta (elephants), Dirghadhvajas (messengers), Banu (records), Mahaprathihara (palace guard), Sarvagata (secret service). Soldiers Chatas, Bhatas; cavalry officers Brihadisvaras; infantry Baladhikritas. Divisions: province → bhukti → vishaya → pathaka → village.
  • Taxes: Bhaga (1/6, in kind), Hiranya (cash); no reference to Bali. Crown land in 4 parts: state, officials, men of letters, religious charity. Justice: Mimamsakas; criminal law harsher than Guptas; trial by ordeal.
  • Hiuen Tsang: born 612; in India 630–643 (~13 yrs); "prince of pilgrims"; 5 yrs at Nalanda; took 150 relics, 657 manuscripts. Kanauj replaced Pataliputra; floors smeared with cow dung; Indians mostly vegetarian; caste firm (butchers, fishermen etc. outside city); purdah not among higher class; sati – Yasomatidevi (Prabhakaravardhana's wife).
  • Religion: Siva worshipper till 631; then Mahayana Buddhist. 643 assemblies at Kanauj (20 kings incl. Bhaskaravarman of Kamarupa) and Prayag; quinquennial Mahamoksha Parishad at Prayag.
  • Literature: Bana – Harshacharita, Kadambari; Harsha wrote Priyadarsika, Ratnavali, Nagananda; ¼ of revenue on culture. Nalanda: head Shilabhadra (from Assam); 10,000 students.

II. The Palas (Bengal)

  • Triangular contest for Kanauj: Pratiharas (Jalore), Palas (Bengal), Rashtrakutas (Deccan).
  • Sasanka (590–625) – first prominent king of Bengal. Gopala elected by chiefs in 750 CE; kingdom in Vanga; founded Odantapuri monastery.
  • Dharmapala (770–815): founded Vikramasila (Bhagalpur), Somapura vihara (Paharpur, Bangladesh); patron of Haribhadra.
  • Devapala: up to Kamarupa; defeated Rashtrakuta Amoghavarsha; gave 5 villages to Sailendra king Balaputradeva (Sumatra) for monastery at Nalanda.
  • Mahipala I: revived; checked Rajendra Chola's advance (1020–1025). Ramapala ruled 53 yrs. Last – Madanapala (1130–1150) expelled by Vijayasena (Sena dynasty).
  • Culture: Mahayana; artists Dhiman & Vitapala; scholars Atisha, Saraha, Tilopa…; Agama Shastra (Gaudapada), Nyaya Kundali (Sridhar Bhatta); medicine – Chakrapani Datta; Mahipalageet folk songs; Sandhyakar Nandi's Ramacharitam (on Ramapala).

III. The Rashtrakutas

  • Arab traveller Al-Masudi noted Pratihara–Rashtrakuta enmity. Claim descent from Rathikas (Ashokan edicts). Earlier feudatories of Chalukyas of Vatapi.
  • Dantidurga: after Vikramaditya II (733–746) conquered Nandipuri, Malwa, Berar; defeated Kirtivarman II (746–753, last Chalukya of Vatapi); daughter married to Pallava Nandivarman II; died 756; Hiranyagarbha ritual at Ujjain.
  • Krishna I (756–775): defeated Gangas; built Kailasanatha temple, Ellora (monolith). Govinda II; Dhruva (780–794) – zenith; defeated Pallava Dantivarman, Vatsaraja and Dharmapala.
  • Govinda III (794–814): subdued Pallava Dantika; overlord of Deccan.
  • Amoghavarsha (814–878, 64 yrs): patron of Jinasena, Sakatayana, mathematician Mahaviracharya; wrote Kavirajamarga (earliest Kannada work on poetics) and Prasnottaramalika (Sanskrit); Jain; 5 Jain caves at Ellora.
  • Krishna III (939–968), last great: with Butunga captured Kanchi and Thanjavur (943); held Thondaimandalam; defeated Chola Rajaditya at Takkolam (949) (Vellore dt); victory pillar at Rameshvaram.
  • Literature: Jinasena – Adipurana; Gunabhadra – Mahapurana; three gems of Kannada – Ponna, Pampa, Ranna. Buddhism only at Kanheri.
  • Art: Ellora and Elephanta; Kailasanatha similar to Lokesvara temple, Pattadakal (Vikramaditya II, victory over Pallavas). Elephanta: Maheshamurti 3-faced Siva bust 25 ft; Nataraja, Sadashiva, Ardhanarishvara.

9.1 Chalukyas of Badami (Vatapi)

  • Two families: Chalukyas of Badami and of Kalyani (only Badami here).
  • Pulikesin I (c. 543–566): founder; fortified hill near Badami; independence from Kadambas; asvamedha. Kirtivarman (566–597) founded capital Badami. Mangalesa (597–609).
  • Pulikesin II (609–642): defeated Mangalesa; defeated Harsha on the Narmada; beat Kadambas of Banavasi, Gangas of Talakad; attack on Kanchi thwarted by Mahendravarman; killed when Narasimhavarman I took Badami. Replaced by Rashtrakutas mid-8th c.
  • Kuram plate: Narasimhavarman wrote "vijaya" on Pulikesin's back in battles of Pariyala, Manimangala, Suramara and destroyed Vatapi (like Agastya).
  • Titles: Maharajan, Sathyasrayan, Sri-Prithvi-Vallabhan; Parameswaran after beating Harsha. Hirahadagalli plates – agnistoma, vajapeya, asvamedha.
  • Emblem: Chalukyas – wild boar (varaha); Pallavas – bull.
  • Royal women: Chalukya ladies as provincial governors; Vijaya Bhattarika issued inscriptions. Pallava queen Rangapataka (Rajasimha's queen) in Kailasanatha inscription.
  • Ministers: Pradhana (head), Mahasandhivigrahika (foreign), Amatya (revenue), Samaharta (exchequer). Divisions: Vishayam, Rastram, Nadu, Grama; vishaya → pukti (head pogapati). Village: nala-kavundas (revenue), kamunda/pokigan (headman), karana/gramani (accountant), mahajanam (law & order), mahapurush; towns – Nagarapatis/Purapatis.
  • Religion: Saivism & Vaishnavism; Kartikeya; Jains (Ravikirti Jain; Jain temple at Annigere); Hiuen Tsang – 5000+ Buddhists.
  • Literature: Sanskrit inscriptions (Aihole, Mahakudam); Badami inscription calls Kannada the local prakrit; Saptavataram (Sanskrit grammar).
  • Architecture (soft sandstone): Aihole (Ayyavole merchants' guild HQ; ~70 temples; earliest Lad Khan temple; Durga temple on Buddhist chaitya model; Huccimalligudi; Megudi Jain temple). Badami – 4 caves; largest Vishnu cave by Mangalesa (reclining Vishnu, Narasimha). Pattadakal (Bagalkot) – royal rituals; Virupaksha temple built by queen Lokamahadevi to mark Vikramaditya II's conquest of Kanchi (copied Rajasimha's Mamallapuram style); architect titled Tribhuvanacharya; Papanatha temple (northern shikhara, architect Revadi Ovajja); Indo-Aryan & Dravidian groups.
  • Painting: Vakataka style; Mangalesa's palace painting.

9.2 Pallavas

  • Origin debated (Pahlava/Parthian theory vs native). Region Tondaimandalam – between north Pennar and north Vellar; capital Kanchipuram.
  • Simhavishnu (son of Simhavarman): defeated Kalabhras, took Chola land to Kaveri.
  • Mahendravarman I (590–630): converted from Jainism to Saivism by Appar; poet & musician; Pulikesin II reached near Kanchi; introduced rock-cut temples – Mandagappattu inscription: shrine to Brahma, Isvara, Vishnu without brick, timber, metal, mortar.
  • Narasimhavarman I (630–668): with Sri Lankan prince Manavarman; general Paranjothi captured Badami; two naval expeditions for Manavarman.
  • Paramesvaravarman I (670–695) vs Chalukya Vikramaditya. Rajasimha (700–728) – Shore temple. Narasimhavarman II (695–722) – Nagapattinam Buddhist temple.
  • Rashtrakuta Govinda III invaded Kanchi under Dantivarman. Nandivarman III (with Gangas, Cholas) defeated Pandyas at Thirupurambiyam. Aparajita killed fighting Aditya I Chola → Tondaimandalam to Cholas.
  • Cheras: Chera Perumals in Kerala 6th–9th c.
  • Administration: divine, hereditary kingship; Mantri Mandala; mantri (diplomat) vs amatya (counsellor); Rahasyadhikrita (private secretary); Manikkappandaram-Kappan (treasury keeper); Kodukkappillai (gifts); Kosa-adhyaksa (supervisor of treasury). Courts Adhikarna Mandapa, judges Dharmadhikarins; Kasakudi plates (Nandivarman) – fines Karanadandam (higher court) and Adhikaranadandam (district).
  • Villages: ordinary (tax-paying), brahmadeya (to Brahmins, tax-free), devadana (to temples). Urrukkattukottam plates (found 1879 near Puducherry): 11 plates on a ring, seal with bull & lingam; Nandivarman's 22nd year (753 CE); Sanskrit + Tamil.
  • Sabha (formal village assembly) + urar (informal gathering of village); nadu (district).
  • Eripatti = tank land (only in S. India), revenue for tank upkeep; tank committee; excess water taxed.
  • Land revenue 1/6 to 1/10; extra taxes on draught cattle, toddy-drawers, marriage parties, potters, goldsmiths, washermen, weavers, brokers, ghee-makers etc.
  • Army: foot, cavalry, few elephants; navy with dockyards at Mamallapuram and Nagapattinam. Vaikunta Perumal temple (Kanchi) sculptures show Nandivarman's events.
  • Trade: Kanchipuram; merchant body Manigramam; guilds sudesi, nanadesi, ainurruvar; foreign merchants = Nanadesi (flag with bull, issued virasasanas; chief = pattanswamy/pattnakilar/dandanayaka; members ayyavole-parameswariyar; main guild at Aihole). Exports to Java, Sumatra, Cambodia, Sri Lanka, China, Burma. SE Asian kingdoms: Kambuja (Cambodia), Champa (Annam), Srivijaya.
  • Society: Brahmins tax-free, exempt from capital punishment; sat-kshatriyas; Aryanisation; Sanskrit esteemed.
  • Religion & learning: Kanchi university (fame like Nalanda); Hiuen Tsang saw 100 monasteries, 10,000 Mahayana monks at Kanchi; mathas (8th c.); Jain centres near Madurai, Kanchi; Shravanabelagola.
  • Sanskrit: Bharavi – Kiratarjuniya; Dandin – Dashakumaracharita, Kavyadarsa (Dandin of Kanchi at Pallava court).

II. Ellora, Ajanta, Mamallapuram

  • Ellora & Ajanta in Aurangabad district; Ellora famous for sculpture, Ajanta for painting; c. 500–950 CE; first cave temple made for Ajivikas.
  • Ellora (9.3): 34 caves in Charanadri hills; Chalukya to Rashtrakuta patrons; UNESCO 1983; 12 Buddhist caves (Buddha in dhyana, vyakhyana, bhumi-sparsha mudras; goddesses Tara etc.); Jain caves (Mahavira, Parsvanatha, Gomatesvara); Kailasanatha (cave 16) monolith – life-size elephants, Ravana lifting Kailasa, Ganga on crocodile, Yamuna on tortoise; murals preserved only in Kailasa.
  • Ajanta (9.4): ~100 km N of Aurangabad; 30 caves in volcanic rock; started by Hinayana; phases c. 200 BCE–200 CE and 200–400 CE; early paintings in caves 9 & 10 (Satavahana); plaster of vegetable fibre, husk, grit, sand + lime; colours black, red, white, yellow, blue, green; Jataka themes; chaityas (vaulted halls) and viharas; Avalokitesvara.
  • Mamallapuram (9.5): Shore Temple by Rajasimha (700–728) – three shrines (Siva, Vishnu); Panchapandava Rathas (monolithic) – Dharmaraja Ratha (3-storey vimana), Arjuna, Bhima Ratha (rectangular); Descent of the Ganga (Bhagiratha's / Arjuna's Penance); Krishna mandapa (village life).

III. Devotional movement and literature

  • 9.6 Tamil devotionalism: Bhakti patronised by Pallavas & Pandyas, 6th–9th c.; 12 Azhwars, 63 Nayanmars; women: Andal (Azhwar), Karaikkal Ammaiyar (book writes "Tilakawathi"; her name was Punithavathi – extra) and Pandya queen Mangayarkkarasiyar (Nayanmars). Themes: devotion to personal god; protest against Brahmin exclusiveness; condemnation of Jains & Buddhists. Veena with gourd body ~7th c.
  • 9.7 Azhwars: hymns compiled as Nalayira Divya Prabandham (4000 poems) by Nathamuni (priest at Srirangam), end of 9th c. Periyalvar – Srivilliputtur, under Pandya Srimara Srivallabha; Krishna's childhood. Andal – love for Krishna. Nammalvar of Kurugur (Alvar Tirunagari, Thoothukudi) – greatest Alvar; four works incl. Tiruvaymoli (essence of four Vedas).
  • Nayanmars: hymns compiled into 11 books by Nambi Andar Nambi (end of 10th c.). Thevaram = first 7 books – Sambandar (I–III), Appar (IV–VI), Sundarar (VII); Manikkavasagar (VIII). Sekkizhar's Periyapuranam = 12th Tirumurai (hagiography of 63 Nayanmars). Whole = Panniru Tirumurai.
  • Impact: temples became social institutions; Brahmin settlements; festivals. Kesavan Veluthat: Bhakti = cementing force of kings, Brahmins, people.
  • 9.8 Adi Sankara (788–820): from Kaladi, Kerala; doctrine of Maya; Advaita (non-dualism); mathas at Sringeri, Dvaraka, Badrinath, Puri; smarta tradition.
  • 9.9 Sri Ramanujar (1017–1138): born Sriperumbudur; trained under Yadavaprakasar at Kanchi; invited by Yamunacharya to Srirangam; head of Srirangam monastery; Visishtadvaita (qualified monism); let groups outside varnashrama enter temples once a year; later split – Vedanta Desikar and Manavala Mamuni schools.

10.1 Arab conquest of Sind (712)

  • Hajjaj bin Yusuf (governor of Iraq) sent Muhammad bin Qasim (17-yr-old son-in-law) against Dahar of Sind; Brahmanabad captured (wazir betrayed Dahar); Debal port plundered 3 days. "Triumph without results".

Mahmud of Ghazni

  • Alaptigin seized Ghazni (963); Sabuktigin defeated Shahi Jayapal. Mahmud (from 997/998; aged 27) – title Yamin-ud-Daulah from Caliph; ruled 32 yrs; 17 campaigns; defeated Anandapala; raided Mathura, Kanauj; Somnath (1025) – 20 million dinars (claimed). Romila Thapar: Hindu/Jain sources silent on Somnath.
  • Al-Beruni came with Mahmud; learnt Sanskrit; translated Euclid into Sanskrit; took Aryabhattiyam to West.
  • Last Ghaznavid Khusrau Shah killed 1192 (Lahore taken 1186).

Muhammad Ghori

  • Took Multan (1175), Uchch; defeated by Chalukyas of Gujarat at Mt. Abu (1179).
  • First Battle of Tarain (1191) – Prithviraj Chauhan won; Second Battle of Tarain (1192) – Prithviraj defeated, later executed; Qutb-ud-din Aibak made deputy.
  • Defeated Jaya Chandra of Kanauj (enmity over Samyukta); Ghori assassinated on Indus banks.
  • Rajput dynasties: Tomaras (Delhi), Chauhans (Rajasthan), Solankis (Gujarat), Paramaras (Malwa – Bhoja), Gahadavalas (Kanauj), Chandelas (Bundelkhand – built Khajuraho: Lakshmana, Vishwanatha, Kandariya Mahadeva). Turks won by superior cavalry & mounted archery.

10.2 Slave (Mamluk) dynasty

  • Mamluk = property / slave. Qutb-ud-din Aibak (1206–1210): died playing chaugan (polo) at Lahore; first imposed jizya. General Bakhtiyar Khalji conquered Bihar & Bengal, destroyed Nalanda.
  • Iltutmish (1211–1236): Ilbari Turk, slave & son-in-law of Aibak (set aside Aram Shah); crushed Qabacha; refused help to Jalaluddin of Khwarizm against Chengiz Khan; completed Qutb Minar (243 ft); introduced silver and copper tanka; Corps of Forty (Chahalgani); slaves called Shamsi bandagan.
  • Raziya (1236–1240): Ibn Battuta – rode unveiled; elevated Abyssinian Jalal-ud-din Yaqut as Amir-i-Akhur; deposed during campaign against Altuniya.
  • Nasir-ud-din Mahmud (1246–66); Ulugh Khan = Balban (Sultan 1266–1287): abolished Corps of Forty; crushed Meos of Mewat; Tughril Khan (Bengal) beheaded; son Bughra Khan governor; forts at Bhatinda, Sunam, Samana; deal with Hulagu Khan (Mongols not beyond Sutlej; mission 1259); son Muhammad Khan killed by Mongols; claimed to be god's representative.

10.3 Khaljis (1290–1320)

  • Jalal-ud-din Khalji (1290–96): "Afghanized Turk"; halted Mongols (1292). Murdered by nephew Ala-ud-din (governor of Kara; raided Devagiri).
  • Ala-ud-din Khalji (1296–1316): Mongol raids till 1307–08; campaigns Devagiri (1296, 1307, 1314), Gujarat, Ranthambhor (1301), Chittor (1303), Malwa (1305). Malik Kafur: Devagiri 1307; Kakatiya Prataparudra (Warangal) 1309; Hoysala Vira Ballala III 1310; plundered Chidambaram, Srirangam, Madurai; returned 1311 (Tamil Muslims fought for Pandyas).
  • Reforms: confiscated grants; nobles' marriages need consent; liquor banned; land tax from cultivators directly; postal system; first Sultan to pay soldiers in cash; market/price control with spies.
  • After him Malik Kafur ruled 35 days; Ghazi Malik killed Khusrau → Tughlaq dynasty.

10.4 Tughlaqs (1320–1414)

  • Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq (1320–25). Muhammad bin Tughlaq (Jauna Khan, 1325–51): capital to Devagiri = Daulatabad and back; token (bronze) currency – forged, withdrawn; Diwan-i-Amir Kohi (agriculture department); Doab revolt 1332–34; rebellions in Bengal, Madurai, Warangal etc.; died 1351 in Gujarat.
  • Firuz Shah Tughlaq (1351–88): vizier Khan-i-Jahan (Brahmin convert Kannu from Warangal); hereditary offices; slave department (1,80,000 slaves); Sind campaign 1362; jizya even on Brahmins; canals Sutlej–Hansi and Yamuna; translated Rag Darpan; madrasa at Delhi; Hauz Khas; raised Qutb Minar to 74 m.
  • Timur sacked Delhi under Nasir-ud-din Muhammad Shah (1394–1412); took artisans to Samarkand.
  • Jizya: first by Aibak; abolished by Akbar; reimposed by Aurangzeb.

10.5–10.6 Sayyids and Lodis

  • Khizr Khan (Timur's deputy) founded Sayyid dynasty (1414–51), 4 Sultans; Alam Shah – only Sultan to abdicate voluntarily.
  • Lodi: Bahlul Lodi (1451–89) conquered Sharqi kingdom; Sikander Lodi (1489–1517) moved capital to Agra (1504); Ibrahim Lodi lost First Battle of Panipat (1526) to Babur.

10.7 Administration, society, culture

  • Sultans = lieutenants of the Caliph. Iqta holders = muqtis/walis; crown land = khalisa; Sultan's troops = hashm-i qalb; tax = half of produce; chaudhuris, khots (headmen).
  • Basava – Virashaiva/Lingayat (Karnataka, 13th c.); Siddhars in Tamil Nadu – one God, against caste; Namdev, Ramanand.
  • Economy: cash land tax; cities Delhi, Daulatabad, Multan, Cambay, Gulbarga; paper-making, spinning wheel (via Iran, 14th c.); sericulture in Bengal (15th c.).
  • Education: maktab, madrasa. Historiography: Chachnama. Sufi orders: Suhrawardi (Multan), Chishti (Delhi; Nizamuddin Auliya); Fawaid-ul-Fuad compiled by Amir Hassan.
  • Architecture: arch, dome, lime; Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque, Adhai din ka Jhopra (Ajmer); first true arch – Balban's tomb; first true dome – Alai Darwaza; Tughlaqabad; sloping walls of Ghiyas-ud-din's tomb; Qutb Minar 72.5 m → 74 m, 379 steps; arabesque.
  • Music: Rabab, Sarangi; Sufi Sama; Pir Bhodan. Literature: Amir Khusrau (Nuh Sipihr praises India); dictionaries Farhang-i-Qawas, Miftah-ul-Fuzala; Tuti Namah by Zia Nakshabi; Sanskrit Palam Baoli inscription (1276) praising Balban; Shrivara's Kathakautuka; Bhattavatara's Zainavilas.

I. CHOLAS – origin and sources

  • After Sangam age Cholas were subordinates of Pallavas in Kaveri region. Vijayalaya (850–871) took Kaveri delta from the Muttaraiyar, built Thanjavur, founded kingdom in 850 → "Later / Imperial Cholas".
  • Copper plates trace ancestry to Karikala; eponymous progenitor "Chola"; Killi, Kochenganan, Karikalan named.
  • Key rulers: Parantaka I (907–955), Rajaraja I (985–1014), Rajendra I (1014–1044), up to Kulothunga III (1163–1216).
  • Sources: 10,000+ inscriptions on stone & copper (temple endowments, land, taxes). Literature: Kalingattupparani, Kulotungancholan Pillai Tamizh (quasi-historical), Muvarula, Kamba Ramayanam; grammar – Neminatam, Viracholiyam, Nannul; Pandikkovai, Takkayagapparani.
  • Territory: Chonadu/Cholanadu; core = Kaveri delta Cholamandalam → "Coromandel". Expanded to Pudukkottai–Ramanathapuram, Kongu, Tondainadu (north), Pandinadu (south), Gangavadi (S. Karnataka), Malaimandalam (Kerala); NE Sri Lanka = Mummudi-Cholamandalam.

11.1.1 Empire building

  • Rajaraja I: naval victories on West coast, Sri Lanka; conquered the Maldives; N & E Sri Lanka directly ruled; Siva temple there (Siva Devale); Rajarajesvara temple at Mahatitta. Mentor Karuvur Devar. Built Brihadishvara temple, Thanjavur.
  • Rajaraja made Rajendra I heir; jointly ruled 2 years; boundary to Tungabhadra. Pandyas fled to Sri Lanka with crown → Rajendra conquered Sri Lanka and took the Pandya crown.
  • North Indian expedition (1023): Rajendra up to Godavari, general beyond → Gangaikonda Cholapuram built to commemorate (checked by Pala Mahipala I).
  • Naval expedition against Sri Vijaya (S. Sumatra; c. 700–1300) and Kadaram/Kheda (Kedah, Malaysia).
  • Invasions of Western Chalukyas: 1003 (Rajaraja I), 1009 (Rajendra I); capital Kalyani sacked; dwarapala from Kalyani installed at Darasuram (near Kumbakonam).
  • Rajendra's titles: Mudikonda Cholan, Gangaikondan, Kadaramkondan, Pandita Cholan. (Photo: Karantai copper plates of Rajendra I.)

11.1.2 Chola administration

  • King: hereditary; titles peruman/perumagan, ulagudaiyaperumal, ulagudaiyanayanar; later Chakkaravarti, Tiribhuvana Chakkaravarti; suffix deva at coronation; "comrade of god" (thambiran thozhar); rajagurus, Sarva-sivas. Land to Brahmins – brahmadeyams, chaturvedimangalams.
  • Viceroys (Rajaraja I): Chola-Pandya (Pandinadu), Chola-Lankeswara (Sri Lanka), Chola-Ganga (Gangavadi). Chiefs absorbed: Irukkuvels, Ilangovels, Mazhavas, Banas.
  • Army: infantry, cavalry (kudirai sevagar), elephants (anaiyatkal); bowmen villaligal, swordsmen valilar, spearmen konduvar; ranks perundanam / cirudanam; 13th-c. Chinese geographer: 60,000 war elephants. Military holding = padaipparru; cantonment = padaividu; outposts = nilaipadai; captain = nayagam → padaimudali; C-in-C = senapati, dandanayagam.

11.1.3 Economy

  • Land revenue department = puravuvari-tinaikkalam (chief: puravuvari-tinaikkala-nayagam).
  • Land surveys: Rajaraja I (1001), Kulotunga I (1086), Kulotunga III (1226); surveyors = naduvagaiseykira. Units: kuli, ma, veli, patti, padagam.
  • Taxes: irai, kanikadan, iraikattina-kanikadan, kadamai; kudimai (paid by tenants to govt & landlords); opati; tax in kind = iraikattina-nellu. Temples & Brahmins exempt.
  • Kalam = 28 kg of paddy. Rajaraja I: 100 kalam per veli (veli ≈ 6.5 acres).
  • Irrigation: vativaykkal – crisscross channels (vati N–S drainage; vaykkal E–W supply). Canals: Uttamacholavaykkal, Panca-vanamadevi-vaykkal, Ganavathy-vaykkal; ur-vaykkal, nattu-vaykkal; turn system. Tanks: Cholavaridhi, Kaliyaneri, Vairamegatataka (Pallava), Bahur big tank, Rajendra Cholaperiyaeri.
  • Rajendra's embankment at Gangaikonda Cholapuram, 16 miles of masonry = "jalamaya jayasthambham" (pillar of victory in water); Alberuni wonder-struck (quoted in Nehru's Glimpses of World History).
  • Water management: share = nirkkintravaaru; sluice = kumizh; head-channel = talaivay; common tank = enkalkulam; free labour = vetti, amanji; tank-repair tax = eriayam; water releasers – talaivayar, talaivay-chanrar, eriaraiyarkal; tank group = kulattar.

11.1.4 Society and religion

  • Top: brahmadeya-kilavars (peasants displaced – kudi neekki); temple land = devadana (tax-free); then vellanvagai landholders; tenants ulukudi (got kizh varam; owners melvaram); labourers paniceymakkal, slaves adimaigal.
  • Cholas ardent Saivites; Rajaraja I = Siva Pada Sekaran; fresco of Rajaraja with wives in Big Temple. Kulothunga II – Ramanujar left for Melkote (Karnataka).
  • Siva as Lingodhbhava and Nataraja (Adal Vallan); Tripurantaka form (warrior). Thirumurai recovered by Nambi Andar Nambi; singers Oduvars, Padikam Paduvars, vinnappamseivar.
  • Saiva Siddhanta: foundational text Sivagnana Bodham by Meikandar; 14 texts = Saiva Siddhanta Sastram.
  • Temples: royal temples Thanjavur, Gangaikonda Cholapuram, Darasuram; sepulchral temples = pallip-padai. Officials: koyirramar, koyilkanakku (accountant/auditor), deva-kanmi, srivaisnavar, cantesar (manager). Play Rajarajanatakam staged in Thanjavur temple. Festivals: Chithirai Tiruvizha, Kartigai, Aippasi vizha. Dances kudak-kuthu, sakkaik-kuthu. Oil pressers Sankarapadiyar. Temples acted as banks and schools.
  • Brihadishvarar (Rajarajisvaram): vimana 190 ft, capstone 80 tons; Lakshmi, Vishnu, Ardhanarisvara, Bikshadana; frescoes.
  • Gangaikonda Cholapuram: on Thanjavur model; tank Chola-gangam (Jalastambha); coronation centre; Ardhanariswarar, Durga, Chandesa-anugrahamurti.
  • Darasuram temple by Rajaraja II (1146–1172) – Periyapuranam miniatures.
  • Trade guilds: anjuvannattar (West Asians – Jews, Christians, Muslims; west-coast ports) and manigramattar (hinterland – Kodumbalur, Uraiyur, Kovilpatti, Piranmalai) → merged into ainutruvar / disai-ayirattu-ainutruvar / valanciyar, head guild at Ayyavole. Ports: Munai-santai, Mylapore, Tiruvotriyur, Nagapattinam, Vishakapattinam, Krishnapattinam. Exports: sandalwood, ebony, condiments, gems, pepper, oil, paddy, salt; imports: camphor, copper, tin, mercury. Valanciyar dug tank ainutruvapperari (Pudukottai).

11.1.5 Patrons of learning; end of Chola rule

  • Vedic colleges: Ennayiram (S. Arcot) by Rajendra I – 340 students, 14 teachers; Tirubuvani (near Puducherry, 1048); Thirumukudal (Chengalpattu, 1067).
  • Decline: dependent on Hoysalas; Jatavarman Sundara Pandyan I sacked Gangaikonda Cholapuram (1264); Kanchi lost to Telugu Cholas; 1279 – Maravarman Kulasekara Pandyan I defeated last Chola Rajendra III.
  • Sambuvarayars: chiefs of N. Arcot–Chengalpattu; kingdom Raja Ghambira Rajyam, capital Padaividu; titles Sakalaloka Chakravartin; Rajanarayan Sambuvarayan overthrown by Kumarakampana of Vijayanagar, who then defeated the Sultan of Madurai.

II. PANDYAS

  • Early capital/port Korkai (pearls), then Madurai – "Matirai" in inscriptions, "Kudal" in classics (Puliman Kombai inscription, Pattinappalai, Maduraikkanchi).
  • Sources: Velvikkudi grant of Nedunjadayan (most important); travellers Marco Polo, Wassaff, Ibn Battuta; Madurai Tala Varalaru, Pandik Kovai, Madurai Tiruppanimalai. Term "Sangam" (academy) first in Iraiyanar Akapporul (7th–8th c.); Seethalai Saththanar (Manimekalai) from Madurai.
  • Territory: Pandymandalam / Thenmandalam; north border river Vellar (Pudukkottai).
  • Revival (600–920): Kadunkon recovered land from Kalabhras; Sendan (title Vanavan); Arikesari Maravarman (624–674; Vaigai bed inscription says accession 642) = Kun Pandian, converted from Jainism by Thirugnanasambandar; Kochadayan Ranadhira (700–730); Maravarman Rajasimha I (730–765); Jatila Parantaka Nedunjadayan (Varaguna I) (765–815) – donor of Velvikkudi plates, greatest, expanded to Thanjavur, Tiruchi, Salem, Coimbatore; Srimara Srivallabha (815–862) invaded Ceylon, defeated by Nandivarman III; Varaguna II defeated by Aparajita at Sripurambiyam; Rajasimha II fled after defeat by Parantaka I (920).
  • Rise again (1190–1310): Sadayavarman Srivallabhan anointed at Madurai 1190 (Sundarachola Chaturvedimangalam). Kayal = great port; Marco Polo visited Kayal 1288 & 1293; praised king, noted sati and polygamy.
  • Jatavarman Sundarapandyan (1251–1268): zenith; whole TN up to Nellore; defeated Vira Someshwara at Kannanur (book calls him "Boja king of Malwa"; he was the Hoysala king – extra); Kadava chief of Cuddalore; co-regents Vikrama Pandyan, Vira Pandyan (took Eelam, Kongu, Cholamandalam); anointed in Srirangam, gold-plated its walls.
  • Maravarman Kulasekharan (1268–1312): son Sundara Pandyan III made co-regent (1302) → Vira Pandyan killed father; Sundara fled to Alauddin Khalji → Malik Kafur at Madurai 1311 (Amir Khusrau: 512 elephants, 5,000 horses, 500 maunds of jewels). Madurai Sultanate independent 1335 under Jalaluddin Ahsan Shah.
  • State: titles Kudalkon, Kudal Nagar Kavalan, Madurapura Paramesvaran, Mannar Mannan…; Sanskrit titles Kodanda Raman, Kaliyuga Raman; Tamil – Sembian, Vanavan, Thennavan. Palace = Tirumaligai; royal couches named Munaiya Daraiyan, Pandiya Daraiyan, Kalinkat Traiyan; scribe = Tirumantira Olai; PM = uttaramantri (Manickavasagar, Kulaciraiyar, Marankari were ministers); secretariat = Eluttu Mandapam; attendants Akapparivara Mudalikal.
  • Divisions: Pandy Nadu → valanadus → nadus & kurrams; unique division Kulakkil (area under a tank, e.g. "Madakkulakkil Madurai"). Land rods 14 and 24 ft. Salabogam (Brahmins), tattarkani (ironsmiths), thatchar-maaniyam (carpenters), Bhattavritti (Brahmin education). Manur inscription (Tirunelveli, 800) – village administration.
  • Economy: Brahmin villages = Mangalam/Chaturvedimangalam (titles Brahmmadhi Rajan, Brahmmaraiyan). Arab agency at Kayal of Malik-ul-Islam Jamaluddin for horses; Wassaff: 10,000 horses/yr, 220 dinars each. Traders: nikamattor, nanadesi, ainutruvar, manikiramattar, patinen-vishyattar; guilds at Kodumpalur, Periyakulam; horse traders = kudirai-chetti; busiest port Kayalpattinam; gold coins kasu, palankasu, kanam, kalancu, pon; fairs = Tavalam.
  • Irrigation: tanks named after royals (Vasudeva Peraru, Srivallaba Peraru…); Tirumaleri, Maraneri, Kaliyaneri; Sendan Maran sluice in Vaigai; stone revetment of tank banks; sluice = kumili, madai, madagu; landowners = Pumiputtirar / nattumakkal; Iruppaikkuti-kilavan built tanks.
  • Literacy: kadigai, salai, vidyastanam; academy to translate Mahabharatam; works: Tiruppavai, Tiruvempavai, Tiruvasagam, Tirukkovai, Tirumantiram.
  • Religion & temples: Pandyas first Jains, then Saivites; Vedic rituals (Ashvamedha, Hiranyagarbha, Vajapeya). Sepulchral temple Sundarapandisvaram; monolithic ornamented pillars; Meenakshi temple expanded. Rock-cut caves: Pillayarpatti, Tirumayam, Kunrakkudi, Tiruchendur, Kalugumalai, Kanyakumari, Sittannavasal (also Anaimalai c. 800). Paintings: Sittannavasal, Arittapatti, Tirumalaipuram, Tirunedunkarai. Sittannavasal cave authored by Ilam Kautamar (9th c.), renovated by Sri Maran Srivallaban; frescoes of dancers, king, queen, lotus pool.

Unit 12 · Bahmani and Vijayanagar Kingdoms (PDF p. 179–193)

  • Early 14th c. Deccan: Yadavas (Devagiri), Hoysalas (Dvarasamudra), Kakatiyas (Warangal), Pandyas (Madurai) – crushed by Malik Kafur. Madurai Sultanate independent 1333 (book). Zafar Khan → Bahman Shah, capital Gulbarga; Bahmani dynasty 1347–1527.
  • Sources: Persian court histories; Manucharitram, Saluvabhyudayam; Telugu Rayavachakamu (Nayankara system). Travellers: Ibn Battuta (Moroccan, 1333–45), Abdur Razzak (Persia, 1443–45), Nikitin (Russian, 1470–74), Domingo Paes and Nuniz (Portuguese, 1520–37).
  • Vijayanagar gold coin = Varaha (Tamil Pon, Kannada Honnu); images of deities, bull, elephant, gandaberunda (double eagle).

12.1 Bahmani kingdom

  • Alaudin Hasan Bahman Shah (1347–58): 4 tarafs (Gulbarga, Daulatabad, Bidar, Berar); title "Second Alexander". Rivalry over Raichur doab (Krishna–Tungabhadra).
  • Mohammed I (1358–75): attack on Warangal 1363 → Golkonda fort and Turquoise throne; council of 8 ministers – Vakil-us-sultana, Wazir-i-kull, Amir-i-jumla (finance), Wazir-i-ashraf (foreign), Nazir, Peshwa, Kotwal (police), Sadr-i-jahan (justice/religion); great mosque at Gulbarga (1367).
  • Warangal subdued 1425; capital Gulbarga → Bidar (1429).
  • Mohammed Gawan (Persian; PM of Mohammad III, 1463–82): madrasa at Bidar (3000 manuscripts); used gunpowder vs Vijayanagar at Belgaum; split 4 provinces into 8; governors allowed only one fort; Deccani vs Pardesi nobles → forged letter → executed.
  • Golkonda fort: built by Kakatiya Raja Krishna Dev; handed as jagir to Sultan Quli Qutb (book: "Kali Kutub Khan") (1495–96) – renamed Muhammad Nagar; Qutb Shahi capital; grandeur from Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah; diamond market (Kohinoor); 11 km from Hyderabad, hill 120 m; acoustics; top = Bala Hissar; gate = Fateh Darwaza; Aurangzeb besieged 1687 for 8 months – fell by treachery of an Afghan gatekeeper.
  • Architecture: Jami Masjid Gulbarga, Gol Gumbaz (Bijapur), Chand Minar (Bidar); Indo-Saracenic.
  • Split into 5 sultanates: Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Berar, Golkonda, Bidar; united against Vijayanagar at Talikota (Rakshasi-Tangadi), 1565.

12.2 Vijayanagar Empire

  • Founded c. 1336 by Harihara and Bukka (sons of Sangama, ex-Hoysala servants), after Hoysala Ballala III was killed by the Madurai Sultan. Capital first Anegondi (N bank Tungabhadra), then Hosapattana (near Hampi, S bank) renamed Vijayanagara; Harihara crowned 1346. Emblem: Chalukya boar (varaha). Tradition of Vidyaranya (Madhava) – doubtful.
  • Dynasties: Sangama (1336–1485), Saluva (1485–1505), Tuluva (1505–1570), Aravidu (1570–1650).
  • Kumara Kampana (son of Bukka I) with general Maraya-Nayak conquered Tondaimandalam (Sambuvarayas) and killed the Madurai Sultan c. 1370 – told in Madura-vijayam by his wife Gangadevi. Pandya country annexed only c. 1500.
  • Vijayanagar–Bahmani wars: Raichur doab, tribute, horse trade (Goa); Krishna river ≈ boundary.
  • Devaraya II (1422–46): greatest Sangama; recruited Muslim cavalry; Abdur Razzak visited; tribute from Sri Lanka. After him Gajapati raids up to Tiruchi (1460–65).
  • Saluva Narasimha usurped c. 1485; general Narasa Nayak; Viranarasimha began Tuluva dynasty c. 1505.
  • Krishnadevaraya (1509–29): subdued Ummattur chief; fought Bahmanis and Gajapati (took Udayagiri; victory pillar at Simhachalam); Portuguese allowed fort at Bhatkal; gopuras to Srisailam, Tirupati, Kalahasti, Kanchi, Tiruvannamalai, Chidambaram; wrote Amuktamalyada (Telugu, story of Andal), Sanskrit Usha Parinayam, Jambavati Kalyanam; titles Andhra Bhoja, Abhinava Bhoja, Andhra Pitamaha; reorganised Nayankara system; founded Nagalapura (mother Nagamba).
  • Achyutadevaraya – supported by Chellappa (Saluva Nayak), who later rebelled. Sadasivaraya (1542–70) – real power Ramaraya (son-in-law of Krishnadevaraya; Aravidu); treaty with Portuguese stopping horses to Bijapur.
  • Battle of Talikota / Rakshasi-Tangadi (Jan 1565): Ramaraya captured and executed; city ransacked. Capital shifted to Penugonda; Tirumala founded Aravidu dynasty (1570). 1601 battle near Uttaramerur – loyalist Yachama Nayak vs Vellore Nayak (backed by Thanjavur, Madurai, Senji).

12.3 Administration and Nayak system

  • Mahapradhani (chief minister); Dalavay (commander), Vassal, Rayasam (secretary), Adaippam, Kariya-karta.
  • Provinces = rajyas under pradhani (Hoysala rajya, Araga, Barakur, Muluvay); by 1400 five in Tamil area – Chandragiri, Padaividu, Valudalampattu, Tiruchirappalli, Tiruvarur. Sub-units nadu, sima, sthala, kampana.
  • Nayak system: revenue assignment for military service = nayakkattanam (Tamil), nayaktanam (Kannada), nayankaramu (Telugu); similar to iqta; Kakatiya precedent; fixed under Krishnadevaraya & Achyuta. Nuniz: 200+ captains paid at Mahanavami festival. Nayaks – Kannada/Telugu warriors of many castes (Reddi, Balija, Yadava…); created pettai markets. After Talikota: Madurai, Thanjavur, Ikkeri Nayak kingdoms.
  • Little kingdoms: Ramnad founded by Madurai Nayak Muthu Krishnappa (early 17th c.); kaval chief title Udaiyan Sethupati (lord of the causeway). Pudukottai – Tondaimans.

12.4 Society and economy

  • Migration of Kannada & Telugu warriors into Tamil areas; slavery; revolt of producers in central TN (1430) against unjust taxes – tax reduced.
  • Commercial economy; artisans = pattadai / kasaya-vargam (cash-tax payers); textiles exported.

12.5 Literature

  • Sayana (minister of Harihara II) – Vedic commentaries; Madhavacharya; Gangadevi – Maduravijayam; poetesses Hannamma, Thirumalamma.
  • Tamil: Tirumalainatha (Chidambara Puranam, Chokkanatharula) and son Paranjyothiyar; Sevvaichchuduvar – Tamil Bhagavata Puranam.
  • Telugu: Srinatha – Haravilasam (Devaraya I); Pina Virabhadra – Telugu Shakuntalam. Ashtadiggajas (8 poets) of Krishnadevaraya: Allasani Peddanna, Nandi Thimmana, Tenali Rama, Dhurjathy, Bhattu Murthy, Pingali Surana (book: "Panaji Surana"), Puna Virabhadra, Mallana. Allasani Peddanna = "Andhra Kavita Pitamaha", Manucharita. Tenali Rama – Panduranga Mahatyam.
  • Kannada: Bhima Kavi – Basava Purana; Kumara Valmiki – Kannada Ramayana; Harihara II – "Karnataka Vidyavilasa".

12.6 Art and architecture

  • Tall Raya Gopurams (Tiruvannamalai, Chidambaram) and Kalyana mandapams (Vellore; Varadharaja & Ekambaranatha, Kanchi; Jambukesvara, Tiruchi); horse carvings on pillars; 100/1000-pillar mandapams; Amman shrine became rule.
  • Hampi – World Heritage; Vittalaswamy temple (saptaswara musical pillars, stone chariot), Virupaksha, Hazara Rama temple (Longhurst praise). Paintings: Virabhadra temple, Lepakshi; Dasavatara & Girijakalyanam in Virupaksha. Portrait statues of Krishnadevaraya and two queens.

Unit 13 · Cultural Syncretism: Bhakti Movement in India (PDF p. 194–202)

  • Mother-goddess worship from Harappa; an Indus image identified as Siva. Bhakti = devotional surrender to a supreme god; bhakti-marga in Bhagavad Gita. Adi Sankara gave Advaita (intellectual); Nayanmars and Azhwars won the people. South India = home of religious renaissance 7th–10th c.

13.1 Bhakti movement in the South

  • Bhakti arose among landholding castes; Buddhism & Jainism patronised by merchants.
  • Sources: Thevaram (Appar, Sambandar, Sundarar) = 7 of 12 Thirumurais; 8th = Manickavasakar; Periyapuranam (Sekkizhar, 63 Nayanmars); Nalayira Divya Prabandham.
  • Early conflicts: Mahendravarman I (Jain) persecuted others; Appar was a Jain named Dharmasena, turned Saivite by his sister; king later converted to Saivism.
  • Sambandar defeated Jains in debate (opponents impaled). Maravarman Arikesari (640–670) = Koon Pandyan reconverted to Saivism by Sambandar; legend of massacre of Jains at Samantham (Madurai dt). (Unit 11 gives his dates as 624–674.)
  • Saiva Siddhanta text Sivagnana Sithiyar has section parapakkam refuting Buddhist & Jain views.
  • By 11th c. Buddhism wiped out in Tamil country; Tamil Jain communities survive. Influence of heterodoxy: renunciation, vegetarianism, Tamil supremacy (vs Prakrit), devotion overcoming fate.

13.2 Spread to North India

  • Ramanujar – Vishistadvaita (qualified monism). North Indian outburst from 15th c.; monotheists rejected both Hindu and Muslim orthodoxy; Hindustani, Indo-Muslim music & architecture; Kabir, Guru Nanak, Ravidas.

13.3 Impact of Sufism

  • Sufi, Wali, Darvesh, Fakir = Muslim saints. God = Mashuq (beloved), Sufis = Ashiqs (lovers). Orders (silsilahs): Chistis, Suhrawardis, Qadiriyahs, Naqshbandis. Blunted Hindu–Muslim conflict.

13.4 Salient features of Bhakti

  • Monotheism; salvation by devotion; self-surrender; guru as guide; universal brotherhood; against idol worship, caste, ritualism, pilgrimages, fasts; hymns in people's language.

Unit 14 · The Mughal Empire (PDF p. 203–228)

  • Mughals descended from Chengiz Khan and Timur; 1526–1857; six "Great Mughals"; Akbar's contemporaries: Elizabeth I, Shakespeare, Henry IV of France, Abbas the Great of Persia.

14.1 Babur (1526–1530)

  • Ruler of Samarkand at 11; invaded Bhera, Sialkot, Lahore (1519–24); invited by Daulat Khan Lodi and Rana Sanga.
  • First Battle of Panipat (21 April 1526) – defeated Ibrahim Lodi; artillery (first use in India).
  • Khanwa (1527) – defeated Rana Sanga (with Mahmud Lodi, Hasan Khan Mewati). Chanderi (1528) – Medini Rai. Ghagra (1529) – Afghans (Mahmud Lodi, Nusrat Shah). Died 1530.
  • Memoir Tuzuk-i-Baburi (Baburnama) in Turkish – world classic.

14.2 Humayun (1530–1540 & 1555–1556)

  • Brothers Kamran (Kabul, Kandahar, Punjab), Askari, Hindal; enemy Bahadur Shah of Gujarat. Beat Afghans at Daurah (1532); besieged Chunar; built Dinpanah.
  • Battle of Chausa (1539) – lost to Sher Khan (swam across Ganga). Battle of Kanauj (1540) – routed.
  • Exile in Persia; regained Delhi 1555; died falling from library stairs – Lane Poole: "stumbled out of his life, as he has stumbled through it".

14.3 Sher Shah and Sur dynasty

  • Born Farid; "Sher Khan" for killing a tiger. Died at Kalinjar 1545 (gunshot); son Islam Shah (to 1553).
  • Reforms: centralised; village headmen responsible for theft; land surveyed, revenue by fertility; taxes only at entry and sale; standard gold, silver, copper coins (basis of British coinage); Grand Trunk road (Indus to Sonargaon), roads Agra–Gujarat ports, Agra–Jodhpur, Lahore–Multan; sarais; Purana Qila; mausoleum at Sasaram. Akbar & Todar Mal's system based on his.
  • Jagirdari – revenue & governance of an estate given to an official; zamindari – landowner collects revenue, pays fixed sum.

14.5 Akbar (1556–1605)

  • Born 1542 (Rajputana desert); crowned at 14; regent Bairam Khan (dismissed; murdered en route to Mecca; son Abdur Rahim Khan-e-Khanan).
  • Second Battle of Panipat (Nov 1556) – Hemu (general of Adil Shah) hit in eye by arrow; Mughals won.
  • Conquests: Malwa 1562 (Baz Bahadur); Gondwana 1564 (Rani Durgavati, Vir Narayan); Chittor (Udai Singh; generals Jaimal & Patta – statues at Agra fort gate); Ranthambhor, Kalinjar, Bikaner, Jodhpur, Jaisalmer; Gujarat 1573 (Muzaffar Shah); Bihar & Bengal 1576 (Daud Khan); Kashmir 1586; Sindh 1591; Khandesh 1591; Berar 1596 from Chand Bibi (Ahmednagar).
  • Rajput policy: abolished jizya and pilgrim tax; banned sati and enslaving war captives; married Harkha Bai (Jodha), daughter of Raja Bharmal of Amber; Salim married daughter of Bhagwan Das; Man Singh – trusted general; Todar Mal – Diwan; Birbal. Resistance: Rana Pratap – Haldighati (1576), died 1597; Marwar's Chandra Sen (d. 1581).
  • Capital Agra → Fatehpur Sikri (Buland Darwaza).
  • Mansabdari system: ranks Zat (soldiers, 10 to 10,000) and Sawar (horses); salary via jagir (transferable, not hereditary).
  • Religious policy: Sulh-i-Kul (peace to all); Ibadat Khana (1575), debates stopped 1582; discussed with Purushotam, Devi, Meherji Rana, Aquaviva, Monserrate, Hira Vijaya Suri; Tauhid-i-Ilahi (Din Ilahi) – Sufi order, not a new religion; critic Badauni; translation department (Ramayana, Mahabharata, Atharva Veda, Bible, Quran into Persian).
  • Malik Ambar: Ethiopian slave → regent of Ahmadnagar; united Deccan Muslims and Marathas; died 1626.

14.6 Jahangir (1605–1627)

  • Salim, son of Rajput wife; son Khusrau revolted (blessed by Guru Arjun Dev, who was executed). Mewar's Amar Singh accepted suzerainty (campaign of Khurram); Kangra taken; Kandahar lost to Shah Abbas (1622).
  • William Hawkins failed; Sir Thomas Roe (envoy of James I) got factory at Surat.
  • Nur Jahan (Mehrunnisa) real power; Mahabat Khan's revolt; Asaf Khan helped Khurram become Shah Jahan.

14.7 Shah Jahan (1627–1658)

  • Crushed Khanjahan Lodi; Deccan in 4 provinces (Ahmednagar-Daulatabad, Khandesh, Berar, Telangana) under Aurangzeb; Ahmadnagar annexed 1636; Golkonda vassal (Mir Jumla affair); Kandahar 1638; Portuguese expelled from Hugli.
  • Peacock Throne; Taj Mahal for Mumtaz Mahal (d. 1631; married 1612): chief architect Ustad Ahmad Lahawri; begun c. 1632, mausoleum done 1638–39, decoration till 1647; 20,000 workers.
  • Visitors: Bernier (French physician), Tavernier (French gem merchant), Mandelslo (German), Peter Mundy (English), Manucci (Italian).
  • Dara Shukoh – "Philosopher Prince"; Upanishads into Persian (Sirr-i-Akbar). Shah Jahan imprisoned; died 1666.
  • Deccan Sultanates: Golkonda (Qutb Shahs), Bijapur (Adil Shahs), Berar (Imad Shahs), Bidar (Barid Shahs), Ahmadnagar (Nizam Shahs).

14.8 Aurangzeb (1658–1707)

  • "Alamgir"; revenue raised from 1/3 to 1/2; revolts of Jats (1669), Satnamis, Sikhs – execution of Guru Tegh Bahadur (9th guru); jizya reimposed; Marwar issue (Rani Hadi, Jaswant Singh); Mewar's Raj Singh + Prince Akbar; peace 1681.
  • Deccan from 1682 to death; Bijapur (Sikandar Adil Shah) taken; Golkonda 1687 (Abul Hasan). Shivaji captured by Jai Singh, escaped; died 1680 aged 53; Sambhaji executed 1689. Died at Ahmadnagar 1707. Stopped abwab; Hindu officials increased.
  • Gol Gumbaz (Bijapur): tomb of Mohammad Adil Shah (1627–56); dome second largest after St Peter's; chamber 135 ft walls, 178 ft high.

14.9–14.10 Society and economy

  • Population ~15 crore (16th c.), 20 crore (18th c.). Village headman Muqaddam, Panch; grants Madad-i-Mash; >15% of Akbar's nobles Rajputs; Shaji (Shivaji's father) served Shah Jahan. Muslim brides received mehr.
  • Zabt system (Todar Mal) – cash rates per area by crop; schedules = dasturs. Workshops = karkhanas. Tobacco & maize (17th c.), pineapple (16th c.); Bengal silk. Banjaras – bulk carriers; hundi = letters of credit; traders – Bohras, Marwaris, Chettiars (Coromandel). Mughals not a naval power.

14.11–14.12 Religion, science, architecture, arts, literature

  • Tulsidas – Ramcharitmanas (Awadhi); Vallabhacharya & Vitthalnath; Eknath, Tukaram; Dasakuta movement (Karnataka, Vyasaraya); Kabir (weaver), Ravidas (hides), Sain (barber), Dadu (cotton carder).
  • Christianity: Roberto De Nobili, Francis Xavier; first Lutheran missionaries (Danish) at Tranquebar 1706; Ziegenbalg – Tamil New Testament 1714.
  • Science: Faizi translated Lilavati; Persian wheel (Babur's time); saltpetre cooling; Akbar's "ship's camel"; no cast iron – matchlock vs Europe's flintlock (Irfan Habib).
  • Architecture: Purana Qila; Sher Shah's tomb Sasaram; Humayun's tomb; Agra fort; Fatehpur Sikri, Buland Darwaza; Akbar's tomb Sikandra; Itimad-ud-daula – first fully white-marble Mughal building; Taj Mahal; Red Fort (Diwan-i-Aam, Diwan-i-Khas), Moti Masjid (Agra), Jama Masjid (Delhi), Shahjahanabad; Aurangzeb – Badshahi mosque (Lahore), Bibi-ka-maqbara (Aurangabad); Shalimar gardens; Gomati bridge (Jaunpur); West Yamuna Canal; pietra dura.
  • Painting: Abdus Samad, Mir Sayyid Ali (came with Humayun); Daswant, Basawan (Akbar); Mansur (Jahangir – animals); influenced Rembrandt.
  • Music: Tansen of Gwalior + 35 musicians under Akbar (Ain-i-Akbari).
  • Literature: Abul Fazl – Akbar Nama, Ain-i-Akbari; Padshah Nama (Abdul Hamid Lahori & Muhammad Waris); Alamgir Nama (Muhammad Kazim); Baburnama translated into Persian by Abdur Rahim; Mahabharata translated under Faizi; Dabistan; Sanskrit: Rajavalipataka (Prajna Bhatta), Tajika Neelakanthi (Nilakantha), Rasagangadhara (Jagannatha Panditha, Shah Jahan's poet); rise of Urdu; Mukteshwar (Marathi epics); Shankara Deva (Assamese); Guru Granth Sahib compiled by Guru Arjun Dev.
  • Tamil: Kumaraguruparar (visited Varanasi; Meenakshiammai Pillai Tamil, Neethineri Vilakkam); Thayumanavar (sanmarga); Roberto de Nobili and Constantine Joseph Beschi (Veeramamunivar).

Unit 15 · The Marathas (PDF p. 229–246)

  • Nayak rule in Thanjavur ended 1674 when Maratha general Venkoji (half-brother of Shivaji), leading Bijapur forces, took Thanjavur; Maratha rule there lasted till death of Serfoji II (1832).

15.1 Causes of rise

  • Terrain of Konkan – hill forts; guerrilla warfare; earlier served Bahmanis and Deccan Sultans.
  • Bhakti in Marathi – Tukaram, Ramdas, Eknath. Justice Ranade: Maratha religious revival was not Brahmanical, heterodox in spirit.
  • Decline of Bijapur & Golkonda; Mughal treasury exhausted. Capital Raigarh (Raigad).

15.2 Shivaji (1627–1680)

  • Born at Shivner near Junnar; father Shahji Bhonsle (served Malik Ambar, then Bijapur); mother Jijabai; guardian Dadaji Kondadev (Poona jagirs); Mavali peasants; guru Ramdas.
  • Captured Torna (1646) at 19; Raigad rebuilt; Baramati, Indapura, Purandhar, Kondana; quiet 1649–55 (father's release); Javli (1656); built Pratapgarh; Kalyan, Bhivandi, Mahuli.
  • Afzal Khan (1659) sent by Bijapur; Shivaji later recognised by Bijapur.
  • Shaista Khan (Deccan governor 1660) attacked at night in Poona (400 men as marriage party); recalled 1663. Surat sacked 1664 and again 1670; chauth on Surat 1672.
  • Raja Jai Singh besieged Purandar → Treaty of Purandar (11 June 1665): forts ceded, Shivaji to be mansabdar. Visit to Agra (May 1666) with Sambhaji – imprisoned, escaped in fruit basket. Aurangzeb called him "mountain rat".
  • Coronation 6 June 1674 at Raigarh – title Chhatrapati.
  • Carnatic campaign (1676): secret treaty with Golkonda; captured Senji and Vellore; Venkoji left in Thanjavur; Madurai Nayak promised tribute; Senji = second line of defence.
  • Died 1680 aged 53.

15.3 Marathas after Shivaji

  • Sambhaji captured and killed 1689; Rajaram fought from Senji; died 1700 → widow Tara Bai (raid on Hyderabad, Masulipatnam trade disrupted).
  • Shahu (Sambhaji's son) won civil war, king 1708; Balaji Viswanath made Peshwa 1713; Shahu at Satara, Peshwa at Poona; Tara Bai's rival court at Kolhapur; Sambhaji II of Kolhapur. Later Satara rulers: Rama Raja, Shahu II, Pratap Singh (deposed 1839), Shahji II (d. 1848).

15.5 Rule of the Peshwas (1713–1818)

  • Peshwa = "foremost" (Persian).
  • Balaji Viswanath (1713–20): won over naval chief Kanhoji Angre; revived jagirs; made Peshwaship hereditary; naval bases Konkan, Khanderi, Vijayadurg.
  • Baji Rao I (1720–40): defeated Nizam, Malwa, Gujarat governors; Bundelkhand (got 1/3); killed Trimbak Rao at Dabhai (1731); Treaty of Warna (1731); took Thana, Salsette, Bassein from Portuguese (1738).
  • Balaji Baji Rao (Nana Sahib) (1740–61): Carnatic – Raghoji Bhonsle killed Nawab Dost Ali (1740), imprisoned Chanda Sahib; Mohammed Ali retook Tiruchi (1743). Battle of Udgir (1760) – climax (Sadasiva Rao). Orissa from Bengal Nawab (1751); Delhi entered 1752.
  • Nadir Shah plundered Delhi (1739) – took Kohinoor and Peacock Throne; Ahmad Shah Abdali (Durrani) sacked Delhi 1757; Dattaji Scindia killed 1760.
  • Third Battle of Panipat – 14 January 1761: Abdali (allied with Najib-ud-Daulah of Rohilkhand, Shuja-ud-Daulah of Oudh) vs Marathas (nominal head Vishwas Rao, real Sadasiva Rao); Marathas routed; Peshwa died June 1761; Abdali recognised Shah Alam II.
  • Madhav Rao I (1761–72): beat Nizam (1763), Haider Ali (1765–67, 1772); brought Shah Alam II back to Delhi (1771). Narayan Rao murdered 1773; Sawai Madhav Rao Peshwa at 40 days; Baji Rao II last Peshwa.

15.7 Administration under Peshwas

  • Peshwa Secretariat at Poona; provinces under Sar-subahdars; Mamlatdar, Kamavistar in districts; Deshmukhs, Deshpandes (accounts); deposit Rasad; posts auctioned under Baji Rao II.

15.8 Maratha rule in Tamil Nadu

  • Krishnadevaraya's Nayankara: Tamil country in 3 – Senji, Thanjavur, Madurai; sub-chiefs = Palayakkarars, fiefs = Palayams.
  • Thanjavur Nayak rule eclipsed 1673; Venkoji crowned – Maratha rule from 1676 (book gives both 1674 and 1676). Shivaji (1677) placed Santaji; Venkoji regained.
  • Rulers: Venkoji → Shahji → Serfoji I (1712–28) → Tukkoji → Pratap Singh (1739–63) → Thuljaji (to 1787) → Serfoji II (crowned at 10; regent Amarsingh; English took administration) – last of Bhonsle dynasty of Thanjavur.
  • Raja Desinghu: Bundela Rajput Swarup Singh made kiladar of Senji (1700); son Tej Singh (Desinghu) (from 1714) refused tribute; killed at 22 by Nawab Sadat-ul-lah Khan; wife committed sati; Tamil ballads.
  • Serfoji II: taught by German missionary Friedrich Schwartz; printing press for Marathi & Sanskrit; enriched Saraswati Mahal Library (built by Nayaks; Modi documents, French-Maratha letters); first modern public school for non-Christian natives (1803); free schools for orphans & poor; navavidya; 1823 report (Munro's census): 21 of 44 free schools in Thanjavur run by Serfoji; Muktambal Chattiram school (1822: 15 teachers, 464 students); Dhanvantari Mahal (herbal medicine research, case-sheets, 18 volumes); pandit Kottaiyur Sivakolundu Desigar joined College of Fort St George.
  • C.S. John (Tranquebar) proposed free schools to govt in 1812; Munro's school scheme 1820s; first Company school for natives in Madras only 1841.
  • Serfoji wrote Kumarasambhava Champu, Devendra Kuravanji, Mudrarakshasachaya; introduced clarinet and violin to Carnatic music; Thanjavur painting; first zoo in Tamil Nadu; died 7 March 1832; Bishop Heber: "no one whose deportment was more princely".

Unit 16 · The Coming of the Europeans (PDF p. 247–267)

  • British rule conventionally from Plassey 1757. Vasco da Gama found sea route via Cape of Good Hope (end of 15th c.); Portuguese took Goa 1510; forts at Daman and Diu.

I. 16.1 Political affairs

  • 1600–1650 Mughals: Akbar took Gujarat 1573 → port Surat ("gateway to West Asia & Europe"; pilgrim ships to Mecca; two governors – city and castle on Tapti). Bengal a subah under Jahangir. Dutch, English at Surat – factories but no territorial rights. English got Bombay 1668, HQ there 1687.
  • 1600–1650 South India: Nayak kingdoms Madurai, Thanjavur, Senji virtually autonomous after Talikota; Setupati of Ramanathapuram; Golkonda annexed Pulicat–San Thome area (1646). Dutch got Pulicat (Pazhaverkadu) from Nayak of Senji; English got land from Damarla Venkatadri Nayak → Fort St George 1639 → Chennai.
  • 1650–1700: Shivaji raided Surat 1664 and 1670; defeated Nayaks of Senji and Thanjavur; Thanjavur became Maratha-ruled cultural capital.
  • 1700–1750: successor states – Oudh, Bengal, Hyderabad, Carnatic; Nawabs borrowed from English and assigned land revenue → British as revenue collectors. Carnatic = originally Kannada land; in 18th c. between Eastern and Western Ghats under Nawab of Arcot. Dutch moved HQ from Pulicat to Nagapattinam; capital Batavia (Jakarta).

16.2 The economy

  • Commercial crops: sugarcane, oilseeds, cotton, indigo; Tamil region imported grain from Andhra & Bengal.
  • Weaving = 2nd most important activity; dyeing with alum; Coromandel painted fabrics = kalamkari (staple for SE Asia).
  • Merchant pyramid; shroffs (money-changers/bankers); hundis (bills of exchange); merchant princes – banias & Parsis of Surat, nagarseths of Ahmedabad, Jagat Seths of Bengal. Madras region famine ≥10 times 1678–1750; Dutch shipped slaves to Batavia.
  • Indian Ocean trade in stages; entrepots – Malacca, Calicut, Cambay, Surat; Calicut lost to Gujarat ports; Coromandel ports Masulipatnam, Pulicat.

16.3–16.4 Advent of Europeans; collaboration with Indian merchants

  • No demand in Asia for European goods except gold/silver → Indian textiles used to buy spices; hence Dutch & English on Coromandel coast.
  • Indian merchants not at a disadvantage till c. 1700; later English pressure; merchant princes disappeared; rising European demand strained weavers.

II. 16.5 Portuguese

  • Vasco da Gama: 170 men, 3 ships; welcomed by Zamorin of Calicut; returned 29 Aug 1498 with 55 men. Pedro Alvarez Cabral – 1200 men, 13 ships. Gama's 2nd visit 29 Oct 1502 (20 ships); factory at Cochin, prison at Kannur.
  • Francisco d'Almeida – first Viceroy; "Blue Water Policy" (navy, not settlements); destroyed Zamorin's and Egyptian fleets.
  • Albuquerque (1509–15) – real founder; took Goa 1510 from Yusuf Adil Khan of Bijapur; Malacca; Aden; Ormuz 1515; encouraged mixed marriages; tried to stop sati.
  • Nino da Cunha – Bassein 1534, Diu 1537; Daman 1559; Antonio de Noronha – Akbar visited Cambay (1571). Spain annexed Portugal 1580; Dutch took Ceylon.
  • Impact: first European territorial conquest; artillery; Eurasians; San Thome; Mylapore = Portuguese "Black Town" (British Black Town = George Town). Roberto de Nobili – "father of Tamil prose"; Henriques – introduced Tamil printing, "father of printing press".
  • Paravas of pearl fishery coast sought Portuguese protection (1530s) → mass conversion; St Francis Xavier (Goa 1542) to Thoothukudi, Punnakayal. Cartaz = Portuguese protection pass (racket).

The Dutch

  • First expedition 1595 (Jan Huyghen van Linschoten); VOC (United East Indies Co.) 1602; took Malacca 1641, Ceylon 1658.
  • Amboyna Massacre (1623) – Dutch killed 20 English East India Co. servants etc. at Ambon, Indonesia.
  • Tamil Nadu: Pulicat (60 km N of Chennai) – Castle Geldria; Masulipatnam 1605, Pulicat 1610; also Nagapattinam, Punnakayal, Porto Novo, Cuddalore, Devanampatinam. Pulicat = Coromandel HQ; diamonds exported; gunpowder factory; slave trade (Wil O Dijk: 26,885 slaves 1621–65; 2118 in 1646 from Adiramapattinam, Tondi, Kayalpattinam).

The French

  • French East India Co. 1664 – project of Louis XIV, minister Colbert. Madagascar 1602 (abandoned 1674). Berber got firman from Aurangzeb (1666); first factory Surat 1668, Masulipatnam 1669. De la Haye took San Thome from Dutch (1672). Sher Khan Lodi (Bijapur governor) offered Pondicherry (1673).
  • Francis Martin made Pondicherry the strategic centre; Dutch held it 1693–99; restored by Treaty of Ryswick (1697); Martin governor till 1706. Mahe 1725, Karaikal 1739; Governor Pierre Benoit Dumas.

The Danes

  • Danish East India Co. – charter 17 March 1616 by Christian IV; Admiral Ove Gjedde (Ceylon, 1618); Robert Crappe negotiated with Thanjavur Nayak → Tarangambadi (Tranquebar) by agreement of 20 Nov 1620; also Serampore, Nicobar. Second company 1696. Serampore sold to British 1839, Tranquebar 1845.
  • Ziegenbalg and Heinrich Pluetschau – first Lutheran (Protestant) missionaries, arrived Tranquebar 1706 (book also gives 9 June 1706); jailed 4 months (1707–08); printing press; Tamil New Testament 1715 – first in any Indian language (Unit 14 says 1714); church of 1718; seminary; died 23 Feb 1719.

The English

  • Charter from Elizabeth I (merchants of Leadenhall Street); court of 24 Directors. 1611 – William Hawkins to Jahangir; Prince Khurram (Gujarat viceroy) gave privileges.
  • Madras ceded 1639 by Raja of Chandragiri – Fort St George = first landholding. Presidency 1652, nullified 1655, again 1684; municipal government with Mayor 1688. Bombay (dowry of Charles II) to Company 1668; Charter of 1683 – right to raise forces, declare war.
  • Bengal: privileges from Shah Shuja; rights 1680; Sutanuti settlement 1690; zamindari of Sutanuti, Kalikata, Gobindpur (1698) for Rs 1200/yr; Fort William. Norris mission (1698) to Aurangzeb; Surman mission (1714–17) – firman from Farrukhsiyar.

Unit 17 · Effects of British Rule (PDF p. 268–282)

  • After Bengal famine of 1770 the Company began to govern; by 1830s emigration of ruined peasants and weavers to plantations.

17.1 Establishment of British Raj

  • Buxar = real foundation battle. Clive (Governor of Fort William) – two Treaties of Allahabad (1765): Diwani of Bengal, Bihar, Orissa to Company; Shah Alam II got Allahabad & Kora + Rs 26 lakh a year; Oudh restored to Shuja-ud-daula.
  • Dual System / Dyarchy: Company = Diwan (revenue, civil justice) + Nizamat (military, criminal justice) but Nawab responsible → 1770 famine (1/3 of Bengal died) → Regulating Act 1773; Warren Hastings = Governor-General of Bengal.
  • Charter Act 1833 → Governor-General of India – William Bentinck first. After 1858 "Viceroy and Governor-General" – Canning first.

17.2 Land tenures

  • Pitt's India Act 1784 separated civil and military establishments.
  • Permanent Settlement (1793) – Cornwallis; zamindars of Bengal, Bihar, Orissa got hereditary rights; revenue fixed permanently; cultivators became tenants.
  • Madras: Permanent Settlement tried – Chengalpattu, Salem, Dindigul divided into mittahs and auctioned (failed); then village lease system (3 years; mirasdar or headman responsible) – failed.
  • Mahalwari – 1833 under Bentinck (settlement with estate proprietor, collection from cultivators).
  • Ryotwari – Thomas Munro; decided 1814; enforced 1822; ryot (Arabic ra'iyah) = proprietor & taxpayer; revision every 30 years; pattas – could sell, lease, mortgage → private property in land.
  • Thomas Munro: came to Madras 1780; Mysore wars; Baramahal (Salem) 1792–99; Kanara; collector of Ceded Districts (Kadapa, Kurnool, Chittoor, Anantapur); Governor of Madras 1820 (7 yrs); Indianisation of services; died of cholera at Pattikonda (Kurnool) July 1827; statue in Madras 1839.

17.3 Subsidiary Alliance and Doctrine of Lapse

  • Wellesley (1798–1805): Subsidiary Alliance – Hyderabad, Mysore, Lucknow, Peshwa, Bhonsle, Sindhia. Terms: disband own army, accept British force & Resident; pay for it (or cede territory); no other Europeans (esp. French); no negotiation without Company. Disbanded soldiers swelled the Pindaris.
  • Presidency (seat of chief administrative head – Madras, Bombay, Calcutta) vs Province (Central, United Provinces).
  • Doctrine of Lapse – Dalhousie: adopted sons not recognised → Satara first (1848), Jhansi (1853; Gangadhar Rao; Rani Lakshmi Bai), Nagpur (1853; Raghuji Bhonsle III); pension stopped to Nana Sahib; withdrawn 1858.

17.4 Native states and British paramountcy

  • Tribute to Shah Alam II stopped; Hastings (Moira, 1813) removed imperial phrase from seal; Mysore – rebellion 1830, Bentinck appointed Mark Cubbon; Gwalior – battle of Maharajpur (1843), Ellenborough.

17.5 Civil and judicial reforms

  • Cornwallis: William Jones; collectors limited to revenue; Sadar Diwani Adalat (civil) and Sadar Nizamat Adalat (criminal) at Calcutta; 4 provincial courts (Calcutta, Deccan, Murshidabad, Patna); district courts; lowest – Indian munsifs; civil service reform (no private trade, merit) but Indians excluded; thanas under daroga.
  • Wellesley: College of Fort William (1800), Calcutta; East India College, England (1806). College of Fort St George (1812) by F.W. Ellis – theory that South Indian languages form a family separate from Sanskrit was formulated here.

17.6 Education and development

  • Warren Hastings – Calcutta Madrasa (40 students); Cornwallis – Sanskrit College, Benares (1791); Charter 1813 – education clause; Hindu College, Calcutta (1817); press censorship (1799) abolished; Bengali weekly Samachar Darpan (1818); missionary Alexander Duff.
  • Bentinck: suppressed thuggee, abolished sati, English as medium; Calcutta Medical College (1835); Grant Medical College, Bombay (1845); Thomason Engineering College, Roorkee (1847); girls' school Calcutta (1849).
  • Macaulay (law member 1835; President, Board of Education) – Minute of 1835: "a single shelf of a good European library…"; English made official language.
  • Wood's Despatch (1854) – primary to university; Universities of Madras, Bombay, Calcutta (1857); grants-in-aid; Dalhousie encouraged vernacular schools (James Thomason).
  • Charters renewed every 20 years; Charter of 1853 the last.

17.7 Safety and developmental measures

  • Pindari War (1811–1818) → Central India under British.
  • Thuggee – William Sleeman; 3000+ convicted 1831–37; gone by 1860.
  • Sati Abolition Act 1829 (Bentinck); Raja Rammohan Roy's campaign decisive.
  • Railways: Bombay–Thane 1853; Howrah–Raniganj 1854–55; first in South India: Royapuram–Arcot (Wallajah Road) 1856. Telegraph 1854; Suez Canal 1869 (4000 miles shorter).
  • Irrigation neglected; Arthur Cotton – dam across Kollidam (Coleroon) 1836, Krishna dam begun 1853; Jumna canal 1830, Ganges canal, Bari Doab canal (1856).
  • Forests: Santhals evicted (Jungle Mahals) – first tribal resistance; railway sleepers (sal, deodar, teak); Indian Forest Act 1865; Criminal Tribes Act 1871.

17.8 Deindustrialisation and drain of wealth

  • India became market for Lancashire cloth; Charles Trevelyan (1840): Dacca ("Manchester of India") fell from 150,000 to 30–40,000; Abbe Dubois on starving weavers of Madras; Bentinck: "bones of cotton weavers are bleaching the plains of India".
  • 80% of budget on military & civil administration; Arthur Cotton's irrigation plea rejected. Torture Commission report (Madras, 1855) on revenue atrocities.

17.9 Famines and indentured labour

  • 1800–25: 4 famines; last quarter of 19th c.: 22 famines; R.C. Dutt – 10 mass famines since 1860s, 15 million dead. Orissa famine (1/3 died) → Dadabhai Naoroji's poverty studies. Guntur famine 1833. Madras famine 1876–78 – Lytton's hands-off policy; 3.5 million died.
  • Slavery abolished 1843 → indentured labour (5-year contracts; agents = kanganis). 150 labourers from Thanjavur to Ceylon coffee plantations (1828); 1815 Ceylon request; famines of 1833, 1843; 1843–1868: 1,444,407 went from Madras to Ceylon. Penal contract; Bharathi's song "Karumbu thottathile".
  • Drain of Wealth – Dadabhai Naoroji, Poverty and Un-British Rule in India: Home Charges (dividends, salaries & savings remitted, pensions, India Office costs, war expenses & interest). India's debt £130 m (1837) → £220 m; railway debt £177.5 m (1908 report); 5% guaranteed interest. R.C. Dutt: 1891–1901, £159 m of £647 m drained (book says 44%).

Unit 18 · Early Resistance to British Rule (PDF p. 283–301)

  • Revolts of deposed kings, zamindars, palayakkarars = "primary resistance"; also peasant and tribal uprisings.

18.1 Mysore Sultans

  • Wodeyars independent after 1565; Raja Wodeyar (1578); capital to Srirangapatnam (1610).
  • Haider Ali: father Fateh Muhammad, Faujdar of Kolar; by 1755 commanded 100 horse, 2000 foot; title "Fateh Haider Bahadur"; Dalawai and de facto ruler; king Nanjaraja poisoned 1770. Warren Hastings' "Ring Fence" policy.
  • Northern Sarkars (Ganjam, Vizagapatnam, Godavari, Krishna, Guntur) ceded by Salabat Jung after Col. Forde took Masulipatnam (1759); English promise to help Nizam = genesis of Subsidiary System.

18.2 Southern Palayakkarars

  • Nagama Nayak and son Viswanatha Nayak (Madurai); PM Ariyanatha Mudaliyar → 72 palayams; Madurai fort with 72 bastions; system from 1530s; earlier in Kakatiya kingdom. Palayakkarar = holder of a camp / estate on military tenure; earlier Servaikarars and Talayaris. Duties: tribute, troops, law & order; judicial powers. Western palayams – Maravar chiefs; eastern palayams – Telugu Nayak chiefs.
  • Yusuf Khan (Khan Sahib): born Maruthanayakam (Ramanathapuram dt); converted to Islam at Pondicherry; joined Clive 1752; governor of Madurai & Tirunelveli 1756–61; encouraged Madurai weaving; hanged for treachery 1764.
  • Nawab's brother Mahfuz Khan and Col. Heron (1755) – failed against Panchalamkurichi and Nerkattumseval.
  • Puli Thevar (Nerkattumseval): backed by Pathan officers Mianah, Mudimiah, Nabikhan Kattak; confederacy of western palayams (Uthumalai, Surandai, Thalaivankottai, Naduvakurichi, Singampatti, Urkad, Seithur, Kollamkondan, Wadakarai) + Travancore (promise of Kalakkadu); won Battle of Kalakadu over Mahfuz Khan; rebellion 1756–63; Yusuf Khan took Nerkattumseval, Vasudevanallur, Panayur on 16 May 1761; Capt. Campbell took Nerkattumseval 1767; end unknown.
  • Velu Nachiyar: daughter of Chellamuthu Sethupathy (Ramanathapuram); wife of Muthu Vadugar Periyaudayar of Sivagangai; daughter Vellachi Nachiar; lived 8 years under Haider at Virupachi near Dindigul; with Gopala Nayaker and Haider fought British in 1780 and won; Kuyili set herself alight to destroy arsenal; adopted daughter Udaiyaal also blew up an arsenal; women's army; re-crowned with Marudu brothers – Chinna Marudu adviser, Periya Marudu commander; died 1796.
  • Veera Pandiya Kattabomman (Panchalamkurichi; born 1760; grandfather Jagaveera Kattabomman): tribute arrears (1798); Collector Jackson summoned him to Ramanathapuram; followed Jackson 23 days over 400 miles; met him 19 Sept 1798; arrears only 1080 pagodas; scuffle – Lt. Clarke killed, minister Sivasubramania Pillai captured. Governor Edward Clive's committee acquitted him; Jackson dismissed; S.R. Lushington new collector.
  • Confederacy of Marudu Pandiyan (Sivagangai), Gopala Nayak (Dindigul), Yadul Nayak (Anamalai); Kattabomman met Marudu at Palayanur; march on Sivagiri under Dalawai Kumaraswami Nayak. Major Bannerman ultimatum 1 Sept 1799; siege of Panchalamkurichi (mud fort 500×300 ft) from 5 Sept; Col. Welsh praised defenders; Sivasubramania Pillai taken at Kolarpatti; Vijaya Ragunatha Tondaiman of Pudukottai captured Kattabomman (jungles of Kalapore); tried before palayakkarars and hanged at Kayathar on 16 Oct 1799.
  • Pagoda = gold coin of Vijayanagar origin (varagan); 1 pagoda ≈ 3½ rupees (Tipu's time); "shaking the pagoda tree".

18.3 South Indian Rebellion, 1801 (Marudu brothers)

  • Treaty of 1772 – Company to collect Stalam Kaval, Desa Kaval → kavalkarars aggrieved.
  • Vella (Periya) Marudu and Chinna Marudu proclaimed Vellachi queen of Sivagangai; rallying point Kalayarkoil; capital Siruvayal; sheltered Umathurai (Kattabomman's brother). Rebels made Muthu Karuppa Thevar ruler of Ramnad (Nawab backed Muthuramalinga Thevar).
  • 1801: Shevatha Thambi (son of Chinna Marudu) marched to Thanjavur; defeated by Capt. William Blackburne near Mangudi; Serfoji stood with British. Company recognised Padmattur Woya Thevar as Sivagangai ruler (divide); P.A. Agnew led operations (Manamadurai, Partibanur, Paramakudi).
  • Marudus captured in Singampunary hills; executed at Tiruppathur fort, 24 Oct 1801; Umathurai & Shevathiah beheaded at Panchalamkurichi 16 Nov 1801; 73 rebels banished to Penang (April 1802).
  • Theeran Chinnamalai (Kongu – Salem, Coimbatore, Karur, Dindigul): trained by French & Tipu; allies Gopal Nayak (Virupatchi), Appachi Gounder (Paramathi Velur), Joni Jon Kahan (Attur), Kumaral Vellai (Perundurai), Varanavasi (Erode); attack on Coimbatore fort (1800) failed (49 executed); battles – Cauvery banks 1801, Odanilai 1802, Arachalur 1804, final 1805; betrayed by his cook; hanged at Sankagiri fort, 31 July 1805.

Vellore Revolt (10 July 1806)

  • Vellore = meeting ground of rebels; ~3000 Mysore loyalists nearby; Tipu's sons present.
  • Immediate cause: ban on caste marks, earrings; moustache pattern; new turban by Adjutant General Agnew with leather cockade; May 1806 – 2nd battalion of 4th regiment refused; 21 court-martialled; two given 900 lashes. Governor William Bentinck.
  • 2 a.m. 10 July: sepoys attacked barracks; 13 officers killed, 82 privates dead; prince Jamal-ud-din suspected; Jamedar Sheik Kasim principal accused; Major Armstrong killed. Col. Gillespie rode from Arcot (letter via Capt. Stevenson), blew open gate (Lt. Blakiston) – fort retaken in ~15 min; 800+ bodies (Blakiston); 378 jailed (W.J. Wilson). Regulations withdrawn; princes to Calcutta; Governor, C-in-C, Deputy Adjutant General recalled. Echoes at Hyderabad, Wallajahbad, Bangalore, Nandydurg, Palayamkottai, Bellary, Sankaridurg. Seen as forerunner of 1857.

18.4 Peasant and tribal revolts

  • Mappillas (Malabar; British took Malabar 1792; janmi made absolute owner over kanamdar & cultivator): risings at Manjeri (1849), Kulathur (1851), Mattannur (1852); again 1870.
  • Kol uprising (1831–32): Chotanagpur, Singbhum; against thikadars; leader Buddha Bhagat killed; Bhindrai Manki surrendered 19 March 1832.
  • Santhal Hool (1855–56): Damin-i-koh (Rajmahal hills); against zamindars, mahajans, government ("dikus"); leaders Sido, then Kanoo; battle of Maheshpur; 15–20 thousand killed.
  • Munda Ulgulan (1899–1900): Birsa Munda (b. 1874); Sail Rakab massacre; died in Ranchi jail, 9 June 1900.

18.5 Great Rebellion, 1857

  • Views: Col. Malleson – mutiny became national insurrection; Keene; Edward Thompson – "real war of independence"; V.D. Savarkar – The War of Indian Independence (1909).
  • Causes: annexations (Oudh, Jhansi), Nana Sahib; Inam Commission (1852) – 21,000 estates confiscated; talukdars of Oudh; heavy revenue; Persian abolished in courts; Act of 1856 (overseas service), sati abolition, widow remarriage; Lex Loci Act (1850) – converts keep inheritance; greased cartridges of Enfield rifle (trigger).
  • Course: Mangal Pandey at Barrackpore; Meerut – only 5 of 90 took cartridges; 10 May 1857 Meerut revolt → Delhi; Bahadur Shah II proclaimed emperor. Rohilkhand – Khan Bahadur Khan; Jhansi – Lakshmi Bai (22); Kanpur – Nana Sahib (massacre; Havelock, Neill's vengeance); Tantia Topi; Lucknow residency (Henry Lawrence) fell; Begum Hazrat Mahal (wife of Wajid Ali Shah) made son Birjis Qadra Wali; Lucknow retaken March 1858. Delhi taken by John Nicholson; Bahadur Shah II exiled to Rangoon, died Nov 1862 aged 87. Hugh Rose – Lakshmi Bai died fighting near Gwalior ("best and bravest"); Tantia Tope executed April 1859; Canning declared peace July 1858.

Unit 19 · Towards Modernity (PDF p. 302–313)

  • Indian Renaissance – rationalism, progress, natural rights; printing spread ideas; Bengal first affected. Utilitarians = "greatest happiness of the greatest number".

19.1 Emergence of reform movements

  • Unifying ideas: rationalism, religious universalism, humanism. Two types: Reformist and Revivalist. Keshab Chandra Sen: "all established religions of the World are true". Social Conference, Servants of India, missionaries gave impetus.
  • Brahmo Samaj (August 1828) – Raja Rammohan Roy ("Father of Indian Renaissance"); monotheism from the Vedas; against idolatry, sati; tract (1818) "A Conference between an Advocate for and an Opponent of the Practice of Burning Widows" → sati made a crime 1829. Brahmo aims: against polytheism, idol worship, avatars, caste, child marriage, purdah, sati; for widow remarriage. Roy died at Bristol.
  • Revived by Devendranath Tagore; led by Keshab Chandra Sen from 1857; 54 branches by 1865 (one in Madras). Split (book's wording: Devendranath's "Brahmo Samaj of India" and Keshab's "Sadharan Brahmo Samaj"; standard account – Devendranath's Adi Brahmo Samaj vs Keshab's Brahmo Samaj of India (1866), and the Sadharan Brahmo Samaj split from Keshab in 1878 – extra). Opposed by Hindu Dharma Sabha; Ishwarchandra Vidyasagar used scriptures for same ideas.
  • In Tamil Nadu: Kasi Viswanatha Mudaliar – play Brahmo Samaja Natakam and tract for widow remarriage; Tamil journal Tathuva Bodhini (1864).
  • Prarthana Samaj (1867), Bombay – Atmaram Pandurang; members M.G. Ranade, R.G. Bhandarkar, K.T. Telang; linked to Maharashtra bhakti; inter-dining, intermarriage, widow remarriage. Ranade – National Social Conference (met after each Congress session), Widow Marriage Association, Deccan Education Society; succeeded by Chandavarkar (1901).
  • Arya Samaj (1875) – Dayananda Saraswati (1824–83, Gujarati); Satyarth Prakash; "Back to the Vedas"; rejected puranas, idolatry, priests; Shuddhi movement; influence in Punjab; DAV schools; revivalist.
  • Ramakrishna Mission (1897): Ramakrishna Paramahamsa (1836–86), priest at Dakshineswar; "all religions different ways to same goal"; Ramakrishna Kathamrita. Swami Vivekananda (Narendranath Dutta, 1863–1902) founded the Mission; Parliament of Religions, Chicago, 1893; Valentine Chirol's praise.
  • Theosophical Society (1875) – Madame H.P. Blavatsky and Col. H.S. Olcott in USA; came to India 1879; HQ Adyar 1882; Annie Besant came 1893; revived Buddhism. Iyotheethoss Pandithar met Buddhist monks Anagarika Dharmapala and Acharya Sumangala in Sri Lanka through Olcott.

19.2 Lower-caste and women's movements

  • Satya Shodhak Samaj (1873) – Jyotiba Phule (b. 1827, Mali community); book Gulamgiri (Slavery); girls' school in Poona 1851 with wife Savitribai; schools for untouchables; home for widows' children; beginning of non-Brahmin movement of Maharashtra.
  • Pandita Ramabai (1858–1922): titles "Pandita" and "Saraswati"; inter-caste marriage (1880); Arya Mahila Samaj (Poona; 300 women in 1882); Sharada Sadan (widows); Mukti Sadan at Khedgaon.
  • Sri Narayana Guru (1854–1928), Kerala – Ezhavas (toddy tappers, 26% of population); SNDP Yogam (1902) – schools, jobs, roads & temple entry, representation; built new temples; Kumaran Asan, Dr Palpu, Sahodaran Ayyappan; Vaikom Satyagraha influenced temple-entry movements.

19.3 Islamic reform movements

  • Aligarh Movement (1875) – Syed Ahmad Khan: journal Tahdhib-ul-Akhlaq; Scientific Society (1864); school at Ghazipur (1864); school at Aligarh 1875 → Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College (1877) → Aligarh Muslim University; MAO Educational Conference (1886).
  • Ahmadiya Movement (1889) – Mirza Ghulam Ahmad; claimed to be Messiah; socially conservative.
  • Deoband Movement (1866/67) – Mohammad Qasim Nanotavi, Rashid Ahmed Gangohi; orthodox revivalist seminary (Saharanpur district).
  • Nadwat al-'ulama (1894, Lucknow) – historian Shibli Nu'mani; Farangi Mahal (Lucknow, accepted Sufism); ahl-i-hadith.

19.4–19.5 Parsi and Sikh reform

  • Rahnumai Mazdayasnan Sabha (1851) – Naoroji Furdonji, Dadabhai Naoroji, K.R. Cama, S.S. Bengalee; paper Rast Goftar (Truth Teller).
  • Singh Sabha (1873) – Khalsa schools; Akali movement against Udasi Mahants → Sikh Gurudwara Act 1922 (amended 1925) → SGPC.

19.6 Reform movements in Tamil Nadu

  • Brahmo and Arya Samaj branches; Keshab Chandra Sen lectured in Madras.
  • Ramalinga Swamigal (Vallalar) (1823–1874): born near Chidambaram; no formal schooling; inspired by Thevaram & Thiruvasagam; against Saiva mutts (Thiruvaduthurai, Dharumapuram, Thiruppanandal) orthodoxy; Sathya Dharma Salai at Vadalur – fed the poor (1860s famine) regardless of caste; Sathya Gnana Sabhai; poems published as Thiruvarutpa (1867); attacked by Sri Lankan Saivite Arumuga Navalar (tract war).
  • Buddhist revival: complete editions of Jeevaka Chintamani (1887) and Manimekalai (1898).
  • Iyotheethoss Pandithar (1845–1914): native doctor; influenced by Col. Olcott; 1890s – Adi Dravidars were the original Buddhists made "untouchable" for opposing Vedic Brahminism; following in north TN and Kolar Gold Fields; associates M. Singaravelu, Prof. P. Lakshmi Narasu; weekly Oru Paisa Tamilan (later Tamilan) from 1908.

19.7 Christian missionaries

  • Company neutral, banned missionaries. William Carey and John Thomas (Baptists) came 1793, settled in Danish Serampore; Serampore Mission (1799) with Joshua Marshman, William Ward.
  • Schools for the deprived; civil rights (roads, upper garments for women); orphanages; famine relief → mass conversions in Tirunelveli, among Malas & Madigas in Andhra; hospitals.

19.8 Significance

  • Reformers faced abuse, fatwas; liberated individuals from fear-born conformity; vernacular scriptures; gave middle classes cultural roots.
