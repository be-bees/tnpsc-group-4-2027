Ch 8 – Indian Economy before Independence (British period)

  • Colonialism = political + social relation in which the ruling country also decides the economic policies of the colony; people of a colony cannot decide use of their own resources.
  • India's sea-route trade with Europe existed even in the Sangam Age.
  • Vasco da Gama reached Calicut on 20 May 1498.
  • Portuguese traded in Goa as early as 1510.
  • 1601 – English East India Company chartered (first inroads into Indian Ocean).
  • 1614 – Sir Thomas Roe got permission from Jahangir to set up factories.
  • 1858 – British Parliament law transferred governance of India from the EIC to the British Crown (100 years after Plassey); the transfer did not materially change the exploitation.
  • Britain exploited India for about two centuries.

Three phases of colonial exploitation (by economic historians):

Decline of Indian handicrafts – India's exports: hand-woven cotton & silk, calicoes, artistic wares, wood carving. Causes:

  • Discriminatory tariff policy of British deliberately destroyed handicrafts.
  • Disappearance of nawabs and kings (patrons).
  • Competition from machine-made goods.
  • Railways widened the domestic market for British goods.

Land tenure systems (before Independence)

Land tenure = system of land ownership and management. Distinguished by: (a) who owns, (b) who cultivates, (c) who pays land revenue to government.

SystemFacts
Zamindari (Landlord–Tenant)Created by EIC – Lord Cornwallis, Permanent Settlement Act 1793. Zamindars = owners, pay revenue. Government share fixed at 10/11th of rent; 1/11th to Zamindar as remuneration.
Mahalwari (Communal farming)Later extended to Madhya Pradesh and Punjab. Land owned by a collective body (villagers) = unit of management; it distributed land, collected revenue and paid the state.
Ryotwari (Owner–cultivator)First introduced in Tamil Nadu; extended to Maharashtra, Gujarat, Assam, Coorg, East Punjab, MP. Tiller holds ownership; direct relation owner–tiller; least oppressive system.

Industrial transition and colonial capitalism

  • 19th century: British investors pioneered industries (state support; aim = profit, not India's growth). By end of 19th c.: about 36 jute mills, 194 cotton mills, plantations; coal > 6 million tonnes/year.
  • 20th century: Swadeshi movement stimulated industry; till WW I (1914) >70 cotton mills, 30 jute mills set up; coal doubled; foundation of iron & steel laid; railways extended.
  • 1924–39: iron & steel, cotton textiles, jute, matches, sugar, paper & pulp brought under protection → rapid growth, foreign competition eliminated.
  • British made India a producer of raw materials and a market for finished goods; developed jute, tea, coffee, cotton textiles, paper, sugar for locational advantage and exploited labour.

Problems of British rule: stunted Indian enterprise; retarded capital formation; drain of wealth financed Britain's development; agriculture stagnated; handicrafts collapsed without a modern industrial base; plantations, mines, jute, banking, shipping run by foreign-managed firms → more drain.

Green Revolution

  • = technological breakthrough in agriculture; in the 1960s traditional practices replaced by modern technology.
  • Pilot in 1960–61 in seven districts = High Yielding Varieties Programme (HYVP).
  • Other names: New Agricultural Strategy, modern agricultural technology, seed–fertilizer–water technology.
  • Father of Green Revolution in India: M.S. Swaminathan (book MCQ).

Achievements

  • Boosted wheat and rice; earlier India depended on US wheat under Public Law 480 (PL 480); now food-surplus, exporting grain.
  • Confined to HYV cereals – rice, wheat, maize, jowar; also aimed at cash crops (sugarcane, cotton, jute, oilseeds, potatoes).
  • Per-hectare productivity up; helped industries making tractors, engines, threshers, pump sets; rural prosperity & employment; multiple cropping raised labour demand; loans from banks & co-operatives.

Weaknesses: still a gamble of monsoons; heavy investment needed; gap between large and small/marginal farmers and between irrigated & rain-fed areas widened; mechanisation caused unemployment (except Punjab, partly Haryana); chemicals reduced soil fertility & harmed health → organic farming now encouraged.

Second Green Revolution: target 400 million tonnes foodgrain (vs 214 MT in 2006–07) by 2020; agri growth 5–6% for 15 years. Needs: GM seeds, private sector role in marketing GM foods, government irrigation & water management, linking of rivers.

Small Scale Industries (SSIs) – role

  • SSIs give 60–70% of India's innovations.
  • Employment: labour-intensive; high employment–capital ratio.
  • Balanced regional development: decentralised, rural/backward areas; reduce urban congestion, slums, pollution.
  • Mobilise local resources (savings, talent, family skills, handicrafts).
  • Optimise capital: less capital per unit output, short gestation/pay-back period, high output–capital ratio.
  • Promote exports, no costly imported machinery; ease balance of payments.
  • Complement large industries as ancillaries (parts, components).
  • Meet consumer demand – anti-inflationary; develop entrepreneurship (job seekers → job givers); raise per capita income, equitable distribution.

Development indicators

  • Human Development Report published annually by UNDP since 1990.
  • HDI developed by Mahbub ul Haq (Pakistan) and Amartya Sen (India), 1990. Three indicators: (1) Longevity – life expectancy at birth; (2) Educational attainment; (3) Standard of living – real GDP per capita (PPP$).
  • Dimension index = (Actual – Minimum) ÷ (Maximum – Minimum); value 0 to 1.
  • National HDR 2011 (Planning Commission): India's HDI 0.302 (1981) → 0.472 (2011).
  • HDR 2016: India 131st of 188 (medium human development, with Bangladesh, Bhutan, Pakistan, Kenya, Myanmar, Nepal); HDI 0.580 (2010) → 0.624 (2015). Top 3: Norway (0.949), Australia (0.939), Switzerland (0.939).
  • Biswajeet Guha: four indices HDI1–HDI4; added quality of life, poverty eradication, urbanisation.
  • PQLI (Physical Quality of Life Index) by Morris D. Morris: life expectancy, infant mortality rate, literacy rate; scale 1 (worst) to 100 (best). Life expectancy upper limit 77 yrs (Sweden 1973), lower 28 yrs (Guinea-Bissau 1960).
  • HDI vs PQLI: HDI includes income; PQLI excludes income (only physical aspects).

Ch 9 – Development Experiences in India: background

  • Chapter motto: "Reform, Perform, Transform".
  • At Independence (1947): backward, lop-sided industry, feudal agriculture, poor transport, health, education; twin problems – poverty and unemployment.
  • 1991: severe Balance of Payments crisis → Structural Reforms in industry, trade, fiscal, agriculture.

LPG – Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation

The triple pillars of the New Economic Policy.

  • Liberalisation: removal/relaxation of government restrictions; facets – delicensing, decontrol, deregulation, subsidies, bigger role for financial institutions.
  • Privatisation: transfer of ownership/management from public to private sector; routes – denationalisation, disinvestment, opening reserved PSE areas (book MCQ adds franchising – "all the above").
  • Globalisation: integration of domestic economy with the world; import liberalisation (cut tariff & non-tariff barriers), opening to FDI and FPI.
  • Impacts of liberalisation (book figure) – positive: expansion of market, infrastructure, higher living standards, international cooperation; negative: cut-throat competition, rise in monopoly, discourages domestic firms, more inequality.
  • For LPG: licensing deterred growth; private sector needed opportunities; (book's critical view of globalisation – developed nations exploit resources and markets of developing ones, dump obsolete technology).
  • Against LPG: unrestricted foreign entry hurts local manufacturers; privatisation keeps monopoly power; disparities among people and regions widen; crises of developed countries spread through trade.
  • Changes after 1991: forex reserves rose; rapid industrialisation; consumption pattern changed; expressways, metro rail, flyovers, airports expanded – but benefits did not reach the marginalised; poverty, unemployment, inflation, agricultural stagnation, food insecurity, migration persist.
  • Disinvestment = selling government securities/equity of PSUs to other PSUs, private sector or banks.

Relative position of Indian economy (book: 2016 data)

  • IMF WEO Oct 2016: India's nominal GDP 2016 = $2,251 billion; 2.99% of world GDP; 17.5% of world population, 2.4% of world area; 7th largest economy; 3rd in Asia after China & Japan; 8.5% of Asia's GDP.
  • World Bank GDP growth chart India vs China: 2014 5.6 vs 7.4; 2015 6.4 vs 7.1; 2016 7.0 vs 7.0; 2017 7.1 vs 7.0.

Industrial sector reforms – New Industrial Policy, 24 July 1991

  • Announced by the Prime Minister on 24 July 1991 (P.V. Narasimha Rao – extra).
  • Objectives: free industry from bureaucrats, remove FDI restrictions, free enterprise from MRTP Act, sustain productivity & employment, international competitiveness.
  1. Industrial delicensing – end of "licence raj" / red tape; licensing now only for alcohol, drugs etc.
  2. Dereservation – only three sectors reserved for public sector: atomic energy, mining, railways (book).
  3. Public sector reform – strategic/priority areas identified; loss-making PSUs sold.
  4. MRTP Act (Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices) amended; Competition Commission became watchdog (book: 2010).
  5. Foreign investment policy – automatic approval of FDI up to 51% in high-tech priority industries (1991), later 74% and 100%; FIPB (Foreign Investment Promotion Board) set up to negotiate & approve FDI.

Before 1991 vs after (book figure): licensing for all commodities → only alcohol, drugs etc.; private sector barred from many industries → only defence, energy, railway for public sector, large-scale privatisation & disinvestment; price controls → market fixes prices.

Impact of LPG on agriculture

  • Agri growth fell from 3.62% (1984/85–1995/96) to 1.97% (1995/96–2004/05); shift to cash crops; indebtedness, farmer suicides.
  • Crop insurance: Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana launched 18 Feb 2016; farmer premium 2% Kharif, 1.5% Rabi, 5% annual commercial/horticultural crops.
  • Cold storage: India largest producer of fruits, 2nd of vegetables; post-harvest losses 25–30%; Cold Storage Order, 1964 under Section 3 of Essential Commodities Act, 1955.
  • Post-harvest losses: ₹92,651 crore/year (ICAR, 2012–13 data at 2014 prices). Wastage (MoFPI 2016): cereals 5–6%, pulses 6–8%, oilseeds 3–10%, fruits & vegetables 5–16%, milk 1%, inland fish 5%, marine fish 10%, meat 3%, poultry 7%. Integrated cold chain support since 2008–09.
  • MoFPI central sector schemes: Mega Food Parks, Integrated Cold Chain, Value Addition & Preservation Infrastructure, Modernisation of Slaughter houses, Quality Assurance/Codex standards, R&D.
  • Kisan Credit Card (KCC): launched 1998 by RBI and NABARD; quick, affordable credit; reduces dependence on informal lenders; issued by co-operative banks, RRBs, public sector banks; being converted to smart card cum debit card.
  • APMC (Agricultural Produce Market Committee) = statutory body set up by state government. Functions: PPP in agri markets; market-led extension; transparent pricing; same-day payment to farmers; promote agriculture; display arrivals & rates.

Agrarian crisis after reforms: (a) high input costs – seed market opened to global agribusiness; many state seed institutions closed in 2003; fake seeds; (b) subsidy cuts on pesticide, fertiliser, electricity → prices up 300%; (c) import duties cut – by 2001 curbs removed on ~1,500 items; cheap imports hit cotton, pepper; (d) less bank credit → moneylenders.

Trade reforms

  • Trade policy 1 April 1992 freed imports of almost all intermediate & capital goods; only 71 items restricted (negative list).
  • Chelliah Committee (Raja Chelliah): peak import duty 50%; 1991–92 budget cut peak rate from >300% to 150%.
  • Foreign Trade Policy (EXIM) 2015–2020 announced 1 April 2015 (Ministry of Commerce & Industry): supports Make in India & Digital India; export obligation reduced by 25%; online digitally signed documents; export obligation period for defence, aerospace, nuclear items = 24 months; aim to double India's share in world trade from 3% by 2020.

Special Economic Zones (SEZs)

  • SEZ Policy April 2000; SEZ Act 2005; ~400 zones notified (book).
  • Asia's first Export Processing Zone: Kandla, 1965 (book MCQ: first SEZ at Kandla).
  • Objectives: attract FDI, raise export share, extra economic activity, jobs, infrastructure, technology exchange.
  • Characteristics: demarcated area with security; single authority; streamlined procedures; separate customs area; liberal laws; more freedom to firms. Book: deprives farmers of land; social & environmental impacts disastrous.

Fiscal reforms and GST

  • Fiscal discipline: fiscal deficit to 3% of GDP (Fund–Bank advice); cut fertiliser subsidy, abolished sugar subsidy, partial disinvestment of PSU equity.
  • GST: comprehensive indirect tax on manufacture, sale, consumption of goods & services; replaces central & state indirect taxes; removes cascading effect; "one-point tax" (VAT was multipoint).
  • GST Act passed by Parliament 29 March 2017; in force 1 July 2017; motto "One nation, one market, one tax".
  • GST rates (book figure): 0%, 5%, 12%, 18%, 28% (highest 28%).
  • Advantages: no cascading, single point tax, higher registration threshold, composition scheme for small business, online procedure, defined e-commerce treatment, logistics efficiency, regulates unorganised sector.

Monetary and financial sector reforms

  • Narasimham Committee Report, 1991: cut SLR from 38.5% to 25% in 3 years; CRR to 3–5% over 4 years.
  • Interest-rate liberalisation (earlier RBI fixed deposit and loan rates); competition among public, private and foreign banks; liberal branch licensing; freedom to relocate branches; guidelines for new private banks; new norms on asset classification & bad-debt provisioning.
  • India's GDP crossed $2 trillion in 2015–16; book: ranked 9th in nominal GDP. "Hindu rate of growth" (low growth) – term by Raj Krishna.
  • SLR = share of deposits banks must keep as cash, gold or approved securities.

Ch 10 – Rural economy: introduction

  • Chapter quote: "India lives in villages" – Mahatma Gandhi.
  • Rural economics = application of economic principles to understand and develop rural areas.
  • Census of India: basic unit of rural areas = revenue village.
  • Census 2011: 6,40,867 villages; 68.84% of India's 121 crore people live in rural areas (book MCQ answer for "% rural" = 60, the nearest option it gives; actual ≈ 69%).
  • Rural problems: backward agriculture, low income, poverty, poor infrastructure, low labour productivity, surplus labour, migration, dependence on nature.

Features of rural economy

  1. Village is an institution – primary institution; sense of belonging and unity.
  2. Dependence on agriculture and allied activities.
  3. Simple life; limited public services; faith, superstition; poor living standards; alcoholism rising.
  4. Low population density; scattered houses.
  5. Employment: unemployment, seasonal unemployment, underemployment = disguised unemployment (more people employed than needed; no rise in output).
  6. Poverty: basic needs (food, clothing, shelter) unmet; 2011–12: about 22 crore rural poor.
  7. Indebtedness: Sir Malcolm Darling (1925) – "An Indian farmer is born in debt, lives in debt, dies in debt and bequeaths debt".
  8. Low rural income.
  9. Dependency on social grants and remittances from urban relatives.
  10. Dualism = co-existence of two extremely different features (modern–traditional, rich–poor, organised–unorganised).
  11. Inequality in income, wealth, land, livestock.
  12. Migration to cities; E.F. Schumacher called it "double poisoning" (villages empty, towns congested); book "Small is Beautiful".

Rural development – meaning and need

  • Rural development = overall improvement in economic & social well-being of villagers and their environment. World Bank: "a strategy designed to improve the economic and social life of a specific group of people – the rural poor".
  • Need: majority lives in villages; villages supply water, milk, food, raw materials to cities; better village education/health avoids urban begging, rag-picking, slums; agricultural employment & food output; checks brain-drain & migration; uses idle resources; narrows rural–urban gap – PURA (Provision of Urban amenities/facilities in Rural Areas) by former President A.P.J. Abdul Kalam; improves HDI, Women Empowerment Index, Gender Disparity Index, PQLI, Gross National Happiness Index.

Problems of rural economy (7 groups)

  1. People-related: illiteracy, no technical know-how, low confidence, sentiments/beliefs.
  2. Agriculture-related: poor awareness & skills, inputs unavailable, poor marketing, weak extension services, small land holdings, sub-division & fragmentation, primitive technology, low public investment, farmers have no say in prices.
  3. Infrastructure: water, electricity, transport, schools, health, storage, banking, insurance.
  4. Economic: cannot adopt high-cost technology, costly inputs, underprivileged rural industries, low income, debt, unequal land; absentee landlords.
  5. Social & cultural: rigid caste system, dominant caste owns land, child marriage, honour killings, female foeticide.
  6. Leadership: incompetent, self-interested leaders; political dominance.
  7. Administrative: political interference, low motivation, poor budget use, no monitoring.

Rural poverty

  • Poverty: failure to earn enough to buy basic minimum subsistence. Poverty line = hypothetical income/consumption line dividing BPL and APL.
  • Rural norm: 2,400 calories per person per day (urban 2,100 – extra).
  • Planning Commission: rural poverty 54.10% (earlier) → 33.8% in 2009–10. SC/ST = 80% of rural poor (2005). 2015: >80 crore in villages; ~22 crore (one quarter) BPL. India home to 22% of world's poor.

Causes: uneven land distribution; lack of non-farm jobs; lack of public investment in human resources (root cause); inflation; low productivity; unequal benefits of growth; low growth rate; stress on capital-intensive large industries; social evils (unproductive spending).

Rural unemployment

  • Unemployment: person actively seeking work but cannot find it at prevailing wage.
  • 2010 Economics Nobel: Peter Diamond, Dale Mortensen, Christopher Pissarides – DMP model (jobs, vacancies, wages).
  • As on 4 Oct 2016: rural unemployment 7.8%, urban 10.1%, all-India 8.5%.
  • Types: (i) Open – visible, among agri labourers, artisans, educated; (ii) Concealed / Under-employment (disguised) – small & marginal farmers, livestock rearers, artisans; disguised unemployment in rural India 25–30%; (iii) Seasonal – work only in ploughing, sowing, weeding, harvesting; single-crop farmer idle 5–7 months.
  • Agricultural Labour Enquiry Committee: average 82 days of unemployment a year for 84% of agricultural labourers.
  • Causes: no skill development; seasonal agriculture; lack of subsidiary occupations (poultry, rope-making, piggery); mechanisation; capital-intensive technology; defective education (degrees without skills).
  • Remedies: subsidiary occupations with loans & marketing; rural works in off-season; irrigation → multiple cropping; rural industrialisation (by government); technical/vocational education.

Rural industries

Use local raw materials, skills, little capital. Types: cottage, village, small, tiny, agro-based.

  • Cottage industries: run by artisans at home, own risk; family labour; hereditary; little/no power; local market. E.g. mat, coir, basket making; handloom, pottery, soap, conch shell, handmade paper, horn & mother-of-pearl buttons, cutlery, locks.
  • Village industries: traditional, local raw material – gur & khandsari, cane & bamboo baskets, shoe-making, pottery, leather tanning.
  • SSIs: near urban centres – sports goods, soap, fans, footwear, sewing machines. Also called MSMEs, defined by MSMED Act 2006 (investment in plant & machinery / equipment).
  • Agro-based: process farm produce – textile, sugar, paper, vegetable oil, tea, coffee.

Rural indebtedness

  • = rural people unable to repay accumulated loans; shows weak financial infrastructure.
  • SECC 2015: ~73% of households rural; 18.5% SC, 11% ST.
  • NSSO 2002–03: only ~30% of poor borrowers get formal bank credit. AIDIS 2002: institutional credit share fell from 66.3% (1991) to 57.1% (2002).
  • Features: nearly three-fourth of rural families in debt; heavier for small farmers; cultivators more indebted; mostly short-term, unproductive, high-interest, from moneylenders.
  • Causes: poverty (vicious circle); monsoon failure; litigation over land; moneylenders' high & compound interest.
  • Measures: regulate moneylenders; rural banks, RRBs, microfinance, SHGs, primary co-operative & land development banks, crop loans, Lead Bank Scheme, MUDRA, subsidiary occupations, skill development. (Book: loans to poor/SHGs costlier than to rich – education loan costlier than car loan.)

Regional Rural Banks, microfinance, SHGs, MUDRA

  • RRBs: recommended by a working group on rural banks appointed by GoI in 1975; for small & marginal farmers, agricultural labourers, artisans, small entrepreneurs; set up jointly by Centre, State and commercial banks; book: 64 RRBs; lend only to weaker sections at co-operative rates.
  • Microfinance (micro credit): loans, savings, insurance for those without access to banks; NGOs play pivotal role; ~150 MFIs in 2009.
  • SHGs: informal voluntary groups of poor of similar background, up to 20 women (average 14); save ₹10–50 a month; lend after 6 months of saving; linked to banks under SHG–Bank Linkage Programme (SBLP), 1992 (NABARD). 2009–10: 1.59 million new SHGs credit-linked, ₹14,453 crore; 6.95 million SHGs with savings accounts (March 2010); 2.2 million SHGs, 33 million members took bank loans. Strong in AP, TN, Kerala, Karnataka (~60% of linkage credit, 2005–06).
  • SHG features: economically homogeneous, self-selected; mostly women, 10–20 members; rules, regular meetings, records; participatory decisions.
  • MUDRA Bank (Micro Units Development and Refinance Agency): public sector institution that refinances MFIs/NBFCs lending to MSMEs; launched 8 April 2015. Objectives: regulate microfinance lenders & borrowers; finance MFIs; register all MFIs and rate/accredit them for the first time; credit guarantee scheme; technology for lending.

Ch 5 – Monetary economics: money

  • Chapter quote: "Inflation is taxation without legislation" – Milton Friedman.
  • Monetary economics studies money as medium of exchange, store of value, unit of account, and monetary systems/institutions.
  • Money = anything generally accepted as payment for goods/services and repayment of debts. Money is the basis of credit.
  • Definitions: "Money is what money does" – Walker; "Money can be anything generally acceptable as a means of exchange and at the same time acts as a measure and a store of value" – Crowther.

Evolution of money

  • Barter system – direct exchange of goods for goods; dates to 6000 BC; introduced by Mesopotamian tribes; adopted by Phoenicians; improved by Babylonians. Commodity money: furs, skins, salt, rice, wheat, utensils, weapons.
  • Metallic standard – gold or silver fixes value; standard coins are full-bodied legal tender (face value = intrinsic metal value).
  • Gold standard – monetary unit defined as fixed weight of gold. Silver standard – unit = fixed weight of silver; currency convertible to silver.
  • Paper currency standard = managed currency standard: notes issued by Treasury/Central Bank, unlimited legal tender, not convertible into metal; quantity controlled for price stability.
  • Plastic money – latest form: cash cards, credit, debit, prepaid, store, forex and smart cards (debit card = plastic money).
  • Crypto currency – digital currency using encryption, independent of central bank, e.g. Bitcoin (decentralised).

Functions of money

GroupFunctions
Primary(i) Medium of exchange – basic function; splits transaction into sale and purchase; (ii) Measure of value – prices expressed in money.
Secondary(i) Store of value; (ii) Standard of deferred payments (borrowing/lending); (iii) Transfer of purchasing power across places.
Contingent(i) Basis of credit system; (ii) facilitates distribution of national income (rent, wage, interest, profit); (iii) equalises marginal utilities & marginal productivities; (iv) raises productivity of capital (money = most liquid capital).
OtherMaintains repayment capacity; represents generalised purchasing power; gives liquidity to capital.

Supply of money

  • Money supply = total money in circulation at a time; a stock at a point of time, a flow over a period.
  • In India currency notes issued by RBI; coins by Ministry of Finance (GoI). Notes are fiat money and legal tender.
  • RBI's four measures:
    • M1 = currency + coins + demand deposits
    • M2 = M1 + savings deposits with post office savings banks
    • M3 = M2 + time deposits of commercial & co-operative banks
    • M4 = M3 + total deposits with post offices
    M1, M2 = narrow money; M3, M4 = broad money; liquidity decreases M1 → M4.
  • Determinants: Currency Deposit Ratio (CDR); Reserve Deposit Ratio (vault cash + bank deposits with RBI); CRR (fraction of deposits kept with RBI); SLR (fraction of demand & time deposits kept in specified liquid assets).
  • Rupee symbol ₹: designed by D. Udaya Kumar (PG of IIT Bombay); approved by Union Cabinet and in use from 15 July 2010; blend of Devanagari "Ra" and Roman "R" without stem; India = 5th country with a unique symbol (after USA, Britain, Japan, EU).

Quantity theories of money

  • Relation between quantity of money and value of money. First propounded 1588 by Italian Davanzati.
  • Fisher's transaction approach: Irving Fisher (American), "The Purchasing Power of Money" (1911). MV = PT ("cash transaction equation"); M = money supply, V = velocity, P = price level, T = volume of transactions. P = MV/T – price level varies directly with M if V, T constant. Extended: PT = MV + M′V′ (bank credit money and its velocity). Based on money as medium of exchange. Price curve P = f(M) at 45°; value of money 1/P falls inversely.
  • Cambridge cash-balance approach:
    • Marshall: M = KPY (K = fraction of real income held as money); value of money 1/P = KY/M – depends on M and K.
    • Keynes: n = pk (p = n/k); extended n = p(k + rk′) (r = cash reserve ratio, k′ = bank deposits in consumer-goods terms); with k, k′, r constant, p changes proportionately with n.

Inflation

  • Inflation = consistent and appreciable rise in general price level; purchasing power of currency falls.
  • "Too much money chasing too few goods" – Coulborn; "a state of abnormal decrease in the quantity of purchasing power" – Gregory.

Creeping (mild/moderate)slow, not perceptible; not dangerous
Walking (trotting)single digit, 3–9%

Galloping / Hyper20–100%, two or three digits; serious depreciation of money
  • Demand-pull: demand exceeds supply. Cost-push: rise in raw-material costs and wages. Wage–price spiral: rising wages ↔ rising prices.
  • Box: first hyperinflation of 21st century – Zimbabwe, 3714% (end of April 2007).
  • By inducement: currency (excess money); credit (liberal bank lending); deficit-induced (printing money for deficit budget); profit-induced; scarcity-induced (hoarding, black market – Venezuela 2018); tax-induced = "taxflation" (excise, customs, sales tax – petrol, diesel).
  • Causes: rise in money supply, disposable income, public expenditure, consumer spending (hire purchase); cheap money policy; deficit financing; black money, hoarding; repayment of public debt; rise in exports.
  • Effects on production: mild inflation is an incentive; hyperinflation discourages saving, drives out foreign capital, cuts investment, causes hoarding and speculation.
  • Effects on distribution: debtors gain, creditors lose; fixed-income groups worst hit (wages, salary, pension, interest, rent); entrepreneurs gain windfall; bond investors lose, share investors gain.
  • Control (Keynes & Friedman): (1) Monetary (central bank) – raise bank rate, sell govt securities (OMO), raise CRR & SLR, consumer credit control, higher margins, higher repo & reverse repo; (2) Fiscal (Keynes) – cut govt expenditure, public borrowing, higher taxes; (3) Other – short-term: rationing via fair price shops, imports; long-term: grow wage-goods output.
  • Deflation – falling prices, reduced money supply, unemployment. Disinflation – slowing inflation (credit control) without raising unemployment or cutting output. Stagflation – stagnant growth + high unemployment + high inflation (stag(nation) + (in)flation).

Trade cycle

  • Also called business cycle / industrial fluctuation – periodic ups and downs in employment, output, income in a capitalist economy.
  • Keynes' definition: periods of good trade (rising prices, low unemployment) alternating with bad trade (falling prices, high unemployment).
  • Four phases: Boom → Recession → Depression → Recovery.
    • Boom/prosperity – full employment and beyond; wages, profits, interest up; optimism.
    • Recession – turning point from boom; often triggered by failure of a company/bank; panic in stock market; liquidity preference rises.
    • Depression – worst phase; losses, closures, unemployment; lowest point = "trough"; Keynes: only autonomous government investment can pull the economy out.
    • Recovery – revival of demand for capital goods; started by innovation, investment or govt spending.
  • Summary: currency created by RBI & Union Govt; bank deposits by commercial & co-operative banks.

Ch 6 – Banking: introduction and history

  • Quote: "Commercial banks are the institutions that make short term loans to business and in the process create money" – Culbertson. Finance = life blood of economic activity.
  • World's first lending bank: Bank of Venice, 1157.
  • Oldest central bank: Riksbank of Sweden (from private bank of 1656; sole note-issue right 1897).
  • Bank of England founded 1694 (book: developed central banking art as first bank of issue, 1864).
  • Brussels International Finance Conference 1920 → many central banks 1921–54: South African Reserve Bank 1921, Central Bank of China 1928, Reserve Bank of New Zealand 1934, RBI 1935, Central Bank of Ceylon 1950, Bank of Israel 1954.
  • India: first bank Bank of Hindustan (1770). Three Presidency Banks: Bank of Bengal (1806), Bank of Bombay (1840), Bank of Madras (1843) → merged 1921 into Imperial Bank of India → became SBI (1955).

Commercial banks – functions

Commercial bank: profit-making; accepts public deposits, lends; deals mainly with businesses; no long-term credit (must keep liquidity).

  • Primary: (1) Accepting deposits – demand deposits (withdraw anytime, cheque/ATM) and time deposits (fixed period, higher interest, notice needed); (2) Advancing loans – overdraft, cash credit, discounting bills of exchange.
  • Secondary – agency: collecting cheques (via clearing house of central bank), collecting income (credit voucher), paying expenses (debit voucher).
  • General utility: lockers (bank not responsible for contents), traveller's cheques, foreign exchange (with central bank permission); transfer of funds (draft, telephonic, electronic); letter of credit; underwriting securities; e-banking.
  • Other: increase money supply; credit creation (= multiplication of loans and advances); collection of statistics.

Credit creation

  • Money is "created" when banks' lending adds to money supply; "destroyed" when loans are repaid. "Every loan creates its own deposit."
  • Primary (passive) deposit – customer brings cash. Derived (active) deposit – created without prior cash (loan credited to account).
  • Example: cash reserve 20%; deposit ₹1000 → lends 800 → 640 … total ₹5000. Money multiplier = 1/r = 1/0.20 = 5. Assumptions: no excess reserves, no cash drain.

Role of commercial banks in economic development

  1. Capital formation – mobilise small savings.
  2. Creation of credit.
  3. Channelising funds to productive investment.
  4. Encouraging right type of industries (book: sub-prime lending caused the 2007–08 US crisis).
  5. Monetising debt – discounting bills.
  6. Finance to government – long-term via govt securities, short-term via Treasury Bills. RBI gave ₹68,000 crore to GoI in 2018–19 (99% of its surplus).
  7. Employment generation (post-nationalisation branch expansion).
  8. Promoting entrepreneurship – up to 100% credit for viable projects.

Non-Banking Financial Institutions (NBFI/NBFC)

  • Do not have a full banking licence / not supervised by central bank; borrow and lend; operate in money and capital markets.
  • Two categories: (1) Stock exchange; (2) other – finance companies, finance corporations, chit funds, building societies, issue houses, investment & unit trusts, insurance companies.

Central bank – Reserve Bank of India

  • Central bank manages a country's currency, money supply, interest rates; oversees banks.
  • RBI: commenced 1 April 1935 under RBI Act 1934; originally private shareholders (shares of ₹100); nationalised 1 January 1949; HQ moved Calcutta → Mumbai in 1937; first Governor Sir Osborne Smith; Governor + 4 Deputy Governors + central board appointed by GoI. Called "Lender of Last Resort".
  • Box: paper money first printed by Bank of Bengal, Bank of Bombay, Bank of Madras (18th–19th c.); first rupee by Sher Shah Suri – 40 copper paisa per rupee; "rupee" from Sanskrit Raupya (silver); notes carry amount in 17 languages (English, Hindi on front + 15 on back).

Monetary policy

  • Monetary policy = central bank's macro policy on money supply and interest rate; demand-side policy.
  • Milton Friedman – "monetarism", Nobel 1976; book "A Monetary History of the United States, 1867–1960" – Great Depression of 1930s due to Federal Reserve's bungling.
  • Expansionary = cheap money policy – low interest, more money → fight unemployment. Contractionary = dear money policy – high interest → curbs inflation, may cause recession.
  • Objectives: (1) Neutrality of money – Wicksteed, Hayek, Robertson; (2) Exchange rate stability – traditional, gold-standard era ("expand currency when gold comes in…"); (3) Price stability – Gustav Cassel, Keynes; most genuine objective; not price rigidity; (4) Full employment – after Keynes' General Theory (1936); (5) Economic growth; (6) BoP equilibrium – post-war.

Ch 7 – International economics: meaning and subject matter

  • Quote: "Economies are linked internationally through trade in goods and through financial markets" – Dornbusch, Fischer & Startz.
  • Originated in Western Europe; shaped by Adam Smith, David Ricardo, F.W. Taussig, Haberler, J.S. Mill, Bela Balassa.
  • International economics = exchange of goods and services between two or more countries; economic interdependence among nations.
  • Subject matter: (1) Pure theory of trade (causes, terms of trade, BoP); (2) Policy issues (free trade vs protection, dumping, exchange control, foreign aid, FDI); (3) International cartels & trade blocs (customs unions, monetary unions, MNCs); (4) International financial & trade institutions (IMF, IBRD, WTO).

Theories of international trade

  • Timeline (book chart): Mercantilism (pre-16th c., "us vs them") → Absolute advantage – Adam Smith, 1776 → Comparative advantage – David Ricardo, 1817 → Factor proportions – Heckscher–Ohlin, 1919 → International product life cycle – Raymond Vernon, 1966.
  • Absolute cost advantage (Adam Smith): each country specialises in the good it produces at an absolute advantage. Assumptions: 2×2 model, labour only factor, homogeneous labour, cost = labour, no transport cost. Example: India wheat 20 / cloth 6; China wheat 8 / cloth 14 → India exports wheat, China cloth.
  • Comparative cost theory (David Ricardo) – "Principles of Political Economy and Taxation" (1817); refined by J.S. Mill, Marshall, Taussig, Cairnes, Bastable, Haberler. Trade occurs even without absolute difference if comparative cost differences exist; a country exports what it is relatively best (or least bad) at. 11 assumptions incl. labour only cost, equal efficiency, mobile within/immobile between countries, constant returns, free trade, no transport cost, perfect competition, full employment.
  • Ricardo example (labour units per unit): America cloth 100, wheat 120; India cloth 90, wheat 80 → India has absolute advantage in both but comparative advantage in wheat (80/120 < 90/100); America exports cloth. With trade India needs 160 units instead of 170; America 200 instead of 220.
  • Criticism: labour is a small part of cost; labour efficiency differs across countries.
  • Modern theory = Heckscher–Ohlin / Factor Endowment Theory: Swedish economists Eli Heckscher and his student Bertil Ohlin, 1919. Basis of trade = difference in factor endowments and factor proportions. 2×2×2 model. H-O theorem: "A capital-abundant country will export capital-intensive goods, while a labour-abundant country will export labour-intensive goods." Abundance is relative: India K/L = 40/50 = 0.8; America 30/24 = 1.25 → America is capital-abundant though India has more capital. Limitations: endowments change; factor efficiency differs.

Gains from international trade

  • General: variety of goods, employment, industrialisation of backward nations, better relations, division of labour, transport expansion.

Balance of trade vs balance of payments

  • Balance of Trade (BoT) – exports and imports of commodities only = "visible trade". Favourable if exports > imports; unfavourable otherwise.
  • Balance of Payments (BoP) – systematic record of all economic transactions with the rest of the world (visible + invisible). Receipts = credit; payments = debit.
  • Components: (a) Current account – goods, services (tourism, transport, royalty), unilateral transfers (gifts, aid); (b) Capital account – direct investment, financial instruments, deposits, gold; (c) Official reserve assets account – gold, convertible currencies, SDRs, net IMF position.
  • Balanced: R/P = 1; favourable: R/P > 1; unfavourable: R/P < 1.
  • Types of disequilibrium: Cyclical (different business-cycle phases, different elasticities); Secular (long-run – e.g. India since 1951, investment > saving, imports > exports); Structural (new supply sources, substitutes, resource exhaustion, transport changes).
  • Causes: cyclical fluctuations, structural changes, development expenditure, consumerism, demonstration effect (imitation of western goods), heavy borrowing & debt service, technological backwardness (e.g. unused solar energy → oil imports), global politics (arms purchases).

Correcting BoP disequilibrium

  • Automatic (free exchange rate): price, interest rate, income adjustments, capital flows.
  • Deliberate – monetary: monetary contraction; devaluation (deliberate cut in official external value, e.g. ₹70 → ₹80 per $; makes exports cheaper, imports dearer); exchange control (state takes over forex; exporters surrender forex).
  • Indian rupee devalued three times: 29 September 1949, 6 June 1966, 1 July 1991.
  • Trade measures: export promotion (cut export duties, subsidies, incentives); import control (duties, quotas, licensing, prohibition – may encourage smuggling).
  • Miscellaneous: foreign loans, foreign investment, tourism, inward remittances, import substitution.

Exchange rate

  • FOREX = foreign currencies; system of converting one currency into another and transferring money between countries.
  • Exchange rate = price in home currency for one unit of foreign currency (external value of domestic currency). Book: 1 USD = ₹1 in 1947; ≈ ₹70 in 2018.
  • Equilibrium exchange rate keeps BoP in equilibrium over a period – Ragnar Nurkse; set where demand for forex = supply.
  • Systems: Fixed (pegged/stable) – set by government (gold standard); Flexible (floating) – market-determined; Managed floating – India.
  • Types of rates: nominal, real, NEER, REER. Real exchange rate = e × Pf / P; if = 1 → purchasing power parity.
  • Determinants: inflation differentials (inverse relation), interest-rate differentials, current account deficit, public debt, terms of trade, political & economic stability, recession, speculation; also global political dominance and sanctions.

FDI and trade

  • FDI = investment in a foreign country with some control and participation in management (by MNEs); differs from portfolio investment (short-term, no control).
  • FPI – deposits, stocks, bonds (part of capital account). FII – hedge funds, insurance, pension, mutual funds.
  • Objectives: sales expansion, acquisition of resources, diversification, minimise competitive risk.
  • Advantages: investment, income, jobs; technology transfer; tax revenue; managerial revolution; exports up; competition; fills savings gap and forex gap; ancillary industries; Indian firms now invest in Nepal, Uganda, Ethiopia, Kenya. Largest FDI to India comes from Mauritius.
  • Disadvantages: goes to high-profit not priority areas; inappropriate technology; royalty/dividend drain hurts BoP; political interference; unethical practices; hurts SMEs; monopolies.
  • FDI in India: opened by 1991 reforms. Benefited sectors: finance, insurance, telecom, hospitality & tourism, pharma, software & IT. FDI not permitted (book): arms & ammunition, atomic energy, railways, coal & lignite, mining of iron, manganese, chrome, gypsum, sulphur, gold, diamonds, copper.
  • FDI inflow: $97 million (1990–91) → $5,535 m (2004–05) → $32,955 m (2011–12). UNCTAD WIR 2018: India $44 bn (2016) → $40 bn (2017).

  • Facts: 21% of global GDP, 43% of world population, reserves ≈ $4.4 trillion.
  • Objectives: exclusive trade bloc; use currencies other than US dollar; regional cooperation; bloc for developing countries. Functions: UN Security Council reform; South–South cooperation; bridge developed–developing gap; climate & trade negotiations; protect middle powers.

Ch 9 – Fiscal economics: public finance

  • Chapter opens with Thirukkural 478 (income may be small; no ruin if spending is kept in check).
  • Old name Public Finance, new name Fiscal Economics; "fiscal" from a word meaning basket = the public purse.
  • Public finance = financial aspects of government – revenue, expenditure and adjustment of one to the other.
  • Definitions: "Public finance … lies on the border line between Economics and Politics…" – Hugh Dalton; "an investigation into the nature and principles of state revenue and expenditure" – Adam Smith.
  • Scope (5 divisions): Public Revenue, Public Expenditure, Public Debt, Financial Administration (budget = annual master financial plan), Fiscal Policy (taxes, subsidies, debt, expenditure).

Public vs private finance

  • Similarities: rationality; limit to borrowing; optimum use of limited resources; depend on administration.
  • Differences: government adjusts income to expenditure, individuals adjust expenditure to income ("decide the cloth according to the coat"); government can borrow internally/externally and from its own people; can print currency; long-term vs short-term vision; social benefit vs profit; coercive revenue; ability to make huge deliberate changes.

Functions of the modern (welfare) state

  • Modern state = welfare state, not police state. Functions: defence, judiciary, enterprises, social welfare, infrastructure, macro-economic policy, social justice, control of monopoly.
  • Three roles: producer of goods & services; supplier of public & social goods; regulator.

Public expenditure

  • = spending by Central, State and Local governments to satisfy collective social wants.
  • Classification by benefit (Cohn and Plehn): (a) benefits whole society (defence, education, health); (b) special benefit to some + common benefit (justice); (c) direct benefit to a group, indirect to society (social security, pension, unemployment relief); (d) special benefit to some (subsidy to an industry).
  • Classification by function (Adam Smith): protection, commercial, development functions.
  • Causes of growth:
    1. Population: 36.1 crore (1951) → 121 crore (2011).
    2. Defence: ₹10,874 crore (1990–91) → ₹2,95,511 crore (2018–19).
    3. Subsidies: ₹9,581 crore (1990–91) → ₹2,29,715.67 crore (2018–19); corporate incentives > ₹5 lakh crore.
    4. Debt servicing: interest ₹21,500 crore (1990–91) → ₹5,75,794 crore (2018–19).
    5. Development projects.
    6. Urbanisation: ~17% urban (1950–51) → ~43%; >54 million-plus cities.
    7. Industrialisation.
    8. Grants-in-aid to States/UTs for disasters.

Public revenue

  • Dalton: public receipts (wide – includes loans) vs public revenue (narrow – revenue resources only).
  • Two types: tax revenue and non-tax revenue.
  • Tax = compulsory payment; definitions – "Anatol Murad" (compulsory, without reference to benefit) and Dalton (compulsory contribution, not a penalty).
  • Characteristics of tax: compulsory (refusal punishable); no quid pro quo; involves sacrifice; not a fine. Examples: income tax, corporate tax, sales tax, surcharge, cess.
  • Non-tax revenue: (1) Fees – for a service (passport, licence), no compulsion; (2) Fines – penalty; (3) earnings of public enterprises; (4) special assessment / betterment levy – on owners whose property gains from a public project; (5) gifts, grants, aid (foreign aid); (6) Escheats – state's claim to property of a person who dies without legal heirs or will.

Adam Smith's four canons of taxation: Ability (equality), Certainty, Convenience, Economy. (Canon of simplicity is not Smith's.) Canons refer to an individual tax, not the whole system.

Direct and indirect taxes

  • Direct tax: on income & wealth, paid directly; burden cannot be shifted; progressive. Policy by Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT), Ministry of Finance. E.g. income tax.
  • Merits: equity, certainty, elasticity, economy. Demerits: unpopular, may hurt productivity, inconvenient, tax evasion → black money.
  • Indirect tax: on goods/services, burden can be shifted; same for rich and poor. Types: excise duty (manufacturer), sales tax (retailer), customs duty (imports), entertainment tax (theatre owners), service tax. E.g. GST.
  • Merits: wider coverage (everyone pays; only ~2% pay income tax); equitable if luxuries taxed; economical (traders are honorary collectors); checks harmful consumption – "sin taxes" on tobacco, liquor; convenient.
  • Demerits: higher collection cost; inelastic; regressive; uncertain – Dalton: "under indirect taxes 2+2 is not 4 but 3 or even less"; no civic consciousness.

GST (Goods and Services Tax)

  • Indirect tax replacing many indirect taxes; Act passed 29 March 2017; in force 1 July 2017; comprehensive, multi-stage, destination-based tax on every value addition; one indirect tax for the whole country.
  • Destination-based: goods made in Tamil Nadu and consumed in Karnataka → tax revenue goes to Karnataka.
  • Components: CGST (Centre, intra-state), SGST (State, intra-state), IGST (Centre, inter-state, e.g. Maharashtra → TN; shared by destination).
  • Intra-state sale: CGST + SGST (replaced VAT + central excise/service tax), shared equally. Inter-state: IGST (replaced CST + excise/service tax).
  • Sales tax = multipoint with cascading; VAT = multipoint without cascading; GST = one point without cascading (book).
  • Advantages: removes cascading (tax on tax) → lower cost; technology-driven – registration, returns, refunds online on GST Portal.

Public debt

  • Definitions: Philip E. Taylor (Treasury promises to pay principal + interest, to finance current deficit); Carl S. Shoup.
  • Internal debt – from citizens, banks, LIC, UTI, GIC, central bank (only transfers wealth). External debt – IMF, World Bank, IDA, ADB, foreign governments.
  • External debt (June 2018, World Bank/RBI): USA 1st ($21.17 trn); UK, France, Germany …; China 13th; India 22nd – $529 bn, ~20% of GDP, $380 per capita.
  • Causes of growth: war & war preparation; social obligations of welfare state; development & deficits; employment; controlling inflation (withdraw money); fighting depression (compensatory spending).
  • Redemption methods: (1) Sinking fund – yearly fixed contribution; first in England by Walpole; (2) Conversion – high-interest loan into low-interest one (Dalton: eases burden); (3) Budgetary surplus (rare); (4) Terminal annuity – equal annual instalments (easiest repayment); (5) Repudiation – refusing to repay (destroys credibility); (6) compulsory reduction in interest rate; (7) Capital levy – progressive levy on capital assets – most controversial.

Budget

  • From French "bougette" = small leather bag. Annual financial statement of estimated income & expenditure.
  • Definitions: René Stourm ("preliminary approved plan of public revenue and expenditure"); Bastable.
  • Union Budget – Article 112 (annual financial statement to Parliament); State Budget – Article 202.
  • Types:
    • Revenue budget (revenue receipts – tax & non-tax; revenue expenditure) and capital budget (loans, recoveries, disinvestment; capital expenditure).
    • Supplementary budget – for war, tsunami, floods.
    • Vote-on-account – Article 116; "lame-duck budget" before elections; permits withdrawal from Consolidated Fund of India, usually for not more than two months.
    • Zero-Base Budget – first presented in India in 1987–88; every item evaluated afresh.
    • Performance budget – first in USA, recommended by the Hoover Commission (book: "Administrative Reforms Commission 1949 under Sir Hooper"); in India called "Outcome Budget".
    • Balanced (revenue = expenditure) vs unbalanced – surplus (revenue > expenditure) or deficit (expenditure > revenue).
  • Procedure: prepared by Ministry of Finance (State: Finance Department) → cabinet approval → presented by Finance Minister in Lok Sabha (money bills only in the Lower House) → finance bill → appropriation bill → execution by departments.
  • Parliamentary control: accounts in three parts – Consolidated Fund, Contingency Fund, Public Account; committees – Public Accounts Committee and Estimates Committee.

Federal finance (Centre–State)

  • = assigning revenue sources to Centre and States. Seventh Schedule: book figures – Union List 100 subjects (defence, railways, posts), State List 61 (public health, police), Concurrent List 52 (electricity, trade unions, economic & social planning). [Originally 97/66/47 – extra.]

  • State sources: capitation tax; succession & estate duty on agricultural land; excise on alcohol, opium; land revenue; stamp duty on other documents; agricultural income tax; land & buildings; mineral rights; electricity; entry of goods into local area; sales tax (other than newspapers); ads (non-newspaper); road & inland water transport; vehicles; animals & boats; profession tax; luxuries, entertainment, betting; tolls.

  • Article 269 – levied and collected by Union, assigned to States: succession & estate duty (non-agri), railway fares/freights, stock exchange transactions, newspapers & ads, terminal taxes, inter-state sales tax.
  • Articles 270 and 272 – levied & collected by Union, shared with States: income tax (not corporation tax), Union excise (other than medicinal/toilet); sharing as per Finance Commission.
  • Principles of federal finance (9): independence, equity, uniformity, adequacy, fiscal access, integration & coordination, efficiency (no double taxation), administrative economy, accountability.

Finance Commission

  • Quasi-judicial body under Article 280; first set up 1951; every 5 years (constituted ~2 years before its period); temporary body; appointed by the President.
  • Reduces vertical (Centre–States) and horizontal (between States) imbalances.
  • Functions – Article 280(3): recommend to the President (1) distribution of net tax proceeds between Union and States and among States; (2) grants-in-aid to States under Article 275(1) and principles; (3) any other matter referred by the President (debt relief, calamity relief etc.).

Local finance

  • Four local bodies: Village Panchayats, District Boards/Zila Parishads, Municipalities, Municipal Corporations.
  • Village panchayat: usually one revenue village; settles small disputes, roads, primary schools, dispensaries, water supply. Revenue: general property tax, land tax, profession tax, tax on animals & vehicles; also octroi, theatre, pilgrim, marriage, birth/death, labour taxes – only with state sanction.
  • Zila Parishad: at district level (revenue district); in Tamil Nadu it is a coordinating body supervising Panchayat Samitis. Revenue: state grants, land cess, tolls, fees, property income, centrally sponsored scheme grants, fairs, property tax.
  • Municipalities (urban): sanitation, health, roads, lighting, water, parks, hospitals, drainage, primary education. Revenue: property tax, octroi & terminal tax, profession tax, vehicles & animals, theatre/show tax, state grants.
  • Municipal corporations: wider powers – water, drainage, slum clearance, housing, town planning; taxes on property, vehicles, trades, theatres, goods entering city, advertisements, octroi.

Fiscal policy

  • Became popular after the Great Depression and Keynes' "New Economics". = budgetary manipulation of output, employment, saving, investment.
  • Definitions: Arthur Smithies; Buehler.
  • Fiscal instruments (tools/levers): taxation (raise to curb inflation, cut in depression), public expenditure (raise in recession, cut in inflation), public debt.
  • Objectives (7): full employment (e.g. MGNREGS); price stability; economic growth (tax holidays, rebates); equitable distribution (progressive taxes, free education, noon meal, subsidies); exchange stability (export incentives, import duties); capital formation; regional balance (incentives for backward regions).
  • Glossary: proportional tax (same rate), progressive (rate rises with income), regressive (higher burden on poor); quid pro quo = something in return.

3. GST – Goods and Services Tax

  • 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016 (Class 12 Pol. Sci. annexure). (extra: Art. 246A, 269A, 279A – GST Council, chaired by the Union Finance Minister, State finance ministers as members.)
  • GST Act passed by Parliament 29 March 2017; in force 1 July 2017.
  • Motto: "One Nation, One Market, One Tax". France first country to adopt GST (1954) (Class 10).
  • Nature: comprehensive, multi-stage, destination-based indirect tax on value addition; removes cascading; "one-point tax" (sales tax multipoint with cascading; VAT multipoint without).
  • Components: CGST (Centre, intra-state), SGST (State, intra-state), IGST (Centre, inter-state & imports), UTGST (UTs). CGST+SGST shared equally.
  • Replaced: SGST ← VAT/sales tax, purchase tax, entertainment tax, luxury tax, lottery tax, state surcharges/cesses; CGST ← central excise, service tax, countervailing duty, additional customs duty, surcharges, education cesses (Class 10).
  • Slabs (books): 0%, 5%, 12%, 18%, 28% (four main rates; 28% highest). GST Council fitted >1,300 goods and 500 services into these slabs (Class 8 Maths).
  • Outside GST: petrol, diesel (taxed separately); exempt: egg, honey, milk, salt, vegetables, food grains.
  • (extra – current) From 22 Sept 2025 ("GST 2.0") the Council moved to two main slabs 5% and 18% plus a 40% rate for sin/luxury goods; textbooks still show the 5/12/18/28 structure.
  • Direct-tax policy: CBDT; Chelliah Committee (Raja Chelliah) – tax/tariff reform (peak import duty 50%).

1. Pre-Independence plans and planning bodies

  • ☐ 1934 Visvesvaraya – "Planned Economy for India" – 10-year plan.
  • ☐ 1938 National Planning Committee – Nehru (INC).
  • ☐ Bombay Plan – 8 industrialists, 15-year investment plan (book: 1940; usually 1944 – extra; signatories incl. J.R.D. Tata, G.D. Birla – extra).
  • ☐ Gandhian Plan 1944 – S.N. (Shriman Narayan) Agarwal – agriculture, rural, cottage.
  • ☐ People's Plan 1945 – M.N. Roy – mechanised agriculture, state distribution.
  • ☐ Sarvodaya Plan 1950 – J.P. Narayan (Vinoba Bhave's ideas).
  • ☐ Planning Commission – 15 March 1950, Cabinet resolution, first Chairman Nehru; 1st plan from 1 April 1951.
  • ☐ NITI Aayog – 1 January 2015, Cabinet resolution; PM chairs; first VC Arvind Panagariya.
  • ☐ Planning idea from USSR (1928); Nehru's model = "Nehruvian Socialism"; Preamble "socialist".

4. Land reforms

  • ☐ Agriculture/land = State subject; each state passed its own laws.
  • ☐ British systems: Zamindari (Permanent Settlement 1793, Cornwallis; Bengal & north India), Ryotwari (south India – first in Tamil Nadu; cultivator pays directly), Mahalwari (village collectively).
  • ☐ Zamindari abolition: by 1949 UP, MP, Bihar, Madras, Assam, Bombay; West Bengal only in 1955; protected by 1st Amendment (1951) and 4th Amendment (1955); completed by 1956; ~30 lakh tenants got 62 lakh ha; compensation paid ₹16,420 lakh (only ~1/4 of due). Most successful land reform.
  • ☐ Tenancy reform aims: regulate rent (fixed at 1/4–1/3 of produce), secure tenant rights, confer ownership. Largely failed (oral leases, "personal cultivation" evictions); success in Kerala and West Bengal (Operation Barga – extra).
  • ☐ Land ceiling: maximum land a person can own; Tamil Nadu first implemented 1961; unit changed from landholder to family in 1972; only ~65 lakh ha declared surplus, given to ~55 lakh tenants (~1 ha each); exemptions (orchards, plantations, trusts) misused.
  • ☐ Bhoodan – Vinoba Bhave – voluntary land gift (began 1951, Pochampalli, Telangana – extra).
  • ☐ Appraisal: social justice achieved (zamindari gone, peasants aware); economic efficiency not improved.

5. Green Revolution

  • ☐ Background: food imports under US PL-480 (early 1960s); Ford Foundation helped plan HYV programme.
  • ☐ Pilot 1960–61 in 7 districts (Class 11 calls it HYVP; officially IADP – extra); HYV wheat & rice adopted 1965 (Class 12 History) – during plan holiday.
  • ☐ Other names: New Agricultural Strategy, seed–fertiliser–water technology. Father in India: M.S. Swaminathan (world: Norman Borlaug – extra).
  • ☐ Package: HYV seeds + subsidised fertiliser + cheap co-operative credit + MSP + procurement by FCI + buffer stocks + PDS.
  • ☐ Results: rice 35 MT (1960–61) → 104 MT (2011–12); wheat 11 → 94 MT; foodgrain 54 MT on 98 m ha (early 1950s, 547 kg/ha) → 5-fold output on 122 m ha; milk 8×, eggs 40×, fish 13×. Not self-sufficient in pulses and oilseeds.
  • ☐ Limits: mainly wheat & rice, Punjab-Haryana; regional & farmer inequality; chemical overuse → organic farming push. Second Green Revolution target 400 MT by 2020.

6. Credit to farmers – KCC and institutions

  • ☐ Kisan Credit Card – 1998, by RBI and NABARD (on R.V. Gupta Committee advice – extra); issued by co-operative banks, RRBs, public sector banks; now smart card-cum-debit card.
  • ☐ NABARD – July 1982 (took over ARDC 1963 and RBI's agri-credit role). RRBs – 1975 (Centre 50 : State 15 : sponsor bank 35). MUDRA – 8 April 2015. SHG–Bank Linkage – 1992. Lead Bank Scheme (1969 – extra).
  • ☐ PM Fasal Bima Yojana – 18 Feb 2016: premium 2% kharif, 1.5% rabi, 5% commercial/horticulture.
  • ☐ APMC – statutory body of state government; TN farmers' markets = Uzhavar Sandhai.

7. Rural employment – IRDP and MGNREGA

  • ☐ Scheme years: 20-Point Programme 1975; Food for Work 1977; IRDP 1978 (Class 11) / nationwide 1980 (Class 12 History); TRYSEM 1979; NREP 1980; RLEGP 1983; Indira Awas 1985; JRY 1989.
  • ☐ IRDP (1980–1999): assets to rural poor; all 5,011 blocks; 600 families per block in 5 years (15 million families); subsidy 25% small farmers, 33.3% marginal farmers & labourers, 50% tribals, shared Centre:State 50:50; 53.5 million households by 1999; restructured 1999 (into SGSY – extra).
  • ☐ MGNREGA: National Rural Employment Guarantee Act 2005 (renamed after Mahatma Gandhi – 2009, extra); scheme from 2006 (2 Feb 2006, Anantapur – extra).
  • ☐ Key rules: 100 days wage employment per household per year; adult volunteers for unskilled manual work; legal right to work; job card; work within 15 days or unemployment allowance; worksite within 5 km; no contractors; wage:material = 60:40; one-third women; equal wages; implemented by Gram Panchayats; wages into bank/post office accounts; self-targeting (not BPL-based).
  • ☐ Results: 2006–12 ≈ ₹1,10,000 crore wages, 1,200 crore person-days; ~1/4 rural households each year; raised agricultural wages, cut distress migration, empowered women; good in TN, AP, Rajasthan.

8. PDS and food security

  • ☐ FAO (2009) definition: all people, at all times, physical, social & economic access to sufficient, safe, nutritious food.
  • ☐ M.S. Swaminathan on nutrition security: balanced diet, safe water, hygiene, primary health care, primary education.
  • ☐ Three components: availability, access, absorption.
  • ☐ MSP – fixed by an expert group before sowing (CACP – extra); FCI procures, holds buffer stock (FCI est. 1965 – extra).
  • ☐ Tamil Nadu = only state with Universal PDS; others Targeted PDS (TPDS – 1997, extra).
  • ☐ National Food Security Act 2013: covers 75% rural, 50% urban (priority households) (5 kg grain/person/month; AAY 35 kg/household – extra). TN implemented NFSA on 1 November 2016 (after 3 years).
  • ☐ Consumer co-operatives: three-tier (primary, central stores, state federation); >50,000 village societies; ~94% of TN fair price shops run by co-operatives.
  • ☐ New Agricultural (Export) Policy 2018 – export curbs removed on most organic/processed products.
  • ☐ MPI – UNDP + OPHI, 2010. TN MPI districts (book) – top: Kancheepuram, Chennai, Cuddalore, Coimbatore, Nagapattinam; bottom: Dharmapuri, Perambalur, Ramanathapuram, Virudhunagar, Ariyalur.
  • ☐ PPP: India 3rd largest economy by PPP (2019: China 27.4, US 21.4, India 11.4 Int $ trn).
  • ☐ NFHS 2015–16: underweight women – 27% rural, 16% urban; >half of women anaemic; children anaemic 60% rural, 56% urban; stunted 41% rural, 31% urban; underweight ~20%.

10. Tamil Nadu economy – top facts

  • ☐ TN: 2nd largest state economy; GSDP $207.8 bn (2016–17); per capita ~1.75× India; services 63.7%, industry 28.5%, agriculture 7.76%.
  • ☐ 1st in factories (17%) & industrial workers (16%), MSMEs (15.07%), higher-education GER (46.9%), engineering colleges (517), wind and solar capacity, C–D ratio (119.15%), tourist arrivals; 3rd in HDI and NITI health index (after Kerala, Punjab).
  • ☐ Census 2011: population 7.21 crore; density 555; urban 48.4%; sex ratio 995; literacy 80.33%; IMR 17; MMR 79. Highest sex ratio – Nilgiris (1041); lowest – Theni (900); highest literacy – Kanyakumari; lowest – Dharmapuri.
  • ☐ Wells = 56% of irrigation; NE monsoon main rain; 17 river basins; lignite 87% of India's reserves (Neyveli).
  • ☐ City titles: Chennai – Detroit of Asia, health & banking capital, EMS hub; Tiruppur – Knitting City; Sivakasi – Little Japan (Nehru); Coimbatore – Pump City (wet grinder GI); Salem – Steel City; Thoothukudi – Gateway of TN; TN = Yarn Bowl (41% cotton yarn); Karur – bus bodies & home textiles; TNPL Karur – world's largest bagasse paper mill.
  • ☐ Agriculture: 1st – loose flowers, banana, coconut, plantation crops; productivity 1st – maize, kambu, groundnut, oilseeds, cotton; 2nd in rice (after West Bengal).
  • ☐ Energy: Kudankulam 2×917 MW; Kalpakkam 2×235 MW; Muppandal wind farm; thermal at Ennore, Mettur, Neyveli, Thoothukudi, North Chennai.
  • ☐ Ports: major – Chennai, Ennore (Kamarajar), Thoothukudi; 23 minor (TN Maritime Board). Southern Railway HQ Chennai.
  • ☐ Welfare (Class 10): Puratchi Thalaivar MGR Nutritious Meal Programme – largest noon meal scheme; ICDS – 54,439 centres (49,499 anganwadi + 4,940 mini) in 434 blocks; Dr Muthulakshmi Reddy Maternity Benefit Scheme – ₹12,000 (book; raised to ₹18,000 – extra); CM Comprehensive Health Insurance Scheme 2011–12; free 108 ambulance (TNHSP); "Malnutrition Free Tamil Nadu" policy.
  • ☐ (extra – recent TN schemes often asked) Illam Thedi Kalvi (2021, learning-loss recovery); free town-bus travel for women – Vidiyal Payanam (2021); Pudhumai Penn (2022, ₹1,000/month to government-school girls in higher education); CM Breakfast Scheme (15 Sept 2022, Madurai); Naan Mudhalvan (2022, skilling); Makkalai Thedi Maruthuvam (2021); Kalaignar Magalir Urimai Thogai (15 Sept 2023, ₹1,000/month to women heads of families).
