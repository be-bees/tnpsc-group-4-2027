Read: Class 10 Social Science (2025), Economics Unit 1 "Gross Domestic Product and its Growth: an Introduction", PDF p. 298–308 (p. 297 = "ECONOMICS" title page)

Introduction – goods and services (hotel example)

  • Goods = tangible, physical things (idli, tea). Services = intangible activities (cooking, serving). GDP measures goods and services produced and paid for, nationwide.

Definition of GDP (மொத்த உள்நாட்டு உற்பத்தி)

  • GDP = market value of all final goods and services produced in the country during a time period.
  • GDP = C + I + G + (X − M) — C = Consumption, I = Investment, G = Government expenditure, X = Exports, M = Imports.
  • Market value = price at which goods/services are sold in the market.
  • Final goods (Tyler Cowen & Alex Tabarrok): goods/services that will be used or consumed. Intermediate goods (இடைநிலை) = used to produce other goods and form part of them.
  • Only final goods counted; counting intermediate goods = double counting. Example: tea ₹10 (final) includes sugar ₹2 (intermediate) – sugar excluded.

1.1 National Income and related terms

  • National Income = total money value of goods and services produced by an economy over a period, normally a year. Also called GNP or National Dividend.
TermMeaning / formula
1. GNP (Gross National Product)Total value of goods & services produced and income received in a year by domestic residents; includes profits from capital invested abroad. GNP = C + I + G + (X−M) + NFIA (Net Factor Income from Abroad)
2. GDPTotal value of output produced by factors of production within the geographical boundaries of the country
3. NNPNNP = GNP − Depreciation
4. NDPPart of GDP; NDP = GDP − Depreciation (wear and tear)
5. Per Capita Income (PCI)Output per person; indicator of living standard. PCI = National Income ÷ Population
6. Personal Income (PI)Total money income received by individuals & households from all sources before direct taxes
7. Disposable Income (DI)Income actually available for consumption. DPI = PI − Direct taxes; consumption approach: DI = Consumption expenditure + Savings
  • Box: In 1867–68 Dadabhai Naoroji first estimated per capita income, in his book "Poverty and Un-British Rule in India".

1.2 Gross Domestic Product – features

  • Produced in the country: apples of Kashmir counted in India's GDP; apples of California sold in India are NOT (California is in the US).
  • Produced during a time period: goods produced in earlier periods not included.
  • Box: Modern concept of GDP first developed by Simon Kuznets for a US Congress report in 1934.

Methods of calculating GDP

MethodHow
1. Expenditure approachAdd expenditure on all final goods & services: Y = C + I + G + (X − M)
2. Income approachEarnings of those producing: Y = wages + rent + interest + profit
3. Value-added approachSum of value added by each intermediate good = value of final good (tea powder + milk + sugar = tea)

Importance of GDP (6): study of economic growth; problems of inflation and deflation; comparison with developed countries; estimate purchasing power; study of public sector; guide to economic planning.

Limitations of GDP (4):

  • Leaves out non-market goods/services – parents' services to children, clean air.
  • Measures quantity, not quality – e.g. ballpoint pens not allowed in schools/banks in the 1970s due to poor quality.
  • Does not show income distribution.
  • Does not tell the kind of life people live (health, democracy, pollution, suicide rate).
  • Estimation of GDP: Central Statistical Organisation (CSO), under the Ministry of Statistics, keeps records; conducts annual survey of industries; compiles Index of Industrial Production (IIP), Consumer Price Index (CPI) etc.

1.5 Economic Growth and Development

  • Amartya Sen: economic growth is one aspect of economic development.
  • UN: development focuses not only on man's materialistic needs but overall rise in living standards.
  • Economic growth – key parameters GDP and GNP (measure actual size of economy).
  • Economic development – broader; output + improved living standard; qualitative (technology, labour reforms, institutional change). HDI = apt tool to measure real development.

  • HDI box: introduced in 1990 by Mahbub ul Haq (Pakistani economist at UN). Composite index of life expectancy at birth, adult literacy rate, standard of living (log of GDP adjusted to PPP).
  • India climbed one spot to 130 out of 189 countries (UNDP). India's HDI value rose from 0.427 (1990) to 0.640 (2017) – nearly 50% increase.

1.6 Developmental path based on GDP and employment

  • India first followed closed trade (protect domestic industry) – continued till 1991, when India opened to free trade and liberalised (foreign companies allowed).
  • Employment generation under Five-Year Plans; rural development; poverty alleviation as corollary of rural development (British rule left a poverty-stricken economy).
  • Public sector given significant importance; private industry under strict regulation; govt seen as sole protector.
  • Per capita incomes doubled in 12 years; India just inside Middle Income Country category.
  • Life expectancy at birth 65 years; 44% of children under 5 malnourished; literacy (15+) only 63% vs 71% for lower-middle-income countries.
  • Factors supporting development: 700 million Indians under 35; demographic transition raised working-age share from 58% to 64% in two decades; strong legal system; many English speakers (IT investment – Bangalore software hub); external economies of scale.

1.7 Growth of GDP and Economic Policies

  • Agriculture policy – govt decisions on domestic agriculture and farm imports. Examples: price policy, land reform policy, Green Revolution, irrigation policy, food policy, agricultural labour policy, co-operative policy.
  • Industrial policy – since 1948; e.g. textile industry policy, sugar industry policy, price policy of industrial growth, small-scale industrial policy, industrial labour policy.
  • New Economic Policy – early 1990s reforms = LPG (Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation).
  • Book exercise: import and export policy = a trade policy.

Gross National Happiness (GNH) box

  • Term coined in 1972 by King of Bhutan Jigme Singye Wangchuck (interview to a British journalist of the Financial Times at Bombay airport): "GNH is more important than GNP".
  • 2011 – UN General Assembly resolution "Happiness: towards a holistic approach to development"; happiness a "fundamental human goal".
  • 4 pillars: sustainable & equitable socio-economic development; environmental conservation; preservation & promotion of culture; good governance.
  • 9 domains: psychological well-being, health, time use, education, cultural diversity & resilience, good governance, community vitality, ecological diversity & resilience, living standards.

Summary / Glossary: Depreciation (தேய்மானம்) – fall in money value of an asset due to use, wear & tear, obsolescence. Income – monetary/other returns, earned or unearned, over a period. Unit 2 – Globalization and Trade

  • LPG = Liberalization, Privatization and Globalization.
  • Globalization (உலகமயமாக்கல்) = integration of a country with the world economy; = process of internationalization plus liberalization.
  • Term "Globalization" introduced by Prof. Theodore Levitt.

2.2 History of Globalization – three stages

StageKey facts

2.4 Globalization in India

  • After 1980–81: severe balance of payments difficulties – oil price hike, Gulf war 1990–91, hostilities in West Asia.
  • New government took over June 1991 – unprecedented BoP crisis; credit rating downgraded → capital flight.
  • Govt mortgaged 40 tons of gold to the Bank of England.
  • Budget 1991–92 presented July 1991 with LPG policy changes = New Economic Policy. Strengthened when India signed the Dunkel Draft in 1994.

Reforms made to adopt globalization (NEP):

  1. Abolition of industrial licensing except for a few industries.
  2. Reduction in number of industries reserved for public sector.
  3. Realistic exchange rate of rupee to encourage exports.
  4. Foreign private sector – rupee convertible on trade / current account; import duties reduced.
  5. Foreign exchange regulations amended.
  6. Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) reduced to increase lending.

2.5 Multinational Corporation (MNC)

  • MNC = corporate organisation which owns or controls production of goods/services in at least one country other than its home country. Also called Transnational Company (TNC) and Multinational Enterprise (MNE).
  • Evolution: like the East India Company; first in extractive industries / raw materials in 1920s, then manufacturing and services after 1950s. Most MNCs belong to USA, UK, France, Germany; largest are American – 11 of the 15 largest MNCs are American.
  • Growth in India: common form = collaboration with Indian industrialists. 12,760 foreign collaboration agreements signed between 1948 and 1988. Liberalised Foreign Investment Policy (FIP) announced in 1991 (book answer: July 1991) → more FDI.
  • Reasons for growth of MNCs (5): expansion of market territory; marketing superiorities; financial superiorities; technological superiorities; product innovations (R&D).
  • Advantages: same quality at lower cost without transaction cost; reduce prices / raise purchasing power; take advantage of tax variation; spur job growth.
  • Disadvantages: monopoly; harm to environment; downfall of small local business; breach of ethical standards.

2.6 Fair Trade Practices and WTO

  • Fair Trade = business that keeps small farmers an active part of the world market and empowers consumers to buy according to their values; = better prices, decent working conditions, fair terms of trade.
  • Aims: raise/stabilise incomes of small farmers, farm workers, artisans; distribute gains/risks equitably; raise capacity of producer groups; promote labour rights and right to organise; safe, sustainable farming.
  • Principles of Fair Trade Organisation: opportunities for economically disadvantaged producers; transparency and accountability; fair trading practices and fair price; no child labour and forced labour; non-discrimination, gender equity, freedom of association; capacity building; respect for environment.

GATT (General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade)

  • Signed by 23 countries in 1947; India a founder member.
  • Director General Arthur Dunkel – Draft Final Act = "Dunkel Draft" (India signed 1994).
  • Purpose: increase international trade by reducing tariffs, quotas and subsidies.

World Trade Organization (WTO)

  • Final Act of Uruguay Round signed April 1994 → WTO; agreement signed by 104 members; WTO came into force 1 January 1995.
  • HQ: Geneva, Switzerland. Purpose: regulation of international trade. Staff: Director General (head), four Deputy Directors General, ~600 official staff from ~80 member countries.
  • Objectives: set and enforce rules for international trade; forum for negotiating/monitoring trade liberalisation; resolve trade disputes; sustainable development with environment; better share of world trade for developing countries; transparency of decision making; full employment and effective demand.
  • TRIPs (Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights): IPR = "information with a commercial value"; patents for any invention (product or process) in all fields; TRIPs covers seven areas of IPR.

2.7 Impact and Challenges of Globalization

  • Positive: rapid development of capital market; higher standard of living; more trade and employment; new technologies and research; increases GDP; free flow of goods and more FDI.
  • Negative: unfair income distribution; loss of national integrity / independent domestic policies; ecological and social costs; exploitation of natural resources for foreign exchange; relaxed environmental standards.
  • Challenges: benefits not automatic for all; instability in developing world; "race to the bottom" in wages and labour rights; global imbalance; child labour and slavery; junk food → health degradation; environmental degradation.
  • Glossary: archaic (தொன்மையான), evolution (பரிணாம வளர்ச்சி), mortgaged (அடமானம் வைக்கப்பட்ட), spurt (திடீர்), detrimental (சீரழிவான), thriving (வெற்றிகரமான).
  • Book match: MNC in India – Infosys; MNC – minimise cost of production; GATT – 1947; WTO – enforce international trade.

Unit 3 – Food Security and Nutrition

  • Food = any substance people eat and drink to maintain life and growth.
  • Food security (FAO, 2009): "exists when all people, at all times, have physical, social and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food which meets their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life."
  • Nutrition security (Dr. M.S. Swaminathan): physical, economic and social access to a balanced diet, safe drinking water, environmental hygiene, primary health care and primary education.
  • Three basic components: (1) Availability – physical stocks = domestic production + changes in stocks + imports; (2) Access – mainly purchasing power (employment, assets, education); (3) Absorption – ability to biologically utilise food consumed.

3.2 Availability and Access to Food Grains

  • After Independence – planned development model; after initial focus on agriculture, industrialisation got priority; recurrent droughts → food imports.
  • USA gave food aid through Public Law 480 (PL 480) in the early 1960s.
  • American administration and Ford Foundation → High Yielding Varieties (HYV) of wheat and rice in select irrigated districts → Green Revolution → self-sufficiency in food grains.
  • Early 1950s: area ~98 million ha, production 54 million tonnes, yield 547 kg/ha. Now: area 122 million ha; production five-fold; yield four-fold (over ~65 years).
  • HYV package: fertiliser-responsive seeds + subsidised chemical fertilisers + cheap credit via co-operative banks + MSP announced at start of season + procurement by Food Corporation of India (FCI), which built godowns and buffer stocks.
  • Independence → mid-2000s: milk 8-fold, eggs 40-fold, fish 13-fold.
  • India not self-sufficient in pulses and oilseeds – imports them.

Minimum Support Price (MSP): price fixed by an expert group for a crop considering cultivation costs; state opens procurement centres; farmers free to sell in open market if price is better; if market price < MSP, farmers get MSP by selling to FCI. Declared every year before the sowing season.

Public Distribution System (PDS)

  • Tamil Nadu – "Universal" PDS (all family ration card holders entitled); rest of India – "Targeted" PDS (beneficiaries identified by criteria). Book: TN is the only state with universal PDS.
  • National Food Security Act (NFSA) 2013 – covers 50% urban and 75% rural households ("priority households").
  • NFSA implemented in Tamil Nadu on 1 November 2016, after holding out for three years.

Consumer cooperatives: three-tier – primary consumer cooperative societies, central consumer cooperative stores, state-level consumer federations. >50,000 village-level societies distribute goods. In TN about 94% of fair price shops are run by cooperatives.

Buffer stock: stock of food grains (wheat and rice) procured by the government through FCI from surplus states at MSP and stored in granaries.

3.3 Purchasing Power

  • Purchasing power = value of a currency in terms of goods/services one unit of money can buy. Price ↑ → purchasing power ↓ and vice versa.
  • Factors affecting purchasing power (6): over-population (growth rate "1.7 per 1000" as printed); rising prices of essential goods; demand for goods; price of goods affects value of currency; production and supply of goods; poverty and inequality (share of top 10% rising).
  • Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) – theory estimating adjustment needed to the price of an item; used to compare countries' incomes, cost of living, inflation/deflation. India = 3rd largest economy by PPP; China largest, US second.
  • Chart – largest economies by PPP-GDP 2019 (Int $ trillion): China 27.4, US 21.4, India 11.4, Japan 5.8, Russia 4.5, Germany 4.3.

3.4 Agricultural Policy in India

  • New agricultural policy based on export of agricultural goods announced by the Centre in 2018 – removes export restrictions on most organic and processed agricultural products.
  • Agricultural policy = govt design to raise agricultural production, productivity, farmers' income and living standard in a time frame.
  • Objectives (6): (1) raise productivity of inputs (HYV seeds, fertilisers, pesticides, irrigation); (2) raise value-added per hectare (esp. small/marginal holdings); (3) protect poor farmers – abolish intermediaries via land reforms, institutional credit; (4) modernise agriculture; (5) check environmental degradation; (6) remove bureaucratic obstacles on farmers' cooperatives and self-help institutions.

3.5 Multi-dimensional Nature of Poverty

  • Multi-dimensional Poverty Index (MPI) launched in 2010 by UNDP and OPHI (Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative). Poverty is multi-dimensional, not uni-dimensional.
  • Factors: health, education, living standards, income, disempowerment, quality of work, threat of violence. (Figure indicators: child & adolescent mortality, antenatal care, drinking water, bank account, housing.)
  • MPI 2022–23 report – Tamil Nadu: districts classed as high-poverty (>40% BPL), moderately poor (30–40%), low poverty (<30%).
  • Poverty declined steadily in TN after 1994; reduction faster than many states after 2005; TN led in poverty alleviation programmes during 2014–2017.

3.6 Nutrition and Health Status

India (National Family Health Survey, 2015–16):

  • Undernourished / chronically energy-deficient women (15–49 yrs): 27% rural, 16% urban.
  • More than half of women 15–49 anaemic.
  • Anaemic children (6–59 months): 60% rural, 56% urban.
  • Stunted (low height for age): 41% rural, 31% urban children.
  • Underweight (low weight for age): about 20% of children (6–59 months).
  • Schemes: ICDS, mid-day meals, RCH (Reproductive and Child Health), NRHM (National Rural Health Mission).

Tamil Nadu

  • TN's budget outlays for nutrition and health are the highest in the country. ICDS and Puratchi Thalaivar MGR Nutritious Meal Programme (PTMGRNMP) among the best.
  • Policy: "A Malnutrition Free Tamil Nadu" – goal "reducing human malnutrition of all types to the levels of best performing countries".
  • ICDS in TN: 54,439 Child Centres (49,499 Anganwadi + 4,940 Mini Anganwadi) in 434 Child Development Blocks (385 rural, 47 urban, 2 tribal). ICDS = one of the world's largest programmes of its kind.
  • PTMGRNMP = largest noon meal programme in the country; other states modelled theirs on it.

Important ongoing schemes in TN

  1. Dr. Muthulakshmi Reddy Maternity Benefit Scheme – ₹12,000 to poor pregnant women.
  2. Chief Minister's Comprehensive Health Insurance Scheme – launched 2011–12; universal healthcare, free medical and surgical treatment.
  3. Tamil Nadu Health Systems Project (TNHSP) – free ambulance – 108 Emergency Ambulance Service.
  4. School Health Programme – healthcare for students of Govt and Govt-aided schools.
  5. National Leprosy Eradication Programme.

Some nutrition programmes in TN: Puratchi Thalaivar MGR Nutrition Meal Programme; National Programme of Nutritional Support to Primary Education; General ICDS projects and World Bank-assisted ICDS; Pradhan Mantri Gramodaya Yojana (PMGYS); Tamil Nadu Integrated Nutrition Programme; Mid-Day Meal Programme.

C10 Unit 4 – Government and Taxes: Introduction

  • Tax is levied by government for development of the state's economy. Revenue depends on direct taxes (on income of persons) and indirect taxes (on goods and services) – the govt's "Financial Sources".
  • Three levels of government in India: Union, State and Local.

4.1 Role of Government in Development Policies (7)

  1. Defence – Union Government creates and maintains defence forces.
  2. Foreign policy – friendly relations, exports/imports, investment and labour flows – Union Government.
  3. Conduct of periodic elections – Parliament and state assemblies; state governments conduct local body elections.
  4. Law and order – Union and States enact laws; Union has the judicial system; States administer the police.
  5. Public administration and public goods – departments (revenue, schools, hospitals, rural/urban development); local governments give civic facilities (local roads, drainage, drinking water, waste disposal).
  6. Redistribution of income and poverty alleviation – high-income people pay more tax; spending on basic needs of the poor.
  7. Regulate the economy – Union Govt through RBI controls money supply, interest rate, inflation, foreign exchange rate; also via SEBI and Competition Commission of India; public sector enterprises.

4.2 Tax

  • Word "tax" comes from "taxation" meaning an estimate.
  • Taxation = means by which governments finance expenditure by imposing charges on citizens and corporate entities. Tax came to the forefront due to the concept of the "welfare state".
  • Taxes = compulsory payments to government without expectation of direct return.
  • Prof. Seligman: tax is "a compulsory contribution from a person to the government to defray the expenses incurred in the common interest of all, without reference to special benefits conferred".
  • Tax system = collection of a variety of taxes. Canons of taxation listed by economists from Adam Smith onwards.

  • Taxes collected by all three tiers: almost all direct taxes – Union; taxes on goods & services – Union and States; taxes on property – local governments.
  • India collects more revenue through indirect taxes than direct taxes. Major indirect taxes: customs duty and GST.

4.3 Types of Taxes

  • Direct tax – paid directly; burden cannot be shifted. J.S. Mill: "one which is demanded from the very persons who it is intended or desired should pay it." E.g. income tax, wealth tax, corporation tax.
  • Box: Income Tax first introduced in India in 1860 by Sir James Wilson to meet losses from the Mutiny of 1857.
  • Indirect tax – burden can be shifted; impact on one person, incidence on another; taxpayer is not the tax bearer. E.g. stamp duty, entertainment tax, excise duty, GST.
  • Stamp duty – on official documents (marriage registration, property documents, some contracts).
  • Entertainment tax – on movie tickets, amusement parks, exhibitions, sports events.
  • Excise duty – on manufactured goods at the moment of manufacture, not at sale.
  • Box: France was the first country to implement GST, in 1954.

Goods and Services Tax (GST)

  • Passed in Parliament on 29 March 2017; came into effect 1 July 2017. Motto: "One nation, one market, one tax".
  • SGST (intra-state) – replaced VAT/sales tax, purchase tax, entertainment tax, luxury tax, lottery tax, state surcharges and cesses.
  • CGST (intra-state) – replaced central excise duty, service tax, countervailing duty, additional customs duty, surcharge, education and secondary/higher secondary cess.
  • IGST – inter-state.
  • Four major rates: 5%, 12%, 18%, 28%; necessities like vegetables and food grains exempted.
  • Book exercise: GST is a "one point tax"; it unifies the tax structure (it replaces indirect taxes, not direct taxes).

4.4 How are taxes levied?

  • Progressive tax (வளர்வீத வரி) – rate rises as the tax base (income) rises. Example: ₹10,000 @10% = ₹1,000; ₹20,000 @15% = ₹3,000; ₹30,000 @25% = ₹7,500. E.g. Income tax.
  • Proportionate tax (ஒரேவீத வரி) – fixed rate; all pay the same proportion. Example: 10% on ₹10,000/20,000/30,000 = ₹1,000/2,000/3,000. E.g. Corporate tax.
  • Regressive tax (குறைவுவீத வரி) – higher rate on lower income groups; opposite of progressive. E.g. Sales tax.
  • Note: the book's small chart (p. 331) prints "Tax decreases" under Proportional and "Same tax always" under Regressive – the columns are mixed up; by definition proportional = same rate always, regressive = rate falls as income rises.

4.5 Black Money · 4.6 Tax Evasion

  • Black money = funds earned on the black market on which taxes are not paid; unaccounted money concealed from the tax administrator.
  • Causes (5): shortage of goods; licensing proceedings; contribution of industrial sector; smuggling; tax structure (book MCQ adds high tax rate).
  • Tax evasion = illegal evasion of taxes by individuals, corporations, trusts – under-reporting income, inflating deductions/expenses, hiding money, hiding interest in offshore accounts.
  • Penalties: felony charges; imprisonment up to five years and heavy fines; may pay costs of prosecution.

4.7 Taxes and Development

  1. Resource mobilisation – direct (personal income tax, corporate tax) and indirect (customs, excise) taxes.
  2. Reduction of income inequalities – direct taxes progressive; taxes on luxury goods progressive.
  3. Social welfare – higher taxes on undesirable products like alcohol.
  4. Foreign exchange – encourages exports, restricts imports; export items generally not taxed.
  5. Regional development – tax holidays for industries in backward regions.
  6. Control of inflation – by reducing tax on commodities.

C10 Unit 5 – Industrial Clusters in Tamil Nadu: Introduction

  • Industry = "any human activity which is engaged in the conversion of raw materials into readily usable materials".
  • Industrialisation = using modern techniques of production to produce goods on a large scale for consumers and producers.

5.1 Importance of Industrialisation

  • Why agriculture's share falls with development: (1) demand for food stays constant as income rises; (2) food is transported, processed, branded → farmers get much less than consumer price; (3) declining marginal productivity of land limits labour absorption → low wages, poverty.
  • Benefits of industrialisation (6): inputs to other producers (fertilisers, tractors for agriculture); market for producer and consumer goods (banking, transport, trade depend on it); higher productivity → lower costs; absorbs labour from agriculture (employment generation); technological change → higher labour productivity and wages; expanding incomes → more demand.

5.2 Types of Industries

5.3 Industrial Clusters

  • Industrial clusters = groups of firms in a defined geographic area sharing common markets, technologies and skill requirements.
  • Advantages first observed by Alfred Marshall in the 1920s (metal-working and textile regions of England); notion of "industrial district" by Marshall; popular after success of small firms in Italy in the 1980s.
  • Characteristics of a successful cluster: geographical proximity of SMEs; sectoral specialisation; close inter-firm collaboration; inter-firm competition based on innovation; socio-cultural identity (trust); multi-skilled workforce; active self-help organisations; supportive regional and municipal governments. Firms collaborate and compete at the same time.
  • How clusters originate: artisans settling over centuries (e.g. handloom weaving); around a large firm (input/service suppliers); government encouraging use of regional raw materials.

5.4 Historical Development of Industrialisation in TN

  • Pre-colonial: textiles, ship-building, iron and steel making, pottery; trade with South-East and West Asia. Colonial policy → decline of handloom (machine-made imports from England).
  • Colonial period: (1) cotton cultivation in Western and Southern TN → large textile sector; (2) trade → industry around ports Chennai and Tuticorin. Match factories in Sivakasi (later fireworks and printing). Leather in Dindigul, Vellore, Ambur. Western TN: textiles → textile machinery → workshops. Hydro-electric power in 1930s → oil engines for groundwater → foundries, agricultural machinery.
  • Post-Independence to early 1990s: Integral Coach Factory, Chennai (railway coaches); BHEL, Tiruchirappalli (boilers, turbines) → cluster of small firms; Heavy Vehicles Factory, Avadi (tanks); Standard Motors (cars, Chennai); Ashok Motors (later Ashok Leyland) + Standard Motors → Chennai auto cluster; Avadi industrial estate – 1950s; Salem Steel Plant – 1973 (stainless steel).
  • 1970s–80s: powerloom clusters in Coimbatore; cotton knitwear in Tiruppur; home furnishings in Karur; state industrial estates; Hosur = successful case of industrial estates developing a backward region.
  • Liberalisation phase (since early 1990s): states compete for private investment – cheap land, tax concessions, subsidised quality power; trade liberalisation and currency devaluation opened export markets.
  • Older industries: sugar, fertilisers, cement, agricultural implements, iron and steel, chemicals, transformers, paper.
  • TN today: largest number of factories among all states and largest share of workforce in manufacturing; more labour-intensive than Maharashtra and Gujarat; industries spread across regions – 27 clusters in 13 districts.

C9 Unit 1 – Understanding Development: Perspectives, Measurement and Sustainability

History of metallic money (box, p. 235)

  • Per historian Herodotus, King Midas of Lydia innovated metal coin in the 8th century BCE; but gold coins were in use in India centuries earlier. Earliest coin issuers: ancient Indians, Chinese and Lydians.
  • First Indian coins minted in the 6th century BCE by the Mahajanapadas – called Puranas, Karshapanas or Panas.
  • Mauryas – punch-marked coins (silver, gold, copper, lead). Indo-Greek Kushan kings – Greek custom of engraving portraits on coins.
  • Turkish Sultans of Delhi (by 12th century CE) replaced royal designs with Islamic calligraphy; coins Tanka (gold, silver, copper) and lower-value Jittals.
  • Mughal Empire from 1526 consolidated the monetary system. Sher Shah Suri defeated Humayun and issued silver Rupiya ("178 gms" as printed) divided into 40 copper pieces (paisa).

  • Value of money = purchasing power over goods and services; inverse relation with price level. Two types: internal value (over domestic goods) and external value (over foreign goods).
  • Inflation – prices rise, value of money falls; Deflation – prices fall, value of money rises.
  • Nature of money: Scitovsky – money hard to define as it fulfils three functions (unit of account, medium of exchange, store of value). Sir John Hicks – "Money is what money does" (functional definition). Legal definition: "anything which state declares as money is money" (legal tender).

  • 1961 = "Year of Population Explosion" (growth ~1.96% ≈ 2%).
  • 2001 – population crossed one billion (100 crore). 2011 census – growth of youth = "demographic transition".
  • Crude birth rate = births per 1000 population; crude death rate = deaths per 1000. CBR: 39.9 (1951) → 25.4 (2001) → 21.8 (2011). CDR: 27.4 (1951) → 8.4 (2001) → 7.1 (2011). Fall in birth rate less than fall in death rate.
  • Lowest birth rate Kerala (14.7); highest UP (29.5). Lowest death rate West Bengal (6.3); highest Odisha (9.2). Highest decadal growth (2001–11) Bihar; lowest Kerala. BIMARU = Bihar, MP, Rajasthan, UP.
  • Density = total population ÷ land area (man–land ratio): <100 before Independence; 117 (1951), 325 (2001), 382 (2011). Highest: Bihar 1,102, then West Bengal 880; lowest Arunachal Pradesh 17. Kerala, WB, Bihar, UP above national average.
  • Sex ratio (females per 1000 males): 946 (1951), 933 (2001), 940 (2011). Highest Kerala 1084; lowest Haryana 877.
  • Life expectancy: 1901–11 just 23 years; 1951 32.1 (M 32.5, F 31.7); 1991 58.7; 2001 62.5; 2011 63.5 (M 62.6, F 64.2).
  • Literacy: 1951 18.3% (M 27.2, F 8.9) – about one-sixth; 2001 64.8%; 2011 74.04% (M 82.1, F 65.5). Highest Kerala 94%, Mizoram 91.3, Goa 88.7, HP 82.8, Maharashtra 82.3, Sikkim 81.4, Tamil Nadu 80.1; lowest Bihar 61.8%.

7.4 Natural Resources

  • Natural resource = any stock or reserve drawn from nature – land, forest, water, minerals, energy. India rich in resources but most Indians poor.
  • Renewable (regenerated): forests, wildlife, wind, biomass, tidal, hydro. Non-renewable: fossil fuels – coal, petroleum, minerals.
  • Land: India 7th in area – 32.8 lakh sq km = 2.42% of world area; land–man ratio unfavourable. Agricultural Census: area under large holdings (≥10 ha) declined, marginal holdings (<1 ha) increased – fragmentation.
  • Forests (2007): 69.09 million ha = 21.02% of area; very dense 8.35, moderately dense 31.90, open 28.84 million ha.

7.5–7.6 Infrastructure and Economic Infrastructure

  • Economic infrastructure – transport, communication, energy, irrigation, monetary and financial institutions (helps production and distribution). Social infrastructure – education, training, research, health, housing, civic amenities.
  • Transport: roads over 30 lakh km (one of the largest networks); railways largest in Asia, 4th largest in world – route length ~63,000 km, 13,000 km electrified. Major ports (Kolkata, Mumbai, Chennai, Visakhapatnam, Goa) handle ~90% of sea-borne trade. Domestic air – Indian Airlines and private; international – Air India.
  • Boxes: first railway Wi-Fi at Bangalore station; Air India + Indian Airlines merged 27 August 2007 → NACIL (National Aviation Company of India Ltd); National Harbour Board – 1950 (advice on ports, esp. minor ports).
  • Energy: non-renewable (coal, oil, gas) and renewable/non-conventional (solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, biomass).

7.7 Social Infrastructure

  • Improves quality of manpower; lack of it → low productivity of Indian workers.
  • Education: dates to Gurukul; MHRD formulates policy. Education was a State subject until 1976, then moved to the Concurrent List. Six levels: nursery, primary, secondary, higher secondary, graduation, post-graduation; 10+2 pattern; university accreditation under UGC Act; education budget ~3% of GDP, largest share to school education but lowest per-pupil spending for school students.
  • Health: a State responsibility; Central Council of Health and Welfare formulates projects; Ministry of Health handles administration. Systems: Siddha, Ayurveda, Unani, Homeopathy, Allopathy, Yoga – all under AYUSH. Kerala has the best health status; India's status poorer than Sri Lanka.

7.8 Contributions of Indian Economic Thinkers

7.8.1 Thiruvalluvar – economic ideas in Thirukkural (book of ethics), mainly in Porutpal (wealth); Sangam age ~3rd century CE; "pragmatic idealism".

  • Rain = basic support for life; "It is rain that both ruins and aids the ruined to rise".
  • Factors of production – Kural 61: unfailing harvest, competent men and undiminishing wealth are components of an economy.
  • Agriculture most fundamental – Kural 1032: ploughmen are the linch-pin; "the rest are mere slaves that follow on their toil".
  • Public finance: creation, collection, management of revenue, public expenditure. Recommended a balanced budget – "Budget for a surplus, if possible, balance the budget at other times, but never budget for a deficit." Main expenditure: defence, public works, social services.
  • External assistance – against it; Kural 739: countries relying on external help are not countries at all (self-sufficiency).
  • Poverty = root of all evils; freedom from hunger is fundamental. Wealth only a means; condemned hoarding; industry is real wealth, labour the greatest resource.
  • Welfare state elements: health without disease, abundant wealth, good crop, prosperity and happiness, full security.

7.8.2 Mahatma Gandhi – Gandhian economics based on ethical foundations. 1921: "Economics that hurts the moral well-being of an individual or a nation is immoral, and therefore, sinful." 1924: "that economy is untrue which ignores or disregards moral values".

  1. Village republics – self-sufficient villages; opposed extensive machinery, urbanisation, industrialisation.
  2. Machinery = "Great sin".
  3. Industrialism = a curse on mankind (depends on capacity to exploit).
  4. Decentralisation – small-scale production in homes.
  5. Village Sarvodaya – "Real India was to be found in villages".
  6. Bread labour (body labour) – dignity of manual labour.
  7. Doctrine of Trusteeship – transform capitalist order into egalitarian one (glossary: donors acting as trustees of their property).
  8. Food problem – against food controls (create artificial scarcity).
  9. Population – against contraceptives; favoured Brahmacharya/self-control.
  10. Prohibition – cent per cent; liquor a disease rather than a vice.

7.8.3 Jawaharlal Nehru – first PM till death in 1964.

  • Democracy and secularism (equal respect for all religions).
  • Planning – introduced planning; on the Second Plan in Lok Sabha (May 1956): "the essence of planning is to find the best way to utilize all resources of manpower, of money and so on"; linked to industrialisation and self-reliance; IITs and research institutes; "scientific temper". Book MCQ: Nehru introduced the National Development Council.
  • Democratic socialism – "socialistic pattern of society".

7.8.4 B.R. Ambedkar (1891–1956) – architect of the Constitution; champion of socialism and state planning.

  • Works: "Ancient Indian Commerce" (MA thesis, Columbia University, 1915); "National Dividend of India: A Historical and Analytical Study" (PhD) – published as "The Evolution of Provincial Finance in British India"; "Provincial Decentralisation of Imperial Finance in British India" – M.Sc 1921; "The Problem of the Rupee" – D.Sc, London School of Economics, 1923 → RBI conceptualised on its guidelines.
  • Financial economics (1913–23) – provincial finance stages: Budget by Assignment (1871–72 to 1876–77); Budget by Assigned Revenue (1877–78 to 1881–82); Budget by Shared Revenues (1882–83 to 1920–21).
  • Agricultural economics – 1918 paper "Small Holdings in India and their Remedies"; citing Adam Smith, distinguished "consolidation" vs "enlargement" of holdings.
  • Economics of caste – caste obstructs mobility; free choice of occupation; no social democracy.
  • Socialism – state socialism; nationalise key industries; state ownership of land, collective farming; state monopoly of insurance, compulsory insurance for every citizen.

7.8.5 J.C. Kumarappa – Joseph Chelladurai Kumarappa, born 4 January 1892, Thanjavur.

  • Coined "Gandhian Economics"; combined Christian and Gandhian values (trusteeship, non-violence, human dignity); rejected class war and materialism.
  • Professor at Gujarat Vidyapith, Ahmedabad; editor of Young India during Salt Satyagraha; founded All India Village Industries Association, 1935; jailed during Quit India; wrote "Economy of Permanence" and "Practice and Precepts of Jesus" (1945), "Christianity: Its Economy and Way of Life" (1945).
  • Ramachandra Guha called him "The Green Gandhian" – founder of modern environmentalism in India; worked for Planning Commission and Congress on agriculture/rural policy.

7.8.6 V.K.R.V. Rao

  • P.R. Brahmananda: great trinity of Indian economists = D.R. Gadgil, C.N. Vakil, V.K.R.V. Rao.
  • Themes: national income; food, nutrition and distribution; employment and occupational distribution.
  • National income methodology – pioneer; pupil of J.M. Keynes, worked with Colin Clark; H.W. Singer: "the best equipped of all Keynes' pupils". Paper "Full Employment and Economic Development".
  • International food aid; support for socialism (state controls commanding heights).
  • Pamphlet "What is wrong with Indian Economic Life?" (1938) – reasons for low per capita income: uneconomic fragmented holdings; low water availability; excess population pressure on agriculture (no large industry); absence of capital; no autonomy in currency policy (gold hoarding).
  • Village clusters as units of rural development.
  • "Interrelation between Investment, Income and Multiplier in an Underdeveloped Economy" (1952) – major macro contribution.
  • Institution builder: Delhi School of Economics, Institute of Economic Growth (both Delhi), Institute for Social and Economic Change (Bangalore).

7.8.7 Amartya Kumar Sen – Nobel Prize 1998 for social choice theory, development economics, poverty and famines, entitlements and capability.

  • "Poverty and Famines: An Essay on Entitlement and Deprivation" (1981) – absolute and relative deprivation.
  • Poverty and inequality – distribution among the poor below the poverty line matters.
  • Capability – ability to transform Rawlsian primary goods into well-being; better index than commodities or utilities.
  • Entitlement – famine arises from failure of entitlements (nutrition, food, health, employment, food security).
  • "Choice of Technique" – in a labour-surplus economy, employment cannot be raised initially by capital-intensive techniques.
  • An ethical philosopher, humanist; the poor need empowerment, not charity.

Glossary: economic growth (measured by GDP); economic development (measured by per capita income + other indicators); per capita income = national income ÷ population; deforestation; energy crisis (energy supply < demand). Book MCQ key: Kolar – Karnataka gold; France – developed nation; India's position – seventh; 1961 – population explosion; Kumarappa – Gandhian economics; Rao – student of Keynes.
