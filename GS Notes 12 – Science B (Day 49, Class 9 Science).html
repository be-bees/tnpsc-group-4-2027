Physics · Unit 1 – Measurement (PDF p. 5–17)

  • Measurement = determination of the size or magnitude of a quantity.
  • Physical quantity = a quantity that can be measured; has a numerical value + unit (e.g. 3 kilogram).
  • Fundamental quantities cannot be expressed in terms of other quantities – length, mass, time, temperature etc. Derived quantities are expressed through fundamental ones – area, volume, density etc.
  • Unit = standard quantity with which unknown quantities are compared (adopted by law or convention).
  • Earlier unit systems: CGS (centimetre–gram–second), FPS (foot–pound–second), MKS (metre–kilogram–second).
  • SI system – recommended by the General Conference on Weights and Measures, Paris, 1960 (need felt after World War II). Has 7 fundamental (base) units.

  • Metre = distance travelled by light in vacuum in 1/299,792,458 second.
  • Large distance units: Astronomical unit (AU) = mean distance between centre of Sun and centre of Earth = 1.496 × 10¹¹ m; Light year = distance light travels in vacuum in one year = 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m; Parsec (for objects outside the solar system) = 3.26 light years = 3.08 × 10¹⁶ m.
  • Alpha Centauri (nearest star) ≈ 1.34 parsec from the Sun; most naked-eye stars are within 500 parsec.
  • Small units: fermi = 10⁻¹⁵ m; angstrom (Å) = 10⁻¹⁰ m (fermi and angstrom are outside SI but accepted); nanometre = 10⁻⁹ m; micron (μm) = 10⁻⁶ m; mm = 10⁻³ m; cm = 10⁻² m. Atomic distances, nucleus size, wavelength → angstrom.
  • Mass = quantity of matter in a body; SI unit kilogram = mass of the international prototype platinum–iridium cylinder kept at the International Bureau of Weights and Measures, Sevres, France.
  • 1 g = 0.001 kg; 1 mg = 0.000001 kg; 1 quintal = 100 kg; 1 metric tonne = 1000 kg = 10 quintal.
  • Atomic mass unit (amu) = 1/12 of the mass of a C-12 atom (used for proton, neutron, electron masses).
  • More to know: mass of 1 ml water = 1 g; 1 litre water = 1 kg. 1 ml = 1 cm³; SI unit of volume = m³ (commonly litre).
  • Second = time for light to travel 299,792,458 m in vacuum; also 1/86,400 of a mean solar day; glossary: time of 9,192,631,770 vibrations of the caesium-133 atom. 1 millennium = 3.16 × 10¹⁰ s (book prints 3.16 × 10⁹ s).
  • Temperature = measure of hotness/coldness; SI unit kelvin = 1/273.16 of thermodynamic temperature of the triple point of water (saturated vapour, pure water and melting ice in equilibrium). 0 K = absolute zero. Other units: °C, °F.
  • Mole = amount of substance with as many elementary units as atoms in 0.012 kg of carbon-12. Ampere = current producing a specified force between two parallel wires 1 m apart in vacuum. Candela = intensity of light source of specified frequency giving specified power in a given direction.
  • Unit prefixes: peta 10¹⁵ (P), tera 10¹² (T), giga 10⁹ (G), mega 10⁶ (M), kilo 10³ (k), hecto 10² (h), deca 10¹ (da), deci 10⁻¹ (d), centi 10⁻² (c), milli 10⁻³ (m), micro 10⁻⁶ (µ), nano 10⁻⁹ (n), pico 10⁻¹² (p), femto 10⁻¹⁵ (f).
  • Range in nature: nucleus diameter 10⁻¹⁵ m to distance between stars 10²⁶ m; electron mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg to Milky Way mass 2.2 × 10⁴¹ kg.
  • Rules for SI units: unit names after scientists in small letters (newton, ampere, watt, henry) but their symbols capital (N, A, W, H); symbols not from proper nouns in small letters (m, kg); no full stop after symbol; no plural (10 kg not kgs); no degree sign with kelvin (283 K) but °C/°F need it; only one solidus (m/s or m s⁻¹; J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹); space between number and unit; no non-standard symbols (not "amp", "sec"); values in scientific form (density of mercury 1.36 × 10⁴ kg m⁻³).
  • Least count = smallest length an instrument can measure. Metre scale 1 mm; Vernier caliper 0.1 mm (0.01 cm); screw gauge 0.01 mm.
  • Vernier caliper: main scale (cm, mm), fixed jaws + sliding Vernier scale (10 divisions). Lower jaws → external dimensions, upper jaws → internal dimensions, thin bar (depth probe) → depth. LC = 1 MSD / no. of vernier divisions = 1 mm/10 = 0.1 mm.
  • Zero error (Vernier): vernier zero to the right of main-scale zero → positive error (correction negative); to the left → negative error (correction positive). Correct reading = MSR + (VC × LC) − (zero error). Digital Vernier caliper shows the value directly.
  • Screw gauge: U-shaped frame, pitch scale (mm), head scale (thimble) with 100 divisions, spindle, anvil (stud), ratchet (safety device against undue pressure). Measures thin wire diameter, thin sheet thickness.
  • Screw gauge principle: distance moved by the screw tip in a nut ∝ number of rotations. Pitch = distance moved per full rotation (typically 1 mm). LC = pitch/no. of head-scale divisions = 1/100 = 0.01 mm.
  • Screw gauge zero error: head-scale zero below pitch-scale axis → positive, ZE = +(n × LC); above → negative, ZE = −(100 − n) × LC.
  • Balances: common (beam) balance – accurate up to 5 g; physical balance (labs) – correct to a milligram (accuracy given as 10 mg); digital balance – least value 10 mg (jewellery shops, labs); spring balance – measures weight, works on Hooke's law (extension ∝ added weight).
  • Gold bought in g/mg, medicines in mg, cargo in tonnes. Egg shell = 12% of egg's mass; a blue whale can weigh as much as 30 elephants.
  • Weight w = mg (g = 9.8 m s⁻²): 50 kg man → 490 N. Moon's gravity is 1/6 of Earth's; g on Moon = 1.63 m s⁻²; 70 kg man: 686 N on Earth, 114 N on Moon, mass still 70 kg.

Physics · Unit 2 – Motion (PDF p. 18–29)

  • Motion = change in position of an object with respect to its surroundings. Described by distance, speed, acceleration, time.
  • Rest and motion: objects not changing position are at rest (book on table, walls); changing position → in motion. Motion is relative – roadside trees appear to move backward for a person in a car, but are at rest for a person on the road.
  • Types of motion: linear (along a straight line), circular (along a circular path), oscillatory (repetitive to-and-fro at regular intervals, e.g. swing), random (none of these).
  • Uniform motion = equal distances in equal intervals of time (car 60 km every hour). Non-uniform motion = unequal distances in equal intervals (bus in traffic).
  • Distance = actual length of path travelled, irrespective of direction; scalar; unit metre. Displacement = change in position in a particular direction (shortest straight line from start to end); vector; unit metre. Displacement can be zero when distance is not.
  • Speed = rate of change of distance = distance/time; scalar; SI unit m s⁻¹. Average speed = total distance / total time (16 m in 4 s + 16 m in 2 s → 32/6 = 5.33 m s⁻¹).
  • Lightning example: thunder heard 5 s after flash, speed of sound 346 m s⁻¹ → distance = 1730 m.
  • Velocity = rate of change of displacement; vector; m s⁻¹.
  • Acceleration = rate of change of velocity, a = (v − u)/t; vector; SI unit m s⁻². If v < u → negative acceleration = retardation / deceleration (a = −2 m s⁻² means deceleration 2 m s⁻²).

Graphs

  • Distance–time graph, uniform motion → straight line; slope = speed; steeper slope = greater speed (walking < cycling < car).
  • Distance–time graph, non-uniform (accelerated) motion → curve (not a straight line).
  • Velocity–time graph, uniform velocity → straight line parallel to X-axis. Area under v–t graph = displacement (distance).
  • Uniformly accelerated motion → v–t graph is a straight line (inclined); distance = area of trapezium = ½ × (sum of parallel sides) × distance between them. Non-uniform acceleration → graphs of any shape.
  • Speedometer measures instantaneous speed. In uniform motion in one dimension, average velocity = instantaneous velocity.

Physics · Unit 3 – Fluids (PDF p. 30–42)

  • Pressure on a solid → tension, deforms/breaks; pressure on a fluid → it flows. Gases are easy to compress; liquids are incompressible.
  • Thrust = force acting perpendicular to a surface (force which produces compression); SI unit newton (N).
  • Pressure = thrust per unit area, P = F/A; SI unit N m⁻² = pascal (Pa), after French scientist Blaise Pascal. Same force on larger area → lower pressure.
  • Examples of area effect: pin pushed by pointed end; camel runs easily in desert; trucks/buses have wide tyres; cutting tools have sharp edges; lying on a bed of nails is safe (large contact area); wide foundations of tall buildings (less pressure on ground).
  • Problem: 90 kg man, feet area 0.036 m², g = 10 → P = 900/0.036 = 25,000 Pa.
  • Fluids = all flowing substances (liquids + gases). Fluid pressure acts in all directions, at all points (molecules in constant rapid motion). Gas pressure arises from collisions of molecules.
  • Liquid pressure: force always perpendicular to the surface; acts upward (air-filled balloon rises), laterally (water squirts from side holes) and on the bottom (balloon tied at pipe bottom bulges).
  • Factors determining liquid pressure: (i) depth h, (ii) density ρ, (iii) acceleration due to gravity g. Same at a given depth in all directions; water squirts with more pressure than oil (denser).
  • Pressure due to liquid column: P = hρg – does not depend on area of cross-section. Water column 0.85 m → 8500 Pa; kerosene (800 kg m⁻³) same height → 6800 Pa.

Atmospheric pressure

  • Atmosphere = layer of air around Earth up to nearly 300 km. Air pressure normally referred to = pressure at sea level. Pressure decreases with altitude (shorter air column); higher in mines.
  • Human lung adapted to sea-level pressure 101.3 kPa; mountain climbers need oxygen cylinders; miners need special equipment.
  • Barometer measures atmospheric pressure; mercury barometer first designed by Italian physicist Torricelli – glass tube filled with mercury inverted in a mercury trough; space above mercury is a vacuum (Torricellian vacuum, extra). Sea level column = 760 mm (76 cm) of mercury.
  • 1 atm = 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa = 101.3 kPa = pressure of 76 cm vertical mercury column (density of mercury 13,600 kg m⁻³); 1 bar = 10⁵ Pa → 1 atm = 1.013 bar.
  • Absolute pressure is zero-referenced to a perfect vacuum; gauge pressure is zero-referenced to atmospheric pressure (tyre pressure, blood pressure gauges). Above atmospheric: absolute = atmospheric + gauge; below: absolute = atmospheric − gauge.
  • Pressure inside the sea can be more than twice atmospheric → scuba divers wear special suits.
  • Tyre pressure in petrol bunks is measured in psi (book says "pascal per inch"; actually pound per square inch – extra).

Pascal's law and hydraulic press

  • Pascal's law (Blaise Pascal, 1623–1662, French mathematician & physicist): external pressure applied on an incompressible liquid (at rest) is transmitted equally and undiminished in all directions. Demo: glass vessel with holes + piston.
  • Hydraulic press – based on Pascal's law; two cylinders/pistons of areas a (small) and A (large); F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂ → F₂ = F₁ × A₂/A₁; works as a force multiplier. Uses: hydraulic lift in garages, extraction of oil from oil seeds. Problem: 2000 kg vehicle on 0.5 m² piston, effort piston 0.03 m² → 1176 N.

Density, relative density

  • Density (ρ, rho) = mass per unit volume; SI unit kg m⁻³ (also g cm⁻³). Water 1 g cm⁻³ (= 1000 kg m⁻³); kerosene 0.8 g cm⁻³.
  • Relative density = density of substance / density of water at 4 °C (density of water is 1 g cm⁻³ at 4 °C) = mass of a volume of substance / mass of equal volume of water at 4 °C. A pure number, no unit. Also called specific gravity (when reference substance is water). Mercury RD = 13.6.
  • RD measured with pycnometer (density bottle) – ground glass stopper with a fine hole so the bottle always holds the same volume.
  • Floating and sinking: density of object < liquid → floats (wood); > liquid → sinks (stone). Block 1155 g, 462 cm³ → 2.5 g cm⁻³ → sinks.
  • Hydrometer – direct-reading instrument for density/relative density of liquids; based on principle of flotation (Archimedes' principle); cylindrical stem, spherical bulb with lead shots or mercury (keeps it upright), graduated narrow tube.
  • Calibrated hydrometers: lactometer – density (creaminess)/purity of milk; saccharometer – density of sugar in a liquid; alcoholometer – alcohol in spirits.
  • Lactometer: graduations 15 (top) to 45 (bottom); air chamber makes it float; bulb has mercury; correct reading only at 60 °F; more cream → floats lower; normal milk reading ≈ 32; used at dairies.

Buoyancy, Archimedes, flotation

  • Buoyant force (upthrust) = upward force on a body partly/fully immersed in a fluid, due to greater pressure at the bottom than at the top; phenomenon = buoyancy. High volume + low density → most buoyant.
  • Weighs less than water displaced → floats (positively buoyant); weighs more → sinks (negatively buoyant).
  • Salt (sea) water gives more buoyant force than fresh water (denser) → easier to swim in sea. Buoyant force depends on density of fluid and volume displaced.
  • Cartesian diver – pen cap with clay; demonstrates buoyancy (and ideal gas law): squeezing the bottle pushes water in → average density rises → sinks.
  • Archimedes' principle (a consequence of Pascal's law): a body immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced. Upthrust = weight of fluid displaced = apparent loss of weight. Apparent weight = true weight − upthrust. Legend: noticed apparent loss of weight in his bath, ran shouting "Eureka"; devised the "hydrostatic balance". Applies to gases too.
  • Laws of flotation: (1) weight of a floating body = weight of fluid displaced; (2) centre of gravity of the floating body and centre of buoyancy (point where buoyant force acts) lie on the same vertical line.
  • Flotation therapy uses water with Epsom salts rich in magnesium, absorbed through skin; magnesium helps the body process insulin → lowers risk of Type 2 diabetes.
  • Exercise facts: rising air bubble increases in size; clouds float due to low density; pressure cooker – increased pressure raises the boiling point; a drinking straw works due to atmospheric pressure; helium balloon floats (less dense than air).

Physics · Unit 4 – Electric Charge and Electric Current (PDF p. 43–55)

  • Electric charge = a fundamental property of all matter (like mass, length). Electron – negative, proton – positive, neutron – no charge. Atom normally neutral (electrons = protons).
  • Atom loses an electron → positive ion; gains an electron → negative ion.
  • Comb rubbed on dry hair: electrons move from hair to comb → comb negative, hair positive; rubbing transfers charge (does not create it). Positive charge = absence of electrons.
  • SI unit of charge = coulomb (C). Charge of an electron e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C (fundamental unit). Any charge q = ne (n whole number) – quantisation. 1 C = 6.25 × 10¹⁸ electrons. Practical units: µC = 10⁻⁶ C, nC = 10⁻⁹ C, pC = 10⁻¹² C.
  • Charge is additive (+5 C and −2 C → +3 C). Electrostatic forces between point charges obey Newton's third law.
  • Electric force: like charges repel, unlike charges attract; acts even without contact. Coulomb's law (extra): force between two point charges ∝ product of charges and ∝ 1/(distance)².
  • Electric field = region around a charge in which another charge experiences electric force; direction = direction of force on a small positive charge. Electric field at a point = force on unit positive charge there (unit N C⁻¹).
  • Electric lines of force – imaginary straight/curved paths along which a unit positive charge tends to move; closer lines = stronger field; radially outward from positive, inward to negative charge (start on +, end on −).
  • Electric potential = work done on unit positive charge to bring it to that point against electrical forces.
  • Electric current = moving charges; electrons flow from lower to higher potential; potential difference usually produced by a cell/battery. Conventional current = flow of positive charge (higher → lower potential, opposite to electron flow); electron current = flow of electrons.
  • Circuit symbol: positive terminal = long line, negative = short line. Battery = combination of more than one cell.
  • Current I = q/t; SI unit ampere (A); 1 A = 1 C s⁻¹. 25 C in 50 s → 0.5 A; 0.2 A for 1 h → 720 C.
  • Ammeter measures current – connected in series (current enters at red + terminal, leaves at black −).
  • Electromotive force (e.m.f, ε) = work done by a source in driving unit charge round the complete circuit, ε = W/q; unit J C⁻¹ = volt (V). Battery is analogous to a water pump in a pipeline. 1.5 V cell, 0.5 C → 0.75 J.
  • Potential difference (V) = W/q, electrical energy converted to other forms per coulomb across a device; unit volt. Heater: 5 × 10⁶ J for 2 × 10⁴ C → 250 V.
  • e.m.f vs p.d.: e.m.f = voltage across terminals of a source when it does not produce current; p.d. = voltage between any two points when current flows.
  • Voltmeter measures potential difference – connected in parallel across the component.
  • Resistance (R) = opposition to flow of current; SI unit ohm (Ω): 1 Ω when 1 V drives 1 A. Depends on temperature, geometry (length, area) and nature of material.
  • Good conductors (negligible resistance): copper, aluminium. High resistance: nichrome, tin oxide. Insulators (do not conduct): glass, polymer, rubber, paper.
  • Resistors – fixed or variable; variable resistor = rheostat.
  • Ohm's law (extra, asked in exercise): at constant temperature, current through a conductor ∝ potential difference across it, V = IR.
  • Four main circuit components: cell, connecting wire, switch, resistor (load). Standard symbols exist for galvanometer (G), ammeter (A), voltmeter (V), fuse, diode, transformer, LDR, thermistor, capacitor, potentiometer etc.
  • Series circuit – components one after another in a single loop; one path; same current at every point. Parallel circuit – two or more loops; more than one path; sum of branch currents = main current; same potential difference across each branch. Home appliances are connected in parallel.

Effects of electric current – heating, chemical, magnetic

  • Heating effect = Joule heating (studied by Joule): work done against resistance → heat. Principle of iron box, water heater, toaster; connecting wires also get warm. (Aluminium-foil arrow tip melts with 9 V cell. Never experiment with 220 V ac mains.)
  • Chemical effect: copper deposited on a carbon rod (cathode) from copper sulphate solution using a copper wire (anode) = electroplating. Conduction of current through solutions = electrolysis; solution = electrolyte; positive terminal = anode, negative = cathode. In electrolytes current is due to both electrons and positive ions.
  • Do you know: very weak currents in the human body (charged particles) = synaptic signals, produced electro-chemically, travel between brain and organs through the nervous system.
  • Magnetic effect: a current-carrying conductor produces a magnetic field perpendicular to the current (Oersted; right hand thumb rule – thumb = current, fingers = field).

Types of current

  • Direct current (dc) – unidirectional flow; sources: battery, solar cells, thermocouples; used in cell phones, radio, electric keyboard, electric vehicles.
  • Alternating current (ac) – changes direction alternately, varies sinusoidally; electrons move to and fro. Frequency = number of complete cycles per second. Domestic supply is ac.
  • Rectifier converts ac → dc (battery eliminator, dc adaptor); inverter converts dc → ac.
  • Advantages of ac: voltage easily changed by a transformer; transmitted over long distances with step-up transformers with negligible loss (dc cannot be transmitted as such); easily converted to dc; easier to generate; produces electromagnetic induction.
  • Advantages of dc: electroplating, electro-refining, electrotyping only with dc; electricity can be stored only as dc.

Physics · Unit 5 – Magnetism and Electromagnetism (PDF p. 56–69)

  • Einstein was attracted by magnets in childhood. Ship captains used magnets to find direction at sea.
  • Natural magnets – found in rocks and sandy deposits; strongest natural magnet = lodestone (magnetite); magnetism permanent; lodestones were used to make old compasses. Artificial magnets – made by us (those sold in shops).
  • Magnetic field (B) = region around a magnet where its influence is felt; SI unit tesla (T); direction found with a compass. Penetrates all materials. Earth's magnetic field shields the ozone layer from the solar wind and helps navigation.
  • Magnetic field line = curve whose tangent at any point gives the field direction; path along which a free north pole would move.
  • Magnetic flux (ϕ) = number of field lines through a given area; unit weber (Wb). Magnetic flux density = lines per unit area normal to the field; unit Wb/m² (= tesla).
  • Do you know: loggerhead sea turtles return to their birth beach decades later, perceiving and remembering Earth's magnetic field intensity.
  • Properties of magnetic lines of force: closed continuous curves (pass through the magnet); start at N pole, end at S pole (outside); never intersect; field strongest at the poles; tangent gives field direction.

Magnetic effect of current

  • Hans Christian Oersted (Danish physicist), 21 April 1820, during a lecture noticed a compass needle deflect when current flowed. Wire along N–S: compass above deflects east, below deflects west → current produces a magnetic field.
  • Right hand thumb rule: hold wire in right hand, thumb along current → fingers curl in the direction of the magnetic lines. Field is perpendicular to the current; lines are concentric circles, stronger near the wire.
  • Field strength depends on: current, distance from wire, orientation of point, magnetic nature of the medium.
  • Before Oersted, electricity and magnetism were separate; after him united as electromagnetism. Magnetism of lodestone (understood in the 20th century) is due to motion of electrons around the nucleus; in magnetic materials these add up.

Force on a conductor

  • H.A. Lorentz: a charge moving in a magnetic field (not along it) experiences a force = magnetic Lorentz force.
  • Michael Faraday (1821): a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field gets deflected.
  • F = I L B (conductor perpendicular to field) – maximum when perpendicular, zero when parallel. Force is a vector. 5 A, 50 cm, 2 × 10⁻³ T → 5 × 10⁻³ N; I×4, L÷2, B×3 → force 6 times.
  • Fleming's Left Hand Rule (John Ambrose Fleming) – for direction of force (motor rule): thumb, forefinger, middle finger mutually perpendicular; Forefinger = Field (N to S), seCond (middle) finger = Current (+ to −), thuMb = Motion/force.
  • Parallel current-carrying conductors: currents in the same direction attract; opposite directions repel; forces equal and opposite.

Electric motor

  • Electric motor converts electrical → mechanical energy; used in water pump, fan, washing machine, juicer, mixer, grinder.
  • Principle: current-carrying coil in a magnetic field experiences a turning effect (opposite forces on AB and CD). Split ring commutator reverses current every half rotation → continuous rotation; carbon brushes; permanent magnet.
  • Speed of motor increases by: more current, more turns, larger coil area, stronger magnetic field.

Electromagnetic induction

  • Michael Faraday, 1831: e.m.f produced across a conductor when the magnetic flux linked with it changes. Experiments: (1) two coils on a soft iron ring – galvanometer deflects at switch on and off (opposite directions); (2) moving a magnet in/out of a coil – more turns, more voltage; (3) moving the coil in a stationary magnet's field.
  • Electromagnetic induction = producing an induced e.m.f by changing magnetic flux linked with a closed circuit; induced e.m.f ∝ rate of change of flux (Faraday's laws).
  • Lenz's law: induced current flows so as to oppose the change causing it.
  • Fleming's Right Hand Rule = generator (dynamo) rule: forefinger = field, thumb = motion of conductor, middle finger = induced current.
  • Know your scientist: Michael Faraday (22 Sep 1791 – 25 Aug 1867), British; discoveries – electromagnetic induction, diamagnetism, electrolysis.

Generator and transformer

  • Electric generator (dynamo) converts mechanical → electrical energy; works on electromagnetic induction.
  • AC generator: rotating rectangular coil = armature between poles of a permanent magnet; ends joined to slip rings S1, S2; stationary brushes B1, B2 pass current to the external circuit; current reverses every half rotation. DC generator uses a split ring commutator → unidirectional current.
  • Transformer – converts low ac voltage to high and vice versa; works on electromagnetic induction; primary and secondary coils on an iron core. Works on AC only (with DC, flux does not change → no induced e.m.f).
  • Step-up: Vs > Vp, Ns > Np, increases voltage but decreases current. Step-down: Vs < Vp, Ns < Np. Energy lost as heat, sound.
  • Formulae: Vs/Vp = Ns/Np; Ip/Is = Ns/Np. 800 primary, 8 secondary turns, 220 V → 2.2 V. A mains-run portable radio uses a step-down transformer.

Applications of electromagnets

  • Speaker: electromagnet (movable) in front of a fixed permanent magnet; current pulses make it vibrate; attached cone (paper/plastic) amplifies vibrations into sound.
  • Maglev (magnetic levitation) trains: suspended only by magnetic fields; one set of magnets repels to lift the train, another moves it forward without friction; no moving part; guideway of magnets controls stability and speed.
  • Medical: hyperthermia treatment for cancer, implants, MRI (magnetic resonance imaging – images of internal parts), scanners, X-ray equipment.
  • Match: magnetic material – iron; non-magnetic – wood; current & magnetism – Oersted; electromagnetic induction – Faraday.

Physics · Unit 6 – Light (PDF p. 70–83)

  • Light = a form of energy that travels as electromagnetic waves and produces the sensation of sight. Branch dealing with light = optics.
  • Laws of reflection: (1) incident ray, reflected ray and normal at the point of incidence lie in the same plane; (2) angle of incidence = angle of reflection (i = r).
  • Lateral inversion (Latin latus = side) = apparent sidewise (left–right) inversion in a plane mirror; mirrors actually reverse "inside out" – it is an effect of perception. Mirror writing: "AMBULANCE" written mirrored on the front of ambulances.
  • Real image – rays actually meet; always inverted; can be caught on a screen. Virtual image – rays appear to meet when produced backward; always erect; cannot be caught on a screen.
  • To see your full body, a plane mirror must be at least half your height.
  • Spherical mirrors: reflecting surface part of a sphere. Concave – curved inwards (faces centre of sphere); convex – curved outwards. (Parabolic mirrors are another type of curved mirror.)
  • Glossary: centre of curvature = centre of the hollow sphere; radius of curvature = its radius; pole = midpoint of the mirror; aperture = diameter of the circular rim; principal axis = normal through the centre of the mirror; principal focus = point where rays parallel to the axis meet (or appear to meet) after reflection; focal length = distance pole to focus, f = R/2.
  • Ray rules: (1) ray through C reflects back along its own path; (2) ray parallel to axis passes through (convex: appears to come from) F; (3) ray through F reflects parallel to axis; (4) ray at the pole reflects with equal angles.

  • Do you know: bioluminescence = organisms making their own light (worms, fish, squid, starfish in the dark sea) to scare predators.

Refraction

  • Refraction = bending of light passing obliquely from one transparent medium to another, due to change in velocity of light. Light is faster in a rarer (low optical density) medium. Pencil in water appears bent; coin in water appears raised.
  • Rarer → denser: bends towards the normal; denser → rarer: away from the normal; normal incidence (i = 0°): no deviation.
  • Snell's law (laws of refraction): (1) incident ray, refracted ray and normal lie in the same plane; (2) sin i / sin r = constant = refractive index (µ) of second medium w.r.t. first, for a given colour and pair of media. µ has no unit; µ = speed of light in vacuum (air) / speed in medium. Glass: 3 × 10⁸/2 × 10⁸ = 1.5; water 1.33; diamond 2.41; i = 45°, r = 30° → 1.414.
  • White light through a prism is deviated and dispersed.
  • Critical angle = angle of incidence in the denser medium for which angle of refraction = 90° (refracted ray grazes the surface).
  • Total internal reflection (TIR): when angle of incidence > critical angle, light is totally reflected back into the denser medium. Conditions: light travels denser → rarer; angle of incidence > critical angle.
  • TIR in nature: mirage (hot air near road is less dense, lower refractive index), brilliance of diamond, twinkling of stars.
  • Optical fibres – bundles of high-quality glass/quartz fibres; each has a core (higher refractive index) and cladding; work on TIR; transmit audio/video signals over long distances; used by physicians to see inside the body (endoscopy) without major surgery. Narinder Kapany (Indian-born) = Father of Fibre Optics.

Physics · Unit 7 – Heat (PDF p. 84–94)

  • Internal energy = kinetic energy + potential energy of molecules; internal energy flowing out = heat energy; flows from hot to cold substances.
  • Effects of heat: expansion (gaps left in railway tracks for summer expansion; expansion: solids < liquids < gases (maximum)); change of state; change in temperature (KE of particles rises); chemical changes (heat starts reactions and decides their speed; cooking).
  • Heat transfer – from higher to lower temperature until temperatures are equal. Three ways: conduction, convection, radiation.
  • Do you know: a dog pants with tongue out – evaporation of moisture removes heat and cools its body.

Conduction (mainly solids)

  • Conduction = transfer of heat in solids from higher to lower temperature without actual movement of molecules (molecules vibrate and pass energy to neighbours). Slow process; larger area conducts more; metals are good conductors; liquids and gases conduct very slowly (water is a bad conductor).
  • Daily life: aluminium utensils (good conductor); mercury in thermometers (good conductor); woollen clothes in winter – trapped air, a bad conductor, stops body heat escaping.
  • Rods with wax-fixed matchsticks: match falls first from copper → order of conduction copper > aluminium > brass > iron. Copper-bottom vessels cook faster.

Convection (liquids and gases)

  • Convection = flow of heat through a fluid from hotter to cooler places by movement of the fluid itself. Demo: potassium permanganate crystals in heated water – purple streaks rise. Warm air (less dense) rises, cool air sinks → circular flow.
  • Daily life: hot air balloons; sea breeze (day: land heats faster, air over land rises, cool sea air moves to land); land breeze (night: air over sea warmer, rises, cool land air moves to sea); winds (from high pressure to low pressure); tall chimneys in kitchens and furnaces (hot gases and smoke rise).

Radiation

  • Radiation = flow of heat by electromagnetic waves, travels at the speed of light, needs no medium (occurs in vacuum); fastest mode. Sun's heat reaches Earth by radiation. Emitted by all bodies above 0 K.
  • Burning wood shows all three: along the wood – conduction; air above – convection; warmth on hands – radiation.
  • Daily life: white/light clothes reflect heat (summer); blackened base of cooking vessels absorbs more heat; airplane surface highly polished to reflect sun's heat. Thermos flask minimises loss by conduction, convection and radiation.

Temperature

  • Temperature = degree of hotness or coolness of a body; SI unit kelvin (K); daily use °C; measured by a thermometer. Calorie is a unit of heat.
  • Three scales: Fahrenheit (freezing 32 °F, boiling 212 °F, 180 divisions), Celsius/Centigrade (0 °C, 100 °C, 100 divisions), Kelvin/Absolute.
  • Conversions: F = (9/5)C + 32; C = (5/9)(F − 32); K = C + 273.15. (25 °C = 77 °F = 298.15 K; 14 °F = −10 °C; 200 K = −73.15 °C.)
  • Absolute zero (0 K = −273.15 °C ≈ −460 °F) – molecules have lowest possible energy; pressure and volume of a gas theoretically reach zero.
  • Triple point of water = 273.16 K (solid, liquid, vapour coexist); kelvin = 1/273.16 of this.

Heat capacity, change of state, latent heat

  • Heat absorbed/lost depends on mass, change in temperature, nature of material: Q = mCΔT.
  • Specific heat capacity (C) = heat required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C (1 K); SI unit J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ (also J/kg °C, J/g °C).
  • Water has the highest specific heat capacity – 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ → used as coolant in car radiators and factories; lake temperature changes little; land heats/cools faster than water. Ice 2100; steam 460 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ (book values). 2 kg water 10 → 50 °C needs 3,36,000 J.
  • Heat capacity (thermal capacity) C′ = heat needed to raise the temperature of the whole body by 1 °C = Q/ΔT; SI unit J/K (also cal/°C). Iron ball 5000 J for 20 K → 250 J K⁻¹.
  • Change of state at a definite temperature: melting/fusion (solid → liquid, absorbs heat; melting point), freezing (liquid → solid, releases heat; freezing point) – water 0 °C; boiling/vaporisation (liquid → vapour, absorbs heat) and condensation (vapour → liquid, releases heat) – water 100 °C.
  • Sublimation = solid → gas directly: dry ice (solid CO₂), iodine, naphthalene balls.
  • Latent heat ("latent" = hidden) = heat absorbed/released during change of state without change in temperature (ice stays at 0 °C while melting; water at 100 °C while boiling). Latent heat of fusion (melting/freezing); latent heat of vaporisation (boiling/condensation).
  • Specific latent heat L = Q/m; SI unit J/kg. Specific latent heat of ice = 336 J/g (exercise uses 3,34,000 J/kg); 5 kg ice needs 1.68 × 10⁶ J.
  • Exercise facts: ice kept in double-walled container (reduces heat transfer); water in an earthen pot stays cool by evaporation through pores.

Physics · Unit 8 – Sound (PDF p. 95–105)

  • Sound = form of energy producing the sensation of hearing; produced by vibrations (tuning fork struck on a rubber pad). Cymbals – vibrating metal plates.
  • Sound needs a material medium (air, water, steel); cannot travel through vacuum – Bell-jar experiment (sound fades as air is pumped out). No sound heard on the Moon.
  • Wave = disturbance carried forward in a medium; only the disturbance (energy) travels, particles only vibrate to and fro.
  • Longitudinal wave = propagates by compressions (particles crowded, high pressure/density) and rarefactions (spread apart, low pressure); particles move along the direction of propagation. Sound = longitudinal mechanical wave (slinky/spring demo).

Reflection, echo, reverberation

  • Laws of reflection of sound: angle of incidence = angle of reflection; incident sound, reflected sound and normal lie in the same plane. Needs a large obstacle (polished or rough).
  • Multiple reflection uses: megaphones, loudspeakers, horns, nathaswaram, shehnai, trumpet (tube + conical opening sends sound forward); stethoscope (sounds reach doctor's ears by multiple reflections in the tube).
  • More to know: long use of earphones → inner-ear infection, eardrum damage; dangerous on roads and while sleeping.
  • Echo = repetition of sound due to reflection. Persistence of hearing ≈ 0.1 s → for a distinct echo, minimum distance of obstacle = 17 m (total path 34 m at 340 m s⁻¹); varies with air temperature. Rolling of thunder = successive reflections from clouds and land.
  • Reverberation = persistence of sound in a big hall due to repeated reflections; reduced with sound-absorbing materials – compressed fibreboard, flannel, rough plaster, draperies, seat materials. Science of designing auditoria = acoustics.

Ultrasound, SONAR, ECG, ear

  • Ultrasound (> 20,000 Hz) – dogs can hear it; ultrasonic whistles in cars warn deer. Echogram = image from reflected ultrasound (medical diagnosis); also marine surveying.
  • More to know – echolocation (book: "echolation"): bats, dolphins, rats, whales, oil birds locate objects by echoes; bats navigate dark caves and find insects; dolphins/whales emit ultrasonic clicks.
  • Applications of ultrasound: cleaning minute particles in a liquid bath; detecting cracks/flaws in metal blocks; echocardiography (image of heart); breaking kidney stones into fine grains.
  • SONAR = SOund Navigation And Ranging – uses ultrasonic waves to find distance, direction and speed of underwater objects; transmitter + detector at bottom of ships; 2d = v × t (echo-ranging). Finds sea depth, underwater hills, valleys, submarines, icebergs. (3.42 s at 1531 m s⁻¹ → 2618 m.)
  • ECG (electrocardiogram) – one of the simplest and oldest cardiac investigations; records the electrical activity of the heart muscle over time, printed on paper.
  • Human ear: pinna (outer ear, collects sound) → auditory canal → eardrum (tympanic membrane) vibrates → middle-ear bones hammer, anvil, stirrup amplify vibrations → inner ear cochlea converts them to electrical signals → auditory nerve → brain.

Physics · Unit 9 – Universe (PDF p. 106–116)

  • Geocentric model (Earth at centre) – Greek astronomer Ptolemy (2nd century), Indian astronomer Aryabhatta (5th century). Heliocentric model (helios = Sun) – Polish astronomer Nicolaus Copernicus. Telescope invented in the Netherlands, 1608.
  • Universe = everything that exists – matter, energy, even time. Basic constituent = galaxies (luminous matter). Observable universe ≈ 93 billion light years across (1 light year = 9.4607 × 10¹² km). Universe is expanding, at an increasing rate. Atoms make up only about 4%; the rest is dark matter and dark energy.
  • Big Bang – universe began from a single point of hot dense matter about 13.7 billion years ago. Hydrogen and helium were created in the Big Bang; oxygen, carbon, calcium, iron, silicon are formed in the cores of stars and released when stars explode.
  • Galaxy = massive collection of gas, dust and billions of stars; about 100 billion (10¹¹) galaxies in the observable universe. Shapes: spiral, elliptical, irregular. Occur in groups, clusters, super clusters.
  • Milky Way – our galaxy; spiral; ~100 billion stars; diameter 1,00,000 light years; solar system is 25,000 light years from its centre; Sun takes about 250 million years (p. 108) / 225 million years (p. 111) to go round it – this period is a cosmic year; Sun moves at 250 km/s.
  • Andromeda – nearest neighbouring galaxy, about 2.5 million light years away.
  • Stars – fundamental building blocks of galaxies; gas and plasma (superheated matter); hydrogen fuses to helium (heat). Emit heat, light, UV, X-rays. About 3,000 stars visible to the naked eye on a dark night. Most stars exist in pairs. Colour depends on temperature: hot – white/blue, cooler – orange/red.
  • Constellation = group of stars forming an imaginary pattern; 88 formally accepted (Aries, Gemini, Leo, Orion, Scorpius, Cassiopeia; zodiac: Aries … Pisces).
  • Pole star appears stationary in the north (lies on Earth's axis of rotation); not visible from the southern hemisphere.

Solar system

  • Sun – medium-sized star; ¾ hydrogen, ¼ helium; over a million times as big as Earth; energy by nuclear fusion (H → He); more than 4.6 billion years old; formed from a spinning molecular cloud of H, He and dust that flattened into a disc.
  • Planet's path = orbit (elliptical); period of revolution = one trip round the Sun; period of rotation = one spin on its axis. Earth's rotation = 23 h 56 min (day taken as 24 h).
  • Inner (terrestrial/rocky) planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars – no rings. Outer planets (gas giants): Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune – H, He, dense atmospheres, have rings (rock pieces covered with ice). Saturn–Uranus distance ≈ 20 × Earth–Mars distance.

Satellites, Kepler, ISS

  • First artificial satellite Sputnik – book says 1956 (actually launched 4 Oct 1957 by USSR – extra). India's first satellite Aryabhata – 19 April 1975.
  • Orbital velocity = horizontal velocity given to a satellite at a height so that it moves in a circular orbit; v = √(GM/(R + h)); G = 6.673 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻²; M (Earth) = 5.972 × 10²⁴ kg; R = 6371 km. Nearer to Earth → higher orbital velocity. At 500 km: v ≈ 7.6 km/s, T ≈ 95 min.
  • Geostationary satellite – period 24 h, same as Earth's rotation, so it stays over the same spot (book links this to 200 km/27,400 km/h; actually geostationary height ≈ 36,000 km – extra, as in the exercise).
  • Time period of a satellite T = 2π(R + h)/v.
  • Microgravity – condition where people/objects appear weightless (astronauts float).
  • Kepler's laws (Johannes Kepler, early 1600s, from data of his mentor Tycho Brahe): (1) Law of Ellipses – planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus (closest point perihelion, farthest aphelion); (2) Law of Equal Areas – line joining planet and Sun sweeps equal areas in equal times (faster near the Sun); (3) Law of Harmonies – T² ∝ R³. (T of A = 8 × T of B → distance 4 times.)
  • International Space Station (ISS) – large spacecraft/science lab in low Earth orbit at ~400 km; first part placed 1998; core completed 2011; first crew 2000, continuously occupied since; at least six humans aboard; largest man-made object in space, visible to the naked eye; planned to operate till 2024 (possible extension to 2028).
  • Benefits of ISS (NASA): water purification (Water Recovery System and Oxygen Generation System saved a village in Iraq); eye-tracking device (laser surgery, helping disabled people); robotic arms for removing inoperable tumours (brain) and biopsies; better vaccines, breast cancer detection/treatment, ultrasound for remote regions.
  • ISS cooperation: five space agencies of 16 countries – NASA (USA), Roskosmos (Russia), ESA (Europe), JAXA (Japan), CSA (Canada); plus Belgium, Brazil, Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, Holland, Norway, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, UK.
  • A 60 kg man would "weigh" 1680 kg on the Sun (Sun's gravity ≈ 28 times Earth's).

Chemistry · Unit 10 – Matter Around Us (PDF p. 117–128)

  • Matter = anything that has mass and occupies space. Sunlight, sound, force and energy are not matter.
  • Chemical classification: pure substances (one kind of particle) – elements (copper, oxygen, hydrogen) and compounds (water, sugar, salt); impure substances = mixtures – homogeneous (uniform: sugar + water, water + alcohol) and heterogeneous (non-uniform: sand + sugar, water + oil).
  • Element – Robert Boyle used the name for a substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances; made of only one kind of atom (aluminium → only Al atoms).
  • 118 elements in the modern periodic table: 92 naturally occurring, 26 artificial.
  • Atom = smallest unit of an element; may or may not exist independently but always takes part in chemical reactions. Molecule = smallest unit of a pure substance that exists independently and retains its properties (H₂; H₂O).
  • Elements: metals (copper, chromium, gold, mercury), metalloids (boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic), non-metals (carbon, oxygen, neon, chlorine).
  • Compound = two or more elements chemically combined; properties differ from the elements. Cane sugar C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ (C, H, O); common salt = sodium chloride (metal Na + non-metal Cl).
  • Uses of compounds: P, N, K compounds – fertilisers; silicon compounds – computer industry; fluorine compounds – toothpaste (strengthen teeth).
  • Element vs compound: one kind of atom vs more than one; smallest unit atom vs molecule; cannot vs can be broken down by chemical methods.
  • Mixture = two or more elements/compounds physically mixed in any ratio: tap water, lemonade, air (N₂, O₂, CO₂, water vapour…), soil, milk, ice cream, rock salt, tea, smoke, wood, sea water, blood (platelets, RBC, WBC, plasma), toothpaste, paint. Alloys are mixtures of metals – brass ≈ 70% copper + 30% zinc.
  • More to know – LPG (Liquefied Petroleum Gas): highly inflammable hydrocarbon mixture of butane and propane, liquefied by pressure; cooking, heating, auto fuel.
  • Iron + sulphur: mixture = dirty yellow powder, magnet attracts iron; on heating → iron(II) sulphide, black brittle solid, not attracted by magnet.
  • Mixture vs compound: any proportion vs definite proportion; shows constituents' properties vs new properties; separated by physical methods vs only by chemical reactions.
  • Homogeneous mixtures: tap water, milk, air, ice cream, sugar syrup, ink, steel, bronze, salt solution. Heterogeneous: soil, iodine + salt, sugar + sand, oil + water, sulphur + iron filings, milk + cereals. (Book's classification.)

Chemistry · Unit 11 – Atomic Structure (PDF p. 129–141)

  • Earlier models (Class 8): Dalton's atomic theory (first atomic theory), J.J. Thomson's plum pudding model.
  • Rutherford's gold foil (α-scattering) experiment, 1911 – Lord Rutherford (New Zealand) bombarded thin gold foil (gold = most malleable metal) with alpha particles (positive). Observations: most passed straight through; some slightly deflected; very few bounced back.
  • Rutherford's model: atom mostly empty space; positively charged nucleus at centre; nucleus very small compared to atom; electrons revolve in circular orbits; atom neutral (protons = electrons). Like the solar system. Led to discovery of the nucleus.
  • Limitation: by electromagnetic theory a revolving electron should lose energy and fall into the nucleus → could not explain stability of atom.
  • Bohr's model (1913, Niels Bohr, Danish physicist): electrons revolve in fixed stationary orbits / shells / energy levels – K, L, M, N … (n = 1, 2, 3, 4 …); no energy lost or gained while in an orbit; jumps between shells by absorbing/releasing fixed energy. K shell (closest) has least energy. Limitation: applicable only to hydrogen and hydrogen-like ions (He⁺, Li²⁺, Be³⁺), not multi-electron atoms. (Match: Bohr – hydrogen atom model.)
  • Neutron – discovered by James Chadwick, 1932 (student of Rutherford): beryllium + α-particles → carbon + neutron. Rutherford had predicted a neutral particle in 1920. Symbol ₀n¹; not deflected by electric/magnetic field; mass 1.676 × 10⁻²⁴ g (≈1 amu).

  • Atomic number Z (Z = Zahl, German "number"; Atomzahl) = number of protons = number of electrons (in neutral atom); decides the element (H 1, He 2, gold 79).
  • Mass number A (nucleon number) = protons + neutrons. Neutrons n = A − Z (²⁴₁₂Mg → 12). Written as ᴬ_Z X, e.g. ¹⁴₇N. Mass 39, neutrons 20 → Z = 19 = potassium.
  • Atomic mass in amu (new unit u); atom size in nanometre (10⁻⁹ m); atoms can be viewed with SEM (Scanning Electron Microscope).
  • Electronic configuration – Bohr–Bury rules: (1) max electrons in a shell = 2n² (K 2, L 8, M 18, N 32); (2) shells filled stepwise in increasing order of energy; (3) outermost shell cannot have more than 8 electrons. Ca (20) = 2, 8, 8, 2; Al (13) = 2, 8, 3; K = 2, 8, 8, 1; O = 2, 6.
  • Valence shell = outermost shell; valence electrons decide chemical properties.
  • Valency = combining capacity. Valence electrons 1, 2, 3, 4 → valency 1, 2, 3, 4; 5, 6, 7 → valency 3, 2, 1 (8 − valence electrons); completely filled shell (Ne 2,8; Ar 2,8,8) → valency 0. Mg (2,8,2) = 2; S (2,8,6) = 2.
  • 1, 2 or 3 valence electrons (except H) → metals; 4 to 7 → non-metals. Same number of valence electrons → similar properties (Li, Na, K all end in 1; Be, Mg, Ca end in 2).

  • Isotopes = atoms of the same element with same atomic number, different mass number: hydrogen ¹H, ²H, ³H (protium, deuterium, tritium – extra names); ¹²C, ¹³C; ³⁵Cl, ³⁷Cl (same chemical properties – same electrons).   • Isobars = different elements, different atomic numbers, same mass number: ⁴⁰Ca and ⁴⁰Ar.
  • Thumb rule: isotope – top value (mass) changes; isobar – bottom value (atomic number) changes.
  • Isotones = different elements, different Z and A, same number of neutrons: ¹¹B and ¹²C (6 neutrons each); F & Ne, Na & Mg, Al & Si.

Laws of chemical combination (five): conservation of mass; constant proportions; multiple proportions; reciprocal proportions; Gay-Lussac's law of gaseous volumes.

  • Law of multiple proportions – John Dalton, 1804: when two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in a simple ratio. CO : CO₂ oxygen = 16 : 32 = 1 : 2; SO₂ : SO₃ oxygen = 2 : 3.
  • Law of reciprocal proportions – Jeremias Richter, 1792: if two elements combine separately with the same weight of a third element, the ratio of their masses is the same as (or a simple multiple of) the ratio in which they combine with each other. CH₄ (H 4 : C 12), CO₂ (O 32 : C 12) → H : O = 1 : 8; in H₂O H : O = 2 : 16 = 1 : 8.
  • Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes: reacting gases (and gaseous products) bear a simple whole-number ratio of volumes at the same temperature and pressure. 1 vol H₂ + 1 vol Cl₂ → 2 vol HCl (1 : 1 : 2); N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ (1 : 3 : 2).
  • Quantum numbers (four; designate orbitals and electrons): principal n – main energy level; azimuthal l – sub-shell/shape of orbital; magnetic m – orientation of orbitals; spin s – spin of the electron.
  • Glossary: orbital = region around the nucleus where an electron is likely to be found; neutron is present in all nuclei except ordinary hydrogen.

Chemistry · Unit 12 – Periodic Classification of Elements (PDF p. 142–151)

  • Known elements: 31 in 1800, 63 by 1865, 118 now.
  • Döbereiner's triads (1817) – Johann Wolfgang Döbereiner, German chemist: groups of three elements in ascending atomic mass; atomic mass of the middle element ≈ average of the other two (law of triads). Triads: Li 6.9 – Na 23 – K 39.1 ((6.9 + 39.1)/2 = 23); Cl 35.5 – Br 79.9 – I 126.9; Ca 40.1 – Sr 87.6 – Ba 137.3. Limitation: only three triads found; not valid for very low/high atomic masses.
  • Newlands' law of octaves (1866) – John Newlands arranged 56 elements by increasing atomic mass; every eighth element resembles the first, like the eighth note of music (sa re ga ma pa da ni sa); F is 8th from H. Limitations: two elements in one slot (Co & Ni); dissimilar elements together (Co, Ni, Pd, Pt, Ir with halogens); not valid beyond calcium; only 56 elements, no room for new ones; discovery of noble gases (Ne between F and Na) made the 9th element similar.
  • Mendeleev's periodic table (1869) – Dmitri Mendeleev, Russian chemist. Law of periodicity: physical and chemical properties are periodic functions of their atomic masses. 56 elements; "short form" of periodic table; 8 groups (vertical, each with subgroups A and B) and 7 periods (horizontal).
  • Merits: similar elements in same group; corrected wrong atomic masses (beryllium 14 → 9); left gaps and predicted unknown elements – Eka-aluminium (gallium) and Eka-silicon (= germanium, discovered in his lifetime). Germanium: predicted (1871) atomic mass ~72, sp. gravity 5.5, dark grey, EsO₂, EsCl₄; actual (1886) 72.59, 5.47, dark grey, GeO₂, GeCl₄.
  • Limitations: dissimilar elements in one group (hard Cu, Ag with soft Na, K); no proper place for hydrogen; increasing atomic mass not followed throughout (Co & Ni, Te & I); no place for isotopes.
  • Henry Moseley (1913), English physicist – X-ray diffraction experiments proved properties depend on atomic number, not atomic mass.
  • Modern periodic law: chemical and physical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers. Modern table = long form, extension of Mendeleev's.
  • Features: increasing atomic number; 7 periods (horizontal rows; period = number of shells); 18 groups (vertical columns).

Chemistry · Unit 13 – Chemical Bonding (PDF p. 152–165)

  • No atom exists as an independent single entity in nature except noble gases. A group of atoms existing together = molecule.
  • Chemical bond = force of attraction between atoms that binds them together as a molecule (like thread holding flowers in a garland).
  • Noble gas configurations: He 2; Ne 2,8; Ar 2,8,8; Kr 2,8,18,8; Xe 2,8,18,18,8; Rn 2,8,18,32,18,8. All except helium have 8 valence electrons; valency zero; so inert they exist as monoatomic gases.
  • Kossel–Lewis theory (1916) = electronic theory of valence / Octet rule: atoms combine (by transfer or sharing of valence electrons) to attain a stable outer shell of 8 electrons like noble gases – "rule of eight". Na (2,8,1) loses 1 e⁻ → Na⁺ (Ne configuration); Cl (2,8,7) gains 1 e⁻ → Cl⁻ (Ar configuration).
  • Atoms with 1, 2, 3 valence electrons tend to lose electrons; with 5, 6, 7 tend to gain. Electrons lost by a metal = valency of metal; gained by a non-metal = its valency.
  • Lewis dot structure = element symbol surrounded by dots for valence electrons (single dot = unpaired, pair = paired; crosses/circles for another atom). H· ; He: ; ·Be· ; C with 4 dots; N 5; O 6. Dots placed one per side first, then paired.
  • Types of bond – strong: ionic, covalent, coordinate, metallic; weak: hydrogen bond, van der Waals interaction.

Ionic (electrovalent) bond

  • Formed by transfer of electrons; electrostatic attraction between cation (lost electrons) and anion (gained electrons). Also called electrostatic bond. Usually between a metal and a non-metal; group 1 and 2 metals form ionic compounds with non-metals.
  • Electrovalency = number of electrons lost or gained to form an electrovalent bond.
  • Examples: NaCl (Na 2,8,1 → Na⁺ 2,8; Cl 2,8,7 → Cl⁻ 2,8,8); MgCl₂ (Mg loses 2 e⁻ to two Cl atoms); CaO, KBr, CaCl₂.
  • Properties of ionic compounds: crystalline solids; do not conduct in solid state but conduct when molten or in aqueous solution; high melting and boiling points; soluble in polar solvents (water), insoluble in non-polar (benzene C₆H₆, carbon tetrachloride CCl₄); high density, hard but brittle; ionic reactions are rapid/instantaneous.

Covalent bond

  • Formed by sharing of electrons; each atom contributes one electron to the shared pair; also called atomic bond.
  • Single bond (—) H—H, Cl—Cl; double bond (=) O=O (two shared pairs); triple bond (≡) N≡N (three shared pairs). Methane CH₄ – carbon shares its 4 electrons with 4 H atoms. Covalent: H₂O, HCl, CCl₄, HF, CO₂.
  • Properties of covalent compounds: gases, liquids or solids (oxygen gas, water liquid, diamond solid); bad conductors (no ions); relatively low melting points (except diamond, silicon carbide); soluble in non-polar solvents (benzene, CCl₄), insoluble in water; soft and waxy, not hard or brittle; slow molecular reactions.
  • More to know: polar solvents (atoms of very different electronegativity – water, ethanol, acetic acid, ammonia) dissolve ionic compounds; non-polar solvents (acetone, benzene, toluene, turpentine) dissolve covalent compounds.
  • Fajans' rules (1923, Kazimierz Fajans, X-ray crystallography): some ionic compounds show covalent character. More covalent when cation is small, anion is large, and cation charge is high. NaCl (+1, large cation, small anion) – ionic; aluminium triiodide (+3, large anion) – covalent.

Coordinate covalent (dative) bond

  • Shared pair comes from only one atom (a lone pair); donor atom gives, acceptor atom (short of two electrons) receives; shown by an arrow (→) from donor to acceptor. Examples: NH₄⁺, NH₃→BF₃ (ammonia donates lone pair to electron-deficient boron trifluoride).
  • Properties: gases, liquids or solids; bad conductors (no ions); melting/boiling points higher than covalent but lower than ionic; insoluble in water, soluble in non-polar solvents (benzene, CCl₄, toluene); slow molecular reactions.

Oxidation, reduction, redox

  • Oxidation = addition of oxygen / removal of hydrogen / loss of electrons: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO; CaH₂ → Ca + H₂; Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻; Na → Na⁺ + e⁻.
  • Reduction = addition of hydrogen / removal of oxygen / gain of electrons: 2Na + H₂ → 2NaH; CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O; Fe³⁺ + e⁻ → Fe²⁺.
  • Redox reaction = oxidation and reduction occur simultaneously: 2PbO + C → 2Pb + CO₂; Zn + CuSO₄ → Cu + ZnSO₄.
  • Oxidising agents = electron acceptors: H₂O₂, MnO₄⁻, CrO₃, Cr₂O₇²⁻. Reducing agents = electron donors: NaBH₄, LiAlH₄, metals palladium, platinum.
  • Oxidation in daily life: cut apple turns brown; iron bolts rust; metal surfaces tarnish = corrosion; spoiling of food left open = rancidity.
  • Oxidation number (oxidation state) = number of electrons an atom gains or loses to form a bond; positive = lost, negative = gained. Sum for a neutral compound = 0; for an ion = its charge; negative value goes to the more electronegative atom. Electronegativity = tendency of an atom in a molecule to attract the shared pair.
  • Rules/examples: H = +1 (except hydrides); O = −2 (most cases); K +1, Br −1 in KBr; N in NH₃ = −3; S in H₂SO₄ = +6; Cr in K₂Cr₂O₇ = +6; Fe in FeSO₄ = +2; Mn in KMnO₄ = +7; C in CO₂ = +4; N in HNO₃ = +5.

Chemistry · Unit 14 – Acids, Bases and Salts (PDF p. 166–175)

  • Body metabolism uses hydrochloric acid secreted in the stomach.
  • Acid – from Latin "acidus" = sour; sour-tasting substances; form H⁺ ions in water. Base – forms OH⁻ ions in water. Acid + base → salt (neutral) + water.

  • All acids contain hydrogen, but not all H-compounds are acids – methane (CH₄) and ammonia (NH₃) give no H⁺.

Uses of acids

  • Sulphuric acid = "King of Chemicals" – used to prepare many other compounds; in car batteries.
  • Hydrochloric acid – toilet cleaning agent. Citric acid – effervescent salts, food preservative. Nitric acid – fertilisers, dyes, paints, drugs. Bases

  • Bases = give OH⁻ in water, or metal oxides that give salt + water with acids (ZnO + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂O). Alkalis = water-soluble bases. All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis: NaOH, KOH – alkalis; Al(OH)₃, Zn(OH)₂ – bases.
  • Ions: CaO → Ca²⁺ + O²⁻; Na₂O; KOH → K⁺ + OH⁻; Ca(OH)₂ (2 OH⁻); Al(OH)₃ (3 OH⁻).
  • Acidity of a base = number of replaceable OH groups: monoacidic NaOH, KOH; diacidic Ca(OH)₂, Mg(OH)₂; triacidic Al(OH)₃, Fe(OH)₃. Strong bases (ionise completely) NaOH, KOH; weak bases NH₄OH, Ca(OH)₂ (book). Concentrated vs dilute alkali.

Special features of carbon (more than 5 million carbon compounds – more than compounds of all other elements together)

  • Catenation = binding of an element to itself (or others) through covalent bonds forming open (linear, branched) or closed (ring) chains; carbon shows it most → organic chemistry = chemistry of catenated carbon compounds (starch, cellulose, plastics are long chains).
  • Tetravalency – C (2,4) shares its 4 valence electrons to complete its octet (Ne configuration) → 4 covalent bonds (methane). Carbon forms only covalent compounds.
  • Multiple bonds: single – methane, alkane; double – ethene, alkene; triple – ethyne (H–C≡C–H), alkyne.

Chemistry · Unit 16 – Applied Chemistry (PDF p. 189–202)

  • Applied chemistry = applying chemical principles to solve real-world problems (drugs, fertilisers, pesticides, diagnostics). Branches: nano, pharmaceutical, electro, radio, dye, agricultural & food, forensic chemistry.

Nanochemistry

  • Branch of nanoscience dealing with chemical applications of nanomaterials (synthesis and manipulation at atomic/molecular level). "Nano" from Greek "Nanos" (dwarf) = billionth: 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m.
  • Scale: nm vs m ≈ golf ball vs Earth; nails grow 1 nm per second; common-cold virus 30 nm; cell membrane ~9 nm; DNA double helix 2 nm across; hydrogen atom ~0.2 nm.
  • Properties differ from atoms and bulk: larger surface area, high surface energy, spatial confinement, fewer imperfections. Surface studied with SEM (scanning electron microscope), TEM, AFM (atomic force microscope).
  • Applications: very active catalysts (metal nanoparticles); nano-coatings/composites in sports equipment, bicycles, automobiles; synthetic skin, implant surgery; microchip circuits; cosmetics, deodorants, sunscreen; anti-bacterial fabrics; stain-resistant, wrinkle-free textiles; space, defence, aeronautics.
  • Drawbacks: unstable with oxygen; exothermic combustion can explode; react with impurities; biologically harmful/toxic; hard to synthesise/isolate; no safe disposal policy.

  • Chemistry of drugs – preparation, composition, structure, effects, storage, therapeutic uses; drug discovery is its core.

  • Two categories: drugs that treat/cure a specific disease; drugs with characteristic effects but no remedial effect (morphine, cocaine).

Electrochemistry

  • Relation between electrical energy and chemical change; processes between electrode and electrolyte. Only redox reactions in a device produce electricity.
  • Electrochemical cell: two electrodes (metal or graphite) – anode (oxidation: metal → ion + e⁻) and cathode (reduction: ion + e⁻ → metal) – and an electrolyte (ionic solution or molten salt).
  • Galvanic cell: chemical → electrical energy; two separate half-cells joined by wire and a salt bridge; electrons flow anode → cathode. (Lemon/potato cell; Daniel cell in concept map.)
  • Electrolytic cell: electrical → chemical energy; both electrodes in the same electrolyte. Electrolysis = decomposition of an electrolyte into its elements by passing electricity through its aqueous or fused (molten) state.
  • Significance: production and purification of non-ferrous metals; electro-synthesis of organic compounds; predicting reactions; breath test for drunken drivers (redox of ethanol); producing aluminium and titanium from ores; lead-acid, lithium-ion batteries, fuel cells (fuel cell – direct chemical → electrical energy); electroplating (corrosion prevention, decoration), electro-refining.

Radiochemistry

  • Unstable isotopes lose energy as radiation to become stable = radioactive decay; such isotopes = radioisotopes; property = radioactivity. Uranium in the ground decays to radon gas – thought to be the second leading cause of lung cancer.
  • Radiochemistry = chemistry of radioactive and non-radioactive isotopes (natural and artificial). Radiocarbon dating – age of fossil wood/animal using C-14.

Dye chemistry

  • Old colourants were natural – inorganic pigments soot, manganese oxide, hematite; organic colourants = dyes (aromatic compounds from plants, insects, fungi, lichens). Synthetic dyes from coal-tar aromatics = coal tar dyes.
  • Dye must: have suitable colour; fix to fabric; be fast to light; resist water, dilute acids and alkalis. All coloured compounds are not dyes. Potato has no natural dye (beetroot, carrot, turmeric do).
  • By method of application: acid dyes – animal/synthetic fibres, wool, silk (picric acid, naphthol yellow-S); basic dyes – with –NH₂, –NHR, –NR₂ groups, animal and plant fibres; mordant (indirect) dyes – fibre pretreated with a mordant (Latin mordere = to bite; Al, Cr, Fe salts) forming an insoluble complex "lake" (alizarin); direct dyes – high affinity for cotton, rayon, cellulose (Congo red); vat dyes – only cotton, dyed in a large vessel (vat) (indigo).

Biology · Unit 18 – Organisation of Tissues (PDF p. 214–229)

  • Tissue = group of cells designed to perform a particular function; organ = collection of tissues (plant organs: root, stem, leaf; tissues: xylem, phloem; stomach = epithelial + gland + muscle cells).

Plant tissues – meristematic and permanent

  • Meristem (Greek meristos = divisible) = immature cells capable of division, in growth zones (stem/root apex, leaf primordia, vascular cambium, cork cambium). Features: living; small, oval/polygonal/round; thin walls, dense cytoplasm, large nucleus, small vacuoles; divide by mitosis; no stored food; no intercellular spaces.
  • By position: apical – root/shoot tips, increase in length; intercalary – between permanent tissues, at base of leaf (Pinus) or base of internodes (grasses); lateral – parallel, increases thickness. Meristems cause primary (elongation) and secondary (thickness) growth.
  • Permanent tissues – growth stopped (may become meristematic again); simple (similar cells) and complex (more than one cell type).
  • Parenchyma – living, thin-walled, oval/round/polygonal, intercellular spaces. In aquatic plants (hydrophytes) with air spaces = aerenchyma (buoyancy); with chloroplasts = chlorenchyma. Stores water (succulents, xerophytes), food (potato – starch; apple – sugar); absorption, buoyancy, secretion.
  • Collenchyma – living, beneath epidermis; elongated cells with unevenly thickened, non-lignified primary walls; mechanical support to growing organs.
  • Sclerenchyma – thick, often lignified walls; dead at maturity; two kinds: fibres (elongated, pointed; avg 1–3 mm, but 20–550 mm in flax Linum usitatissimum, hemp Cannabis sativa, jute Corchorus capsularis) and sclereids (broad, isodiametric, lignified, prominent pits; common in fruits and seeds).
  • Complex tissues – xylem and phloem (parenchyma + sclerenchyma cells; no collenchyma).
  • Xylem – conducts water and minerals upward from root to leaves; mechanical support. Elements: tracheids (dead, elongated, thick lignified, tapering ends; conduction + support); xylem fibres (support); vessels (dead, long tubes with perforated end walls like a water pipe; water transport); xylem parenchyma (only living element; stores starch and fat).
  • Phloem – conducts food. Elements: sieve elements/sieve tubes (end walls perforated = sieve plates; translocate food from leaves to storage organs); companion cells (attached laterally to sieve tubes); phloem parenchyma (living, stores food); phloem fibres (sclerenchyma, strength).

Cell division – amitosis (direct), mitosis (indirect), meiosis (reduction)

  • Amitosis – simplest; nucleus elongates, constricts and divides, then cytoplasm; in unicellular animals, ageing cells, foetal membranes.
  • Mitosis – first discovered by Fleming (Flemming), 1879; one parent → two identical diploid (2n) cells; equational division. Interphase = resting phase between divisions. Karyokinesis (nuclear division) + cytokinesis (cytoplasm divides by constriction).
  • Phases: Prophase (pro = first) – chromosomes short, thick, visible; centrioles to poles with aster rays; spindle forms; nuclear membrane and nucleolus disappear. Metaphase (meta = after) – chromosomes on the equator (metaphase plate), attached to spindle by centromere. Anaphase (ana = up/back) – centromeres divide, chromatids move to opposite poles. Telophase (telo = end) – chromatin network, spindle disappears, nuclear membrane and nucleolus reappear.
  • Significance of mitosis: equal DNA to diploid daughter cells; growth, organ development; repair of wounds.
  • Meiosis – term by Farmer, 1905; forms gametes; reduction division (2n → n); one parent → four haploid cells. Heterotypic (first) division – diploid → two haploid; homotypic (second) division – mitosis-like, → four haploid.
  • Prophase I stages: Leptotene (long threads, bouquet stage); Zygotene (pairing of homologous chromosomes = synapsis); Pachytene (bivalents, four chromatids = tetrad; chiasmata; crossing over → genetic recombination); Diplotene (homologues repel; terminalisation of chiasmata); Diakinesis (nuclear membrane and nucleolus disappear, spindle appears). Anaphase I: homologue with two chromatids = diad.
  • Significance of meiosis: keeps chromosome number constant in a species; crossing over gives genetic variation.

Excretory system

  • Main excretory product in humans = urea. System: pair of kidneys (form urine) → ureters → urinary bladder (stores) → urethra (voids). Other excretory organs: skin (sweat – water, urea, salts), lungs (CO₂, water vapour); intestine – undigested food.
  • Skin – outermost covering, about 15% of adult body weight; body works at about 37 °C; sweat = water + ammonia, urea, lactic acid, salts (mainly NaCl). Protein is not a constituent of sweat.
  • Kidneys – bean-shaped, reddish brown, on either side of vertebral column attached to dorsal wall; right kidney lower than left (liver); each ~11 cm × 5 cm × 3 cm; covered by renal capsule; outer cortex, inner medulla with renal pyramids; notch = hilum.
  • Ureters carry urine by peristalsis; bladder in pelvic cavity; urethra with sphincters; urination = micturition.
  • Functions of kidney: fluid and electrolyte balance; acid-base balance; osmotic pressure; retain glucose and amino acids.
  • Nephron (uriniferous tubule) = structural and functional unit; >1 million per kidney (~2 million in two). Parts: renal (Malpighian) corpuscle = Bowman's capsule + glomerulus (blood enters by afferent arteriole, leaves by efferent arteriole); renal tubule = proximal convoluted tubule → loop of Henle (U-shaped) → distal convoluted tubule → collecting tubule → renal pelvis → ureter.
  • Urine formation: (1) glomerular filtration (ultrafiltration; filtrate has essential and non-essential substances); (2) tubular reabsorption (selective – glucose, amino acids, vitamins, Na, K, bicarbonate, water); (3) tubular secretion (H⁺, K⁺). Human urine is hypertonic. Healthy person excretes 1–2 litres urine/day.
  • Kidneys filter about 170–180 litres of blood a day; 99% reabsorbed, only 1% becomes urine.
  • Kidney (renal) failure → dialysis with artificial kidney = haemodialysis; else kidney transplantation.
  • First successful kidney transplant – 1954, Joseph E. Murray and colleagues, Peter Bent Brigham Hospital, Boston, USA, between identical twins Ronald and Richard Herrick (recipient Richard lived 8 more years).

Reproductive system

  • Sexual dimorphism in humans. Primary sex organs = gonads (gametes + hormones; dual function); secondary = ducts and glands. Puberty: males 13–14 years, females 11–13 years.
  • Male: testes (outside abdomen; produce sperms and testosterone); epididymis (coiled tubules); Sertoli cells nourish developing sperms; scrotum = thermoregulator – sperms form at 1–3 °C below body temperature; vas deferens → seminal vesicles (seminal plasma rich in fructose – sperm energy, calcium, enzymes) → ejaculatory duct → urethra (common passage for urine and sperm). Accessory glands: seminal vesicles, prostate, Cowper's glands → seminal fluid + sperm = semen.
  • Sperm = smallest cell in the male body; a man produces >500 billion sperms in a lifetime; formation = spermatogenesis.
  • Female: ovaries (almond-shaped, near kidneys; produce ova, hormones oestrogen and progesterone); fallopian tubes (oviducts) with funnel-shaped end and fimbriae that pick up the ovum; uterus – pear-shaped, between bladder and rectum, foetus develops here; lower narrow part = cervix; vagina – birth canal (parturition).
  • Ovum = largest human cell; formation = oogenesis.
  • Glossary: emulsification, mastication (chewing), metabolism, osmoregulation, regurgitation (bringing swallowed food back to mouth).

Biology · Unit 22 – World of Microbes (PDF p. 262–276)

  • Microbiology (Greek mikros = small, bios = life, logy = study) – study of bacteria, fungi, algae, protozoa, viruses. Microbes live even in hot springs, ice sheets, salty low-oxygen waters, arid places. Useful for curd, bread, cheese, alcohol, vaccines, vitamins.
  • Microbes: unicellular (bacteria), multicellular (fungi), acellular (virus).
  • Antonie van Leeuwenhoek – first microbiologist, made his own microscope; in 1674 saw tiny moving organisms in plaque from his own teeth.

Bacteria

  • Single-celled prokaryotes – no true nucleus (nucleoid), no membrane-bound organelles; 1–10 µm long, 0.2–1 µm wide; motile by flagella; also pili.
  • Shapes: cocci (spherical), bacilli (rod), spirilla (spiral).
  • Structure: rigid cell wall; some have slimy polysaccharide capsule; cytoplasm with nucleoid, ribosomes (protein synthesis), DNA; extra-chromosomal circular DNA = plasmid; endospore.

Viruses

  • "Virus" (Latin) = venom / poisonous fluid. Non-cellular, self-replicating parasites: protein coat (60–95%) around nucleic acid – DNA (T4 bacteriophage) or RNA (Tobacco mosaic virus, TMV). Virus particle = virion. Multiply only in living cells; smallest infective agents, 18–400 nm.
  • Living characters: nucleic acid that replicates; multiply in host cells; attack specific hosts. Non-living: inert outside host; no cell membrane, wall, ribosomes, mitochondria; can be crystallised.
  • Viroids = protein-free pathogenic RNA; cause plant diseases.
  • Types: plant viruses (TMV, cauliflower mosaic virus, potato virus); animal viruses (adenovirus, retrovirus – HIV, influenza, polio); bacteriophages – infect bacteria (T4).

Fungi and prions

  • Fungi – no chlorophyll; parasites (on living hosts) or saprophytes (on dead matter); body = thallus. Yeast – single-celled, spherical, 1–5 µm, non-motile. Multicellular body = mycelium of filaments = hyphae (5–10 µm). Cell wall of cellulose or chitin; food stored as glycogen or oil. Reproduction: vegetative (fission, budding, fragmentation), asexual (conidia), sexual (antheridium, oogonium). Examples: yeast, Penicillium, Agaricus.
  • Prions – term coined by Stanley B. Prusiner, 1982; infectious particles of only protein, no nucleic acid; smaller than viruses; rod-shaped, in neurons; degenerate nervous tissue.

Applications of microbes

  • Microbes cycle C, N, O, S, P – "biological scavengers".
  • Biofertilisers (bacteria, cyanobacteria, fungi) fix nitrogen: free-living – Azotobacter, Nitrosomonas, Nostoc; symbiotic – Rhizobium (root nodules of legumes), Frankia.
  • Biocontrol agents (biopesticides): Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) makes "cry" protein toxic to insect larvae; spores sprayed.
  • Industry: wine by fermenting grapes with yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae; curing of coffee/cocoa beans, tea and tobacco leaves by Bacillus megaterium (aroma); curd – Lactobacillus; oxalic, acetic, citric acids by fungus Aspergillus niger; enzymes (lipase, invertase, protease, glucose oxidase); yeasts – rich in vitamin B-complex.
  • Antibiotics – first, penicillin, by Alexander Fleming (book: 1929); used against cholera, diphtheria, pneumonia, typhoid.

Disease (dis = against, ease = comfort)

  • By occurrence: endemic – in a certain area, low incidence (goitre in sub-Himalayan regions); epidemic – many people in a region at the same time (influenza); pandemic – global (AIDS); sporadic – occasional (malaria, cholera – book).
  • Infectious (communicable) – external pathogens (influenza, TB, chickenpox, cholera, pneumonia, malaria). Non-infectious – internal causes: organ malfunction, genetic, hormonal, immune defect (diabetes, coronary heart disease, obesity, cancer, goitre).
  • Reservoir of infection – environment where pathogens thrive without causing disease (water, soil, animals). Incubation period – interval between infection and first symptoms. Infection – entry, development or multiplication of an infectious agent.
  • Pathogens harm by tissue damage (TB bacterium – lungs; hepatitis virus – liver) and toxin secretion.
  • Robert Koch (German) = Father of Bacteriology; 1876 showed anthrax of sheep is caused by Bacillus anthracis (spores in pastures).
  • Days: World Health Day – 7 April; World Malaria Day – 25 April; World AIDS Day – 1 December; World TB Day – 24 March.

Vector-borne diseases (vector = intermediate carrier)

  • Malaria – protozoan Plasmodium: P. vivax, P. malariae, P. falciparum, P. ovale; P. falciparum – malignant and fatal; ~300 million infected yearly; vector = female Anopheles mosquito; headache, chills, shivering, fever subsiding with sweating; treated with quinine.

  • Chikungunya – virus; Aedes aegypti (bites in day time); severe long-lasting joint pain, rashes; incubation 2–12 days; paracetamol.
  • Dengue = "break bone fever" – virus; Aedes aegypti; incubation 5–6 days; high fever, joint/muscle pain, rashes, haemorrhage, fall in platelet count; paracetamol, rest, fluids; papaya leaf extract and Nilavembu Kudineer (AYUSH) raise platelet count.   • Filaria – nematode Wuchereria bancrofti in lymphatic system; vector Culex mosquito; incubation 8–16 months; chronic stage = elephantiasis (legs, scrotum, arms).

Diseases from animals

  • Swine flu – originated in pigs; Influenza virus H1N1; airborne droplets; respiratory system; prevention – nasal spray vaccine, avoid contact, hygiene. First surfaced April 2009; WHO declared pandemic June 2009; India 2015 – over 31,000 infected, 1,900 deaths.
  • Avian influenza (bird flu) – H5N1; poultry, wild and pet birds; incubation 2–7 days; via bird secretions/droppings; fever, conjunctivitis, breathing difficulty. H5N1 emerged 1996 (Southern China, Hong Kong); found in humans 1997; first outbreak December 2003.

Sexually transmitted diseases

  • AIDS (Acquired Immuno Deficiency Syndrome) – HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus), a retrovirus (RNA virus) with reverse transcriptase; attacks WBCs/lymphocytes, weakens immunity. Spread: sexual contact, unscreened blood, infected needles/syringes, mother to foetus. Symptoms: weight loss, prolonged fever, night sweats, chronic diarrhoea. Prevention: disposable syringes, safe sex, blood screening, no sharing razors.
  • HIV first recognised in the USA in 1981 (book prints "Hatai (USA)"); in India first confirmed AIDS case April 1986, Tamil Nadu. AIDS vaccine trial RV 144 – Thailand, 2003 (results 2011).
  • Hepatitis B (serum hepatitis) – HBV; liver inflammation, cirrhosis; mother to baby, sexual contact, saliva, sweat, tears, breast milk, blood; jaundice symptoms; hepatitis B vaccine highly effective. More dangerous than hepatitis A.

Manures and biofertilisers

  • Organic manures from plant debris, animal faeces, microbes; add nitrogen.
  • Farmyard manure – cattle dung, urine, litter, dairy wastes; 0.5% N, 0.2% phosphate, 0.5% potash. Sheep and goat manure – richer: 3% N, 1% P₂O₅, 2% K₂O.
  • Compost – soil conditioner + fertiliser from microbial decomposition of crop residues, animal, food, industrial, municipal wastes.
  • Green manure – undecomposed green material, mostly leguminous plants: sunhemp (Crotalaria juncea), dhaincha (Sesbania aculeata), Sesbania speciosa; improves soil structure, water holding, reduces erosion and weeds, reclaims alkaline soils.
  • Biofertilisers = living microbes that colonise the rhizosphere/plant and increase nutrient supply: Rhizobium – root nodules of legumes, fixes N₂ → ammonia; Azospirillum – maize, barley, oats, sorghum; raises cereals 5–20%, millets 30%, fodder >50%; Azotobacter – wheat, rice, maize, sorghum; also antifungal/antibacterial compounds; Mycorrhizae – fungi in symbiosis with roots, increase phosphorus uptake (citrus, papaya); Azolla – free-floating aquatic fern with cyanobacterium Anabaena, "live floating nitrogen factory".
  • TN Govt launched a Biofertiliser Scheme (natural farming, soil fertility).

Mushroom cultivation – "wealth out of waste"

  • Mushroom = fungus of Basidiomycetes; rich in protein, fibre, vitamins, minerals; >3000 types: button (Agaricus bisporus), oyster (Pleurotus spp.), paddy straw (Volvariella volvacea). Cultivation takes 1–3 months. Plant body = mycelium.
  • Stages: composting (paddy straw + cow dung + fertiliser, ~50 °C for a week) → spawning (spawn = mushroom "seed", mycelium grown on grains) → casing (thin soil layer) → pinning (small white buds = pins) → harvesting (grow best at 15–23 °C; 3 cm a week; first flush in third week).
  • Preservation: freezing, drying, canning, vacuum cooling, gamma radiation and storage at 15 °C.

Soil-less culture

  • Hydroponics – growing plants without soil in mineral nutrient solutions; demonstrated by German botanist Julius von Sachs (book prints 1980; it was 1860 – extra); used commercially for seedless cucumber and tomato; plants supported from above. Importance: saves water and nutrients, controlled growth, useful in deserts and Arctic regions.
  • Aeroponics – high-tech hydroponics; growth medium = air; roots hang and are misted with nutrients every few minutes (short-cycle timer).
  • Aquaponics – aquaculture + hydroponics: fish wastes → (nitrifying bacteria) nitrites → nitrates used by plants; water recirculated. Grows Chinese cabbage, lettuce, basil, coriander, spinach, tomato, capsicum, chillies, cauliflower, broccoli, brinjal.

Dairy farming

Aquaculture and pisciculture

  • Aquaculture – rearing fishes, prawns, shrimps, crabs, lobsters, edible and pearl oysters, seaweeds under controlled conditions; aims at the Blue Revolution; export earnings, rural employment. Freshwater (ponds, rivers, dams, lakes: tilapia, carps, catfish, air-breathing fish) and mariculture / sea farming (shrimps, pearl and edible oysters, mussels, salmon, sea bass, milkfish, mullets).

  • Pisciculture = fish culture. Types: extensive (large area, low density, natural food); intensive (small area, high density, artificial feed); monoculture (one species); polyculture / composite fish culture (several species); integrated fish farming (with paddy, poultry, cattle, pigs, ducks).
  • Ponds: breeding pond (eggs form frothy mass) → hatching pits/hapas → nursery pond (hatchlings after 2–7 days → fry, ~60 days to 2–2.5 cm) → rearing pond (~3 months, 10–15 cm, fry → fingerlings) → stocking (culture/production) pond (to marketable size).

  • Fish nutrition: easily digestible protein; essential amino acids lysine, methionine; PUFA; Ca, P, Fe, Na, K, Mg; vitamins A, D and B-complex.
  • Prawn culture: marine (shrimp culture) – Penaeus indicus, P. monodon; freshwater – Macrobrachium rosenbergii, M. malcolmsonii. Methods: seed collection and hatchery; paddy-cum-prawn culture = "Pokkali" culture – oldest traditional method in Kerala, in coastal paddy fields after harvest.

Vermitechnology

  • Vermiculture – rearing earthworms to make compost. Species: Perionyx excavatus (Indian blue worm), Eisenia fetida (red worm), Eudrilus eugeniae (African night crawler).
  • Vermicompost = worm excreta (castings) – fine granular, dark brown. Materials: crop residues, sugarcane trash, rice straw/husk, tea, coir and tobacco wastes, leaf litter, fruit/vegetable waste, animal dung, poultry droppings, biogas slurry.
  • Bin method: bedding (cardboard, leaves, paddy husk, straw, sawdust, manure) + little soil/sand (grit), moistened; worms added, organic waste fed; covered with coconut leaves or gunny bags; ready in 60 days.
  • Advantages: rich in nutrients, improves water holding, prevents erosion, has vitamins, enzymes, growth regulators, beneficial microflora, free from pathogens and toxins.

Apiculture (bee keeping)

  • Colony: queen – largest, fertile female, lays eggs (from fertilised egg); drones – fertile males from unfertilised eggs (larger than workers); workers – sterile females, smallest; collect honey, nurse young, clean comb, defend, regulate temperature.
  • Indigenous bees: Apis dorsata (rock/wild bee), Apis florea (little bee), Apis indica (Indian bee – commonly used in apiculture). Exotic: Apis mellifera (Italian bee), Apis adamsoni (African bee).
  • Comb – secreted by wax glands in the abdomen of worker bees; vertical sheet with double layer of hexagonal cells.
  • Honey formation: nectar → honey sac; sucrose + acidic secretion + enzyme action → honey. Products: honey, bee wax, bee pollen, royal jelly, propolis, bee venom.
  • Honey: sweetness from dextrose and sucrose; amino acids, B-complex, ascorbic acid, minerals; formic acid = preservative; enzyme invertase. Uses: antiseptic, antibacterial, blood purifier, builds haemoglobin, used in Ayurveda and Unani, relieves cough/cold/sore throat, heals ulcers, aids digestion.
  • Bee visits 50–100 flowers per trip; makes only 1/12 teaspoon of honey in its lifetime; 1 kg honey = 3200 calories.

Biology · Unit 24 – Environmental Science (PDF p. 294–305)

  • Quote: "Nature has the power to refresh and renew" – Helen Keller. Issues: pollution, global warming, ozone depletion, acid rain, deforestation, landslide, drought, desertification.
  • Biosphere = part of Earth where life exists. Biotic factors (living organisms) and abiotic factors (temperature, pressure, water, soil, air, sunlight – affect survival and reproduction).
  • Biogeochemical cycles (bio = life, geo = earth) = cyclic flow of nutrients between living and non-living parts: water, nitrogen, carbon cycles.

Water (hydrological) cycle

  • Evaporation – liquid → gas below boiling point (oceans, lakes, rivers). Sublimation – polar ice sheets and mountain ice caps → vapour directly. Transpiration – water vapour from plants through stomata. Condensation – vapour → tiny droplets at high altitude → clouds and fog. Precipitation – drizzle, rain, snow, hail. Runoff – surface flow to rivers, seas. Infiltration – water enters soil, raises groundwater. Percolation – flow through soil and porous/fractured rock.
  • Human impacts: urbanisation, plastic dumping, water pollution, deforestation.

Nitrogen cycle

  • Nitrogen – component of proteins, DNA, chlorophyll; atmosphere has about 78% nitrogen, unusable directly (needed as ammonia, amino acids, nitrates).
  • Nitrogen fixation – inert N₂ → reactive compounds by bacteria and blue-green algae; legumes (pea, beans) with Rhizobium in root nodules. Assimilation – plants use nitrates for proteins, nucleic acids; animals get them by eating. Ammonification – putrefying bacteria and fungi decompose wastes (urea, uric acid, ammonia) and dead bodies into ammonium compounds. Nitrification – ammonium → soluble nitrates by nitrifying bacteria. Denitrification – Pseudomonas reduces soil nitrate to N₂ gas.

Carbon cycle

  • Elemental carbon: charcoal, diamond, graphite; combined: CO, CO₂, carbonates. CO₂ enters plants by photosynthesis → herbivores → carnivores; returned by respiration, decomposition, burning fossil fuels, volcanic activity.
  • Human impact: fossil fuels and deforestation add CO₂ – a greenhouse gas → greenhouse effect and global warming. (Nitrogen is not a greenhouse gas.)

Adaptations of plants (adaptation = feature enabling life in a habitat)

  • Hydrophytes (in/near water) – challenges: excess water, currents, changing levels, buoyancy. Adaptations: roots poorly developed (Hydrilla) or absent (Wolffia); plant body reduced (Lemna); submerged leaves narrow/finely divided (Hydrilla); floating leaves with long stalks (lotus); air chambers for buoyancy (Eichhornia – swollen spongy petiole).
  • Xerophytes (dry habitat) – absorb and retain water, cut transpiration and water use. Adaptations: very deep roots (Calotropis); succulent water-storing tissue (Opuntia, Aloe vera); small waxy leaves (Acacia); leaves modified into spines (Opuntia); short life cycle when moisture is available.
  • Mesophytes (neither too wet nor too dry) – well-developed roots with root caps; straight branched stem; broad thin leaves; waxy cuticle; stomata close in extreme heat/wind.

Adaptations of animals

  • Bat – only mammal that can fly; lives in caves, trees, logs, rock crevices; controls insects, pollinates plants. Nocturnal (thin black wing membrane = patagium would absorb heat by day); flight – forelimbs modified into wings (skin stretched between long finger bones), tail controls flight, powerful muscles, hind-limb tendons grip when hanging upside down; hibernation (winter inactivity, lowered body temperature and metabolism); echolocation – emits ultrasonic sounds and uses echoes to locate prey.
  • Earthworm – keeps soil fertile (aeration, water infiltration); streamlined cylindrical segmented body for burrows; mucus-covered moist skin (soil does not stick; oxygenation of blood); circular and longitudinal muscles; setae on each segment for movement and anchoring; aestivation – summer dormancy in hot/dry soil (moves deeper, secretes mucus, lowers metabolism); nocturnal and photophobic – no eyes, light-sensitive photoreceptor cells in skin. Called "Farmer's friend"; castings = vermicompost.
  • Glossary: hibernation – winter inactivity; aestivation – summer inactivity.

Water conservation

  • = preservation, control and management of water resources. Importance: efficient use, enough usable water, less pollution, energy saving.
  • Industrial: dry cooling systems; reuse cooling water for irrigation. Agricultural: lined/covered canals; sprinklers and drip irrigation; drought-resistant, low-water crops; mulching. Domestic: bucket bath instead of shower, low-flow taps, recycled water for lawns, fix leaks, reuse water.
  • World Water Day – 22 March.
  • Strategies: rainwater harvesting, improved irrigation, traditional water-harvesting structures, less domestic use, awareness, farm ponds, recycling.
  • Farm pond – dug-out structure with inlet and outlet to collect farm runoff for irrigation. Advantages: water without waiting for rain, less soil erosion, groundwater recharge, better drainage, excavated soil for fields, fish rearing, water for domestic use and livestock.
  • Water recycling – reusing treated wastewater (irrigation, landscape, industry, toilet flushing, groundwater recharge).

IUCN (International Union for Conservation of Nature and Natural Resources)

  • Global authority on the status of the natural world. Founded 5 October 1948 at Gland, Switzerland. Vision: "A just world that values and conserves nature". Mission: influence, encourage and assist societies to conserve integrity and diversity of nature and ensure equitable, ecologically sustainable use.
  • Best known for the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species.
