Introduction and need for a Constitution

  • Constitution – fundamental law of a country reflecting the principles on which its government is based; "vehicle of a Nation's progress".
  • The concept of constitution first originated in the U.S.A.
  • All democratic countries have a constitution; it tells the fundamental nature of our society.

Making of the Indian Constitution

  • Framed by a Constituent Assembly set up under the Cabinet Mission Plan, 1946.
  • 389 members: Provinces 292, (Princely) States 93, Chief Commissioner's provinces 3, Baluchistan 1.
  • First meeting: 9 December 1946. Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha (oldest member) – temporary President; after his death Dr. Rajendra Prasad elected President.
  • Vice-Presidents: H.C. Mukherjee and V.T. Krishnamachari.
  • 11 sessions, 166 days of meetings; 2,473 amendments presented.
  • Drafting Committee chairman Dr. B.R. Ambedkar – 'Father of the Constitution of India'.
  • Adopted on 26 November 1949 – Preamble, 22 Parts, 395 Articles, 8 Schedules. Came into force 26 January 1950 = Republic Day.
  • Do you know – Prem Behari Narain Raizada was the calligrapher; original Constitution handwritten in flowing italic style.

Salient features

  • Lengthiest written constitution in the world.
  • Borrowed most provisions from constitutions of various countries.
  • Partly rigid and partly flexible.
  • Federal system of government; secular state; independent judiciary.
  • Universal Adult Franchise – vote to all citizens above 18 without discrimination.

Preamble

  • Introduction/preface; ideals, objectives, basic principles; called the 'key to the Constitution'.
  • Based on the 'Objective Resolution' drafted by Jawaharlal Nehru, adopted by the Constituent Assembly on 22 January 1947.
  • Amended only once – 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 added three words: socialist, secular, integrity.
  • Begins "We, the People of India" – people are the source of the Constitution.
  • India = Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic (this order); aims at social, economic and political justice.
  • Do you know – Liberty, Equality, Fraternity were slogans of the French Revolution (1789); given importance in the Preamble.

Citizenship

  • 'Citizen' from Latin 'Civis' = resident of a City State.
  • Single and uniform citizenship; Articles 5 to 11, Part II.
  • Citizenship Act, 1955 – acquisition and loss after commencement of the Constitution; textbook: amended nine times so far.

Fundamental Rights (Part III, Articles 12–35)

  • Inspired by the Constitution of USA. Originally seven FRs; now six.
  • Part III = 'Magna Carta of India'. Some FRs for all persons, some only for citizens.
  • Right to Property (Art. 31) deleted by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978; now a legal right under Article 300-A, Part XII.

Right to Constitutional Remedies (Art. 32)

  • Writ – order or command issued by a court in writing under its seal.
  • Both Supreme Court and High Courts can issue five writs; hence SC = "Guardian of the Constitution".
  • Ambedkar: Article 32 is "the heart and soul of the Constitution".

  • Suspension of FRs – on Proclamation of Emergency under Article 352, Article 19 freedoms are automatically suspended; President can suspend other FRs by specific orders, which must be approved by Parliament.

Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV, Articles 36–51)

  • No classification in the Constitution; by content: socialistic, Gandhian, liberal-intellectual.
  • Not enforceable by courts, but fundamental for governance; aim at social welfare.
  • Ambedkar called them 'novel features' of the Constitution.
  • 86th Amendment Act, 2002 – made elementary education a FR under Art. 21A; changed Art. 45 to early childhood care and education until six years.

Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, Article 51A)

  • Inspired by the Constitution of the former USSR (Russia).
  • Sardar Swaran Singh Committee set up in 1976 (by the Congress party) to recommend duties.
  • Added by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 – new Part IVA, single Article 51A; textbook: code of eleven duties.
  • 11th duty, Art. 51A(k) – added by the 86th Amendment Act, 2002: parents/guardians to provide education opportunities to child/ward aged 6–14.
  1. Abide by the Constitution; respect its ideals, institutions, National Flag and National Anthem.
  2. Cherish and follow noble ideals of the freedom struggle.
  3. Uphold and protect sovereignty, unity and integrity of India.
  4. Defend the country and render national service when called.
  5. Promote harmony and common brotherhood; renounce practices derogatory to women's dignity.
  6. Value and preserve the rich heritage of our composite culture.
  7. Protect and improve natural environment – forests, lakes, rivers, wildlife; compassion for living creatures.
  8. Develop scientific temper, humanism, spirit of inquiry and reform.
  9. Safeguard public property; abjure violence.
  10. Strive towards excellence in all spheres.
  11. Provide opportunities for education to child/ward between 6 and 14.

Centre–State Relations

  • Legislative – Parliament legislates for whole/any part of India (incl. UTs). Seventh Schedule – three lists: Union 97, State 66, Concurrent 47 (original). At present 100, 61, 52 (Do you know).
  • Parliament – exclusive power on Union List; State – exclusive on State List; both on Concurrent List; in conflict, Parliament's law prevails.
  • 42nd Amendment, 1976 moved five subjects from State to Concurrent List: education, forests, weights and measures, protection of wild animals and birds, administration of justice (constitution and organisation of all courts except SC and HCs).
  • Administrative – State's executive power limited to its territory and legislative competence; Union has exclusive executive power on matters of exclusive Parliamentary law and on treaties/agreements.
  • Financial – Articles 268–293, Part XII; certain taxes collected by Centre are divided between Centre and states on recommendation of the Finance Commission appointed by the President under Article 280.
  • Sarkaria Commission – appointed by PM Indira Gandhi in 1983 on Centre–State relations; Centre implemented 180 of 247 recommendations.
  • Rajamannar Committee – 1969, appointed by the Tamil Nadu Government; three-member, chaired by Dr. P.V. Rajamannar, to examine Centre–State relations.

Official Language

  • Part XVII, Articles 343–351.
  • Eighth Schedule: initially 14 languages; now 22.
  • Classical languages – category created in 2004. Textbook lists six: Tamil (2004), Sanskrit (2005), Telugu (2008), Kannada (2008), Malayalam (2013), Odia (2014). (extra: in Oct 2024 Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese and Bengali were added – total 11.)

Amendment of the Constitution

  • Article 368, Part XX – Parliament's power and procedure.
  • Bill may be introduced in either House; passed in each House by majority of total membership and two-thirds of members present and voting; then President shall give assent.
  • Only Parliament can amend; State legislatures cannot initiate amendments.
  • Three ways: (1) simple majority of Parliament; (2) special majority of Parliament; (3) special majority + ratification by half of the state legislatures.
  • 42nd Amendment = "mini Constitution".

Constitutional Reform Commissions

  • National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution – set up in 2000 by Govt resolution, headed by M.N. Venkatachaliah.
  • Punchhi Commission – April 2007, three-member, headed by former CJI M.M. Punchhi; relative roles of levels of government and their inter-relations (Centre–State).

Introduction

  • Central Government = supreme government; HQ New Delhi. Federal arrangement given India's vastness and plurality.
  • Articles 52 to 78, Part V – Union Executive.
  • Three organs: Union Executive, Legislature (Parliament), Judiciary (Supreme Court).
  • Union Executive = President, Vice-President, Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister, and the Attorney-General.

The President of India

  • Nominal executive; chief executive of the Union; First citizen; supreme commander of the armed forces; constitutional head of the Union Executive.
  • Article 53 – executive power of the Union vested in the President, exercised directly or through subordinate officers.
  • First President: Dr. Rajendra Prasad.

Do you know – President resides at Rashtrapati Bhavan, New Delhi (residence and office in the same building); two other office-cum-residences used at least once a year: 'The Retreat Building', Shimla and 'Rashtrapati Nilayam', Hyderabad – symbolise unity of North and South.

Powers of the President

  • Executive – Article 77: every executive action in the President's name. Appoints PM and ministers (portfolios on PM's advice); Governors, CJI and judges of SC and HCs, Attorney General, CAG, CEC and Election Commissioners, UPSC Chairman and members, ambassadors and High Commissioners.
  • Military – Article 53(2): supreme command of Defence Forces vested in the President, regulated by law.
  • Diplomatic – appoints Indian diplomats, receives foreign diplomats (ambassador presents credentials); all treaties in the President's name.
  • Emergency – National (Art. 352), State (Art. 356), Financial (Art. 360).
  • Legislative – addresses Parliament after general election and at start of first session each year (like a "Speech from the Throne"); summons Parliament at least twice a year; sends messages to Houses; bills become law only after his assent; money bills need his prior approval; prorogues sessions; can dissolve the Lok Sabha; nominates 12 eminent persons (literature, science, sports, art, social service) to Rajya Sabha.
  • Financial – Budget presented in Lok Sabha by Finance Minister with his permission; Contingency Fund of India at his disposal; no demand for grant without his recommendation; constitutes a Finance Commission every five years.
  • Judicial – Article 72: pardons, reprieves, respites, remissions; commute sentences.

Vice-President

  • Second highest office; next to President in warrant of precedence; modelled on the American Vice-President.
  • Article 63 – provides for a Vice-President; created to maintain political continuity.
  • First Vice-President: Dr. S. Radhakrishnan.
  • Qualifications: citizen; 35 years; no office of profit; qualified to be a Rajya Sabha member.
  • Article 66(1) – indirect election; term 5 years; eligible for re-election; may end early by resignation, death or removal. (Meanwhile the Deputy Chairman of Rajya Sabha performs the Chairman's duties.)
  • Ex-officio Chairman of Rajya Sabha – regulates proceedings, decides order, admissibility of resolutions/questions, adjourns for grave disorder.
  • Acts as President during President's illness/absence for a maximum of six months.
  • Removal – resolution of the Rajya Sabha passed by a majority of all its then members and agreed to by the Lok Sabha; at least 14 days' notice.
  • Casting vote – Article 100: only when there is a tie in the Rajya Sabha.
  • Do you know – if both President and VP posts are vacant, the Chief Justice of India acts as President; happened in 1969 – CJI M. Hidayatullah.

Prime Minister and Council of Ministers

  • Article 74(1) – Council of Ministers headed by the PM to aid and advise the President.
  • PM post follows the Westminster model (England's Parliament sits at Westminster).
  • President appoints the leader of the majority party in Lok Sabha as PM; other ministers on PM's advice. If no absolute majority, President may summon any leader who can form a ministry.
  • President administers oath of office and secrecy. A non-member can be minister but must be elected to Parliament within six months.
  • Ministers are individually and collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha.
  • Size of Council of Ministers incl. PM limited to 15% of total Lok Sabha members (91st Amendment, 2003 – extra).
  • First PM: Jawaharlal Nehru.

Duties of the PM (Article 78): decides ranks and departments of ministers; fixes dates and agenda of Cabinet meetings and presides; informally consults senior colleagues; supervises ministers; communicates all Council decisions and legislative proposals to the President; link between President and Council; leader of the nation and chief spokesperson; represents India at Commonwealth, NAM summit, SAARC.

Attorney General of India

  • Article 76; highest law officer; appointed by the President; must be qualified to be a Supreme Court judge; removable by the President at any time; resigns to the President.
  • Advises Govt on legal matters referred by the President; right of audience in all courts in India.
  • Can speak and take part in proceedings of both Houses, joint sittings and parliamentary committees – but no right to vote; enjoys privileges and immunities of an MP.

Judiciary – Supreme Court

  • Third organ; "Supreme Court is the Guardian of the Constitution"; autonomous of legislature and executive; integrated judiciary = single judicial hierarchy.
  • Inaugurated 28 January 1950, New Delhi; succeeded the Federal Court (Government of India Act, 1935).
  • In 1950: 8 judges including the Chief Justice. (At present 34 including CJI – extra.)

  • Qualifications: citizen; HC judge for 5 years OR HC advocate for 10 years OR a distinguished jurist in the President's opinion.
  • Hold office till 65 years; resign in writing to the President; removed by Parliament through impeachment.
  • Permanent seat New Delhi; may sit elsewhere as decided by CJI with President's approval.

Powers of the Governor

  • Executive: appoints CM (leader of majority party) and other ministers on CM's recommendation; Chairman and members of State Public Service Commission; State Election Commissioner (fixes service conditions, tenure); Chancellor of universities and appoints Vice-Chancellors; rules the State directly under President's Rule.
  • Legislative (integral part of legislature but member of neither House): summons, prorogues, dissolves the Assembly; addresses first session after each general election and first session each year; sends messages; appoints a member to preside if Speaker and Deputy Speaker posts are vacant; nominates one Anglo-Indian to the Assembly (textbook; ended by the 104th Amendment, 2020 – extra); nominates 1/6 of Legislative Council (literature, science, art, co-operative movement, social service); every bill needs his signature – may assent, withhold assent, or return for reconsideration; ordinances under Article 213 when legislature not in session – must be approved within six months, may be withdrawn anytime; lays reports of State Finance Commission, State PSC and CAG (state accounts) before legislature.
  • Financial: annual and supplementary budgets prepared and introduced; Money bills only with his prior recommendation; advances from State Contingency Fund; constitutes a Finance Commission every five years to review finances of panchayats and municipalities.
  • Judicial: appoints the State's chief law officer (textbook says "Attorney-General of the State"; the post is the Advocate General – extra); appoints, posts and promotes District Judges in consultation with the High Court; consulted by the President on appointing the HC Chief Justice; can pardon, commute or reprieve – except death sentence (textbook).
  • Discretionary: reserve a bill for the President; invite any party leader to form ministry when no clear majority; dismiss a ministry that cannot prove confidence.
  • Emergency: recommends President's Rule under Article 356; then administers the State as President's representative.
  • Privileges – Article 361(1): not answerable to any court for official acts; no arrest or imprisonment process from any court during his term.

3.2 Chief Minister

  • Governor = nominal executive, head of the State; CM = real executive, head of the government.
  • Appointed by the Governor (Article 164(1)); term not fixed – holds office while he has majority support (normally 5 years).
  • Re: Council of Ministers – recommends ministers; allocates portfolios; reshuffles; presides over meetings; guides and coordinates ministers.
  • Re: Governor – advises on appointment of Advocate General, State Election Commissioner, Chairman/members of State PSC, State Planning Commission, State Finance Commission.
  • Re: Legislature – advises summoning/proroguing; announces policies; introduces bills; may recommend dissolution of the Assembly anytime.

Council of Ministers

  • Collectively responsible to the State Legislature; all must be members of the legislature (non-members within 6 months); resigns if a no-confidence motion passes.
  • Article 163(1) – Council with CM at head to aid and advise the Governor except in his discretionary functions.
  • Article 164(1A) – total ministers incl. CM ≤ 15% of Assembly strength. Tamil Nadu (234 MLAs) → up to 36 ministers.
  • Functions: formulate and implement policies; legislative programme and important bills; financial policy and tax structure; appoint Heads of Departments; inter-state disputes; expenditure from state reserves; decide bills (ordinary and money); each minister supervises his department; finalise the Budget (Annual Financial Statement).
  • Cabinet – smaller body of cabinet ministers; nucleus and real centre of authority. Works through cabinet committees: standing (permanent) and ad hoc (temporary).

  • Lower house represents people; upper house represents special interests – teachers, graduates, local governments.

Unit 4 – India's Foreign Policy (PDF p. 275–283)

  • Foreign policy – policy to safeguard and promote national interests in external affairs, bilaterally and multilaterally; reflects traditional values and national policies. Interdependence is an incontrovertible fact of international relations.
  • Tools: treaties and executive agreements, appointing ambassadors, foreign aid, international trade, armed forces. Diplomacy = the instrument for implementing foreign policy.

4.1 Main objectives: national security; national prosperity; more friendly nations; world peace and peaceful co-existence; economic development.

4.2 Panchsheel (Sanskrit: panch = five, sheel = virtues)

  • Five Principles of Peaceful Coexistence signed between India (PM Jawaharlal Nehru) and China (Premier Chou En-Lai) on 28 April 1954.
  • Principles: (1) mutual respect for each other's territorial integrity and sovereignty; (2) mutual non-aggression; (3) mutual non-interference; (4) equality and co-operation for mutual benefit; (5) peaceful co-existence.
  • Incorporated in the Bandung Declaration – Afro-Asian Conference, 1955, Indonesia.

4.3 Basic determinants: geography and size; history, traditions, philosophy; natural resources; compulsion of economic development; political stability and govt structure; need for peace, disarmament and nuclear non-proliferation; military strength; international milieu.

Foreign policy in the 1950s–60s – most idealistic phase under Nehru; new nations pressed to align with USA or USSR; Nehru opposed superpower rivalry and chose Non-alignment in the bipolar Cold War, trying to form a third bloc. Nehru: non-alignment means "not tying yourself off with military blocs… trying to maintain friendly relations with all countries".

4.4 Non-Aligned Movement (NAM), 1961

  • Term 'Non-alignment' coined by V.K. Krishna Menon in his speech at the UN in 1953; most important feature of India's foreign policy.
  • NAM: 120 member countries, 17 observer states, 10 international organisations; transformed from a political to an economic movement.
  • Founding fathers: Nehru (India), Tito (Yugoslavia), Nasser (Egypt), Sukarno (Indonesia), Kwame Nkrumah (Ghana).
  • Non-alliance ≠ neutrality; = freedom to decide issues independently; ≠ demilitarisation; meant de-escalation of conflicts.

Cold War era

  • Indo-Soviet Treaty, 1971 – 20-year pact of 'peace, friendship and co-operation'.
  • 1974 – India's first nuclear test at Pokhran (Rajasthan) under the Subterranean Nuclear Explosions Project, in response to China's 1964 test at Lop Nor.
  • Avoiding military blocs was a necessity – India had to redeem itself from acute poverty, illiteracy, chaotic socio-economic conditions.

1990s developments – fall of USSR; LPG (Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation) order; India joined GATT-related pacts. Shifts: better relations with China – Look East Policy (1992); second nuclear test at Pokhran (1998); defence procurement with Israel; energy diplomacy with Arab countries and Iran; agreeing to the US missile defence programme; vote against Iran at the IAEA.

21st century – foreign policy to enable domestic transformation; joined G-20, IBSA (India, Brazil, South Africa), BRICS. Challenges: growth, energy security, climate change, scarce resources, food and water security, pandemics, migration.

4.5 Basic concepts: national interest; world peace; disarmament; cordial relations; peaceful settlement; independence of thought and action (NAM); equality; anti-colonialism, anti-imperialism, anti-racism.

  • Disarmament – global non-proliferation dominant theme; supported UN disarmament; 1974 and 1998 tests only for strategic purposes.
  • Nuclear doctrine – two themes: No first use and Credible minimum deterrence; never against a non-nuclear state. Indo-US civilian nuclear deal = significant progress.

4.6 SAARC (1985) – South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation; 8 members: Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Nepal, Maldives, Pakistan, Sri Lanka. Aims: welfare economics, collective self-reliance, socio-cultural development. SAARC Disaster Management Centre – New Delhi. SAARC satellite – communication-cum-meteorology satellite proposed by ISRO.

4.7 Change and continuity

  • Neighbourhood First – neighbourhood as widening concentric circles; priority to immediate neighbours and Indian Ocean island states like Maldives; peaceful periphery essential.
  • Bridging diplomacy and development – technology, capital, market access, natural resources.
  • Look East → Act East – "South East Asia begins with North East India"; Myanmar = land bridge to ASEAN; three elements: physical connectivity, commercial, security.
  • Economic development – accelerated, balanced, inclusive growth is the primary goal.
  • India as a leading power – member of G20, East Asia Summit, BRICS; aspires to permanent UN Security Council seat.
  • Principle: "in international relations there is no permanent friend and no permanent foe, only the interests are permanent". After independence India joined the Commonwealth.

1.2 What is Democracy? – people choose rulers; only elected leaders rule; freedom to express views, organise and protest.

  • Meaning – supreme power vested in the people; they elect representatives directly or indirectly through free, fair, periodic elections.
  • Word origin – Greek demos (people) + cratia (power) = "power of the people".
  • Mahatma Gandhi: "True democracy cannot be worked by twenty men sitting at the centre. It has to be worked from below by the people of every village."
  • Abraham Lincoln (President of USA): democracy = "government of the people, by the people and for the people".

1.2.3 Salient features: (1) elected representatives with final decision-making power; (2) free and fair elections; (3) voting right of equal value to all who are 18; (4) fundamental rights and protection of individual freedom.

1.2.4 Evolution

  • Democracy began 2,500 years ago in city-states of ancient Greece.
  • Democratic institutions in India as early as the Vedic period.
  • Chanakya's Arthashastra – autonomous village community was the basic unit of local government.
  • Kudavolai system – village administration (voting) of the later Cholas in ancient Tamil Nadu.
  • Values of democracy: freedom, equality, fraternity, accountability, transparency, trust.

1.2.5 Forms of democratic government – Parliamentary (India, England): electorate → legislature → executive. Presidential (USA, France): electorate elects both legislature and executive.

1.2.6 Types of democracy

  • Direct (pure) democracy – people directly express their will on public affairs. Ex: ancient Greek city-states, Switzerland.
  • Indirect / representative democracy – through elected representatives. Ex: India, USA, UK.

1.2.7 Merits: responsible and accountable govt; equality and fraternity; sense of responsibility among people; local self-government; development for all; popular sovereignty; cooperation. Demerits: indirect/representative nature; low interest → low turnout; sometimes unstable govt; delay in decisions.

1.2.8 Democracy in India

  • India = parliamentary democracy; two important elements: participation of people in decision making and consent of citizens.
  • India = largest democracy in the world; five principles: sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic, republic.
  • Universal adult suffrage – every citizen not less than 18 can vote; no discrimination by caste, creed, religion, region, gender, education. (Franchise = right to vote; universal franchise creates political equality – exercise.)
  • Parliament House (Do you know) – designed by British architects Edwin Lutyens and Herbert Baker in 1912–13; construction began 1921, ended 1927.

1.2.9 Elections in India

  • India has a quasi-federal government – elected representatives at federal, state and local levels.
  • General elections conducted by the Election Commission of India.
  • President appoints the Prime Minister – the leader who enjoys majority in the Lok Sabha.
  • Two Houses: Lok Sabha (Lower House / House of the People) and Rajya Sabha (Upper House / Council of States).
  • Lok Sabha members – directly elected, general elections once every 5 years.
  • Rajya Sabha members – elected by an electoral college of elected MLAs of states and UTs; President nominates 12 for art, literature, science, social service.

1.2.10 First elections

  • First Lok Sabha elections: 25 Oct 1951 – 21 Feb 1952; INC won 364 of 489 seats; Jawaharlal Nehru = first democratically elected PM.
  • British India general elections 1920 (box) – to Imperial Legislative Council and Provincial Councils; the first elections in the country's history.
  • Women got the right to vote in 1921 – general election in Madras Presidency.

1.2.11 Major challenges to Indian democracy: illiteracy; poverty; gender discrimination; regionalism; casteism, communalism, religious fundamentalism; corruption; criminalisation of politics; political violence.

1.2.12 Conditions for success: empowering the poor and illiterate; elected people not misusing position and public wealth; eradicating social evils; impartial, efficient press; strong public opinion; tolerance and communal harmony; awareness of fundamental rights; vigilance over representatives; powerful and responsible opposition. Values: equality, freedom, social justice, accountability, respect for all.

2.2 Political parties – link between government and the people.

  • Meaning – organisation of people with a certain ideology and agenda to contest elections and hold power. Three components: a leader, active members, followers.
  • Party systems: Single-party (no opposition) – China, Cuba, former USSR; Two-party – USA, UK; Multi-party – India, Sri Lanka, France, Italy.
  • Types in India (by area of influence): national and state parties (each may be ruling or opposition).
  • National party – a party recognised as a state party in at least four states. Every party registers with the Election Commission (EC gives recognition); recognised parties get a unique symbol usable only by the official candidate. In 2017 there were seven recognised national parties.
  • State parties (regional parties) – recognised by ECI on a certain % of votes or number of seats in Assembly / Lok Sabha elections.

Criteria for national party recognition (any one):

  1. At least 6% votes in at least four states and members elected to the Lok Sabha;
  2. At least 2% of Lok Sabha members from at least three states;
  3. Recognition as a state party in at least four states.

Functions of parties: contest elections; put forward policies and programmes; decisive role in making laws; form and run governments; losers act as Opposition; shape public opinion; link between people and government machinery.

2.2.4 Role of Opposition

  • Leader of the Opposition enjoys the rank of a Cabinet Minister.
  • As Chairman of the Public Accounts Committee questions government departments and examines use of public money.
  • Helps select Chairman and members of the Central Vigilance Commission and Chairperson and members of the Information Commission.

2.3 Pressure groups

  • Term originated in the USA. Group organised to promote and defend common interest by pressuring the government to change public policy.
  • Also called interest groups or vested groups. Unlike parties, they neither contest elections nor try to capture power; non-aligned with parties.
  • Many exist in India, but not developed to the same extent as in the USA or Britain, France, Germany.
  • Categories (10): business groups, trade unions, agrarian groups, professional associations, student organisations, religious organisations, tribal organisations, linguistic groups, ideology-based groups, environmental protection groups.
  • Functions: representation, political participation, education, policy formulation, policy implementation.
  • Examples: FICCI (Federation of Indian Chamber of Commerce and Industry), AITUC (All India Trade Union Congress), All India Kisan Sabha, IMA (Indian Medical Association), AISF (All India Students Federation), All India Sikh Students Federation, Young Badaga Association, Tamil Sangam, Tamil Nadu Vivasayigal Sangam, Narmada Bachao Andolan.
  • Political participation – "informal face of politics": petitions, marches, demonstrations; attractive to young people.
  • Education – research, websites, comments on policy, use of academics/scientists/celebrities.
  • Policy formulation – not policy makers, but sources of information; consulted by government.

2.4 Mobilisation and people's participation – mobilisation for socially productive activity and disaster relief (earthquake, tsunami, floods); democratic participation – supporting tax collection, environment norms, cleanliness, sanitation, immunisation like pulse polio.

Introduction – Gandhi in South Africa

  • 7 June 1893 – Gandhi, travelling to Pretoria with a first-class ticket, was thrown off the train at Pietermaritzburg because of his skin colour → fight against racial oppression → non-violent Satyagraha.
  • Discrimination may be on race, colour, gender, place of birth, caste, religion.

End of Apartheid

  • Apartheid – highest form of discrimination, in South Africa; rule of minority whites over majority non-whites; residence fixed by race.
  • Nelson Mandela imprisoned for 27 years; released in 1990 by President F. W. de Klerk.
  • 1994 – first multiracial general election; Mandela led the African National Congress to victory and became President.

3.1 What are Human Rights?

  • UNO definition: rights inherent to all human beings regardless of race, gender, nationality, ethnicity, language, religion or any other status; everyone entitled without discrimination.
  • Human Rights Day – 10 December.
  • UNO formed 24 October 1945 after World War II; 24 October = UN Day.

3.2 Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR)

  • Proclaimed by the UN General Assembly in Paris on 10 December 1948 (General Assembly resolution 217A) as a common standard of achievement.
  • First document setting out fundamental human rights to be universally protected; translated into many languages.
  • 30 articles; guarantees freedom of expression and civil, political, social, economic, cultural rights.
  • Social, economic and cultural rights – developed after WW II. Social = full participation in society; Economic = conditions to meet needs (economic equality and freedom); Cultural = enjoyment of culture with equality, dignity, non-discrimination.
  • Civil and political rights – protect freedom from infringement by government, organisations, individuals. Civil rights = basic rights by law to every person regardless of race, colour, gender, age, religion. Political rights = exercised in the formation and administration of a government; given to citizens by law.

3.5 National Human Rights Commission (NHRC)

  • Autonomous body, constituted on 12 October 1993 under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993.
  • A chairman and other members (textbook: 3 from judiciary, 4 from other departments). Office: New Delhi.
  • Human rights (per Act) = rights relating to life, liberty, equality and dignity guaranteed by Constitution or international covenants.
  • Functions: inquire into violation / negligence by a public servant; intervene in court proceedings; promote research; human rights education; encourage NGOs.

3.6 State Human Rights Commission (SHRC)

  • Every state has one under Section 21 of the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993. Tamil Nadu SHRC office: Chennai.
  • Inquires into violations in matters of the State and Concurrent Lists; objectives same as NHRC but confined to the state; a chairman and two members.
  • Has the power of a civil court; can act on complaints or suo motu; can recommend compensation to victims. (Its power does not extend beyond the state.)

3.6.1 Child Rights

  • Child = person who has not completed 18 years (minor) as per UNO; principle in Article 25 of UDHR (textbook).
  • Declaration of the Rights of the Child adopted by UN General Assembly on 20 November 1989.
  • Rights: to life; family environment; education; social security benefit; against sexual exploitation; against sale or trafficking; against other exploitation like child labour.
  • Right to life – survival even before birth; right to be born, food, shelter, clothing, dignified living.
  • Family environment – destitute/abandoned/orphaned children may be given for adoption.
  • Right to Education Act, 2009 (RTE) – free and compulsory education for 6–14 years under Article 21A; elementary education in a neighbourhood school, no fee.
  • Trafficking – root causes: poverty, gender discrimination, broken families; purposes: economic and sexual exploitation, abuse, drug trafficking, child labour.
  • Kavalan SOS App – launched by Govt of Tamil Nadu; anyone in emergency (not only women) can reach the State Police Control Room.
  • Social security – financial support from the state when parents cannot provide (illness, disability, old age).
  • Malala (box) – Swat Valley; shot by a masked gunman in October 2012; treated in Birmingham, England; Nobel Peace Prize laureate; fights for 12 years of free education for every girl (130 million girls out of school).
  • Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006 – "child" = male under 21, female under 18; such marriage is voidable (child party can seek annulment); punishment: rigorous imprisonment up to 2 years or fine up to ₹1 lakh or both; offences cognizable and non-bailable.
  • Child labour – glass, match-box, lock-making, rag-picking, carpets, beedi-rolling, mining, quarrying, brick kilns, tea gardens; girls mostly home-based work, boys waged labour.
  • Childline 1098 – India's first 24-hour free emergency phone service for children.
  • POCSO Act, 2012 (Protection of Children from Sexual Offences) – child = below 18; best interest of the child paramount; covers abuse by a person in trust/authority; child's statement recorded exactly as narrated; child not called repeatedly to testify; amended 2019 – stricter punishment including death penalty; POCSO Rules, 2020.
  • International survey: children with disabilities 3.4% more sexually abused.
  • Article 24 – no child below 14 in hazardous employment. Article 45 – free and compulsory education for all children until 14.
  • Kailash Satyarthi – founder of Bachpan Bachao Andolan; freed 86,000+ children; led the 80,000 km Global March against Child Labour in 1998; Nobel Peace Prize 2014.

3.6.2 Women Rights

  • National Commission for Women (NCW) – reviews constitutional and legal safeguards for women, recommends remedies, advises govt.
  • Women have held offices of President, PM, Lok Sabha Speaker, Leader of Opposition, Union Ministers, CMs, Governors.
  • Tamil Nadu gave ancestral property rights to women by the Hindu Succession (Tamil Nadu Amendment) Act 1989.
  • Central Govt amended the Hindu Succession Act in 2005 – equal share for women in undivided property.
  • Periyar E.V. Ramasamy – 1924 Vaikom (Kerala) agitation for temple entry; 1925 started the Self-Respect Movement.
  • Dr. B.R. Ambedkar – laws for women workers: Mines Maternity Benefit Act; Women Labour Welfare Fund; Women and Child Labour Protection Act; Maternity Benefit for Women Labour; Restoration of Ban on Employment of Women on Underground Work in Coal Mines.

Unit 4 – Forms of Government (PDF p. 273–278)

  • Government – main agency of the state; formulates, expresses and realises the will of the state; exercises legislative, executive and judicial powers per Constitution and laws.
  • Three organs: Legislature, Executive, Judiciary.
  • Four forms: unitary, federal, parliamentary, presidential. India = parliamentary.
  • 4.1 Meaning – 'Government' refers to the executive functions of the state; body with authority to make and enforce laws.
  • Word origin – Old French governor ← Latin gubernare = to direct, rule, guide, govern.
  • Oldest form (box) – Monarchy is the oldest form of government in the United Kingdom; British monarchy = constitutional monarchy (Sovereign is Head of State; legislation by elected Parliament).
  • Governance – process of decision making and implementation (Recap).

4.2 Types of Constitution: Written / Unwritten; Flexible / Rigid.

4.2.1 Unitary form – sovereign state governed as a single entity; central govt supreme; administrative divisions have only delegated powers. Even much decentralisation does not make it federal. Examples: England, France, Japan, Sri Lanka.

Unitary features of the Indian Constitution: strong Centre; Centre's control over state territory; single Constitution; flexibility of Constitution; unequal representation of states; emergency provisions; single citizenship; single integrated judiciary; All India Services; Governor appointed by the Centre.

4.2.2 Federal form – unitary/federal classification is based on relations between national and regional governments. Federal: powers divided between national and regional governments by the Constitution itself; each independent in its own sphere. Examples: USA, Switzerland, Australia, Canada, Russia, Brazil, Argentina. National govt = Federal/Central/Union govt; regional = state/provincial govt.

Federal features of the Indian Constitution: dual government; written Constitution; division of powers; supremacy of the Constitution (supreme law; Centre and state laws must conform); rigid Constitution; independent judiciary; bicameralism.

Unit 5 – Local Self Government (PDF p. 279–286)

5.1 Meaning – institutions administering a village, town or city; lowest/grassroot level; management of local affairs by bodies elected by local people; instruments of democratic self-government.

5.2 Historical background

  • Very old concept; at its peak under the later (Imperial) Cholas of Tanjore.
  • References under Mauryan administration; declined in medieval period due to feudalism.
  • Revived in British period (last quarter of 19th century) by Lord Ripon's Resolution, 1882. Lord Ripon = 'Father of Local Self Government'; gave Indians the "first taste of freedom"; advocated decentralisation; wider powers to rural and urban bodies.
  • Government of India Act, 1935 – provincial autonomy; came into force 1937; Congress provinces stressed rural development.
  • Gandhian ideal of Grama Swaraj (Village Republic) influenced constitution makers; restoration of panchayats became an "article of faith" during the freedom struggle.

Unit 3 – Understanding Secularism (PDF p. 188–194)

  • India = land of multi-religious faith and multi-cultural beliefs; birthplace of Hinduism, Jainism, Buddhism, Sikhism.
  • Contributors to secularism: Raja Ram Mohan Roy, Sir Syed Ahmad Khan, Rabindranath Tagore, Mahatma Gandhi, B.R. Ambedkar.
  • Nehru: "India will be a land of many faiths, equally honoured and respected, but of one national outlook."
  • Word origin – Latin 'saeculum' = 'an age' / 'the spirit of an age'. Term coined by George Jacob Holyoake, a British newspaper editor.
  • Secularism – tolerance and peaceful co-existence of all faiths; state neutrality and equality to all religions; separation of state and religion (no interference either way); everyone free to propagate, practise, profess, change or have no faith.
  • Atheism = lack of belief in god/gods. Secularism = non-interference of state in religion and vice-versa.
  • Objectives: no religious group dominates another; no members dominate others in the same community; state neither enforces a religion nor takes away religious freedom.
  • Poet Iqbal: "Religion does not teach us animosity; We are Indians and India is our home!"
  • Ashoka – 12th Rock Edict – first great emperor to announce (3rd century BCE) that the state would not prosecute any religious sect; urged tolerance and respect for all sects.
  • Akbar – religious toleration; Din-i-Illahi (Divine Faith) and Sulh-e-Kul (peace and harmony among religions). Akbar's tomb at Sikandara near Agra combines elements of Islam and Hinduism.
  • Khajuraho – a 19th-century Hindu temple incorporates a Hindu spire, Jain cupola, Buddhist stupa and Muslim-style dome (textbook).

Features of a secular state: Liberty (any religion may be practised); Equality (no preference to any religion); Neutrality (state neutral). No official religion; equal treatment; anyone can enter govt service; no religious instruction in educational institutions; no taxes to support any religion.

Constitution and secularism

  • The word 'secular' was not in the Constitution in 1950; added to the Preamble in 1976 by the 42nd Amendment (Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic). The Preamble describes India as secular.
  • No state religion in India; no special patronage to any religion.
  • Freedom of religion extends to aliens also – Supreme Court in Ratilal Panachand vs State of Bombay (1954).
  • Distinguishing features: state not identified with/controlled by any religion; right to profess any religion; no preferential treatment; no discrimination by faith; fraternity, dignity, unity. Public holidays for festivals of all religions.

Unit 5 – Road Safety Rules and Regulations (PDF p. 204–213)

  • Road safety – protection and security of all road users. Problem is our carelessness, not roads; Indian traffic is a heterogeneous mix of slow and fast vehicles.
  • Direct consequences: fatality, injury, property damage.

Reasons for road accidents

  • Over speeding – higher the speed, greater the risk; most fatal accidents. Tailgating (driving too close behind) is illegal.
  • Drunken driving – reduces concentration, hampers vision; detected by random breath test.
  • Distraction – mainly mobile phones; daydreaming, adjusting mirrors, stereo, animals, billboards.
  • Red-light jumping – to save time.
  • Avoiding safety gear – seat belts (four-wheelers) and helmets (two-wheelers) are compulsory by law.
  • Other factors: drivers, pedestrians (jaywalkers), passengers (footboard travel), vehicles (brake failure, tyre burst, overloading, projecting loads), road condition (potholes, illegal speed breakers, diversions), weather (fog, snow, rain, storms, hail).

Safety measures: keep to the left; slow down on bends; helmets; never exceed speed limit; keep safe distance and stay out of the blind spot (area not seen in rear-view/wing mirror); park only in designated bays; use parking lights and caution triangle at breakdown; follow (pictorial) road signs; no drunk driving or phone use; check mirrors before changing lanes; seat belts. Pedestrians: cross only at zebra crossing; never cross on red or yellow; use sidewalks.

Unit 7 – The Judiciary (PDF p. 223–230)

  • "The excellence of the judiciary is a measure of the excellence of the government."
  • India has a single, unified and integrated judicial system. Judiciary administers justice, settles disputes, interprets laws, protects FRs, guardian of the Constitution.
  • Law = system of rules imposed through government/institution. Judiciary = system of courts administering justice in the name of the State.

Evolution of Indian judiciary

  • Ancient – justice linked to religion; king = fountainhead of justice; justice by dharma. Smritis: Manusmriti, Naradasmriti, Yajnavalkya smriti. Ganas had Kulika courts; among the Vajjis, a board of eight Kulikas investigated criminal cases; appeal from Kula courts to Gana courts.
  • Medieval – Tughlaq period: code of civil procedure Fiqha-e-Feroze Shahi, written in Arabic, translated into Persian; followed till Aurangzeb, replaced by Fatawa-i-Alamgiri (1670).
  • Modern – codified common law from 1727 (Charter of 1726): Mayor's Courts at Madras, Bombay, Calcutta by the East India Company.
  • Regulating Act 1773 – provided for a Supreme Court; first Supreme Court at Fort William, Calcutta; Chief Justice Sir Elijah Impey. Supreme Courts at Madras (1801) and Bombay (1824); replaced by High Courts in 1862.
  • Warren Hastings – Mofussil Diwani Adalat (civil) and Mofussil Fauzdari Adalat (criminal); appeals to Sadar Diwani Adalat (civil appeal) and Sadar Nizamat Adalat (criminal appeal). Textbook: Sadar Diwani and Sadar Nizamat Adalats were established at Allahabad.
  • Lord Cornwallis – reorganised system; abolished District Fauzdari court; Circuit Courts at Calcutta, Dacca, Murshidabad, Patna. William Bentinck abolished the four Circuit courts.
  • Calcutta High Court = oldest High Court (1862); Allahabad High Court = largest.
  • Law Commission set up by Macaulay codified laws → Civil Procedure Code 1859, Indian Penal Code 1860, Criminal Procedure Code 1861.
  • Government of India Act 1935 – created the Federal Court (appeals still to the Privy Council in England); Privy Council jurisdiction abolished by the Abolition of Privy Council Jurisdiction Act, 1949.
  • Supreme Court of India inaugurated 28 January 1950.

Judiciary and Constitution

  • Founding fathers gave the highest place to 'Justice'; Constituent Assembly ensured independent courts with full power of judicial review.
  • Supreme Court Rules, 1966 – framed under Article 145.
  • Supreme Court established by Part V (The Union), Chapter IV 'The Union Judiciary'; Part VI Chapter VI = 'Subordinate Courts'.
  • Articles 124 to 147 – composition and jurisdiction of the Supreme Court.
  • Montesquieu (French philosopher) – first to propound an independent judiciary; theory of separation of powers.
