Introduction – Plassey (1757)

  • Battle of Plassey – 23 June 1757: Nawab of Bengal Siraj-ud-daulah defeated by East India Company (EIC).
  • Robert Clive (commander-in-chief of EIC) orchestrated it with secret support of Mir Jafar (uncle of Siraj-ud-daulah, chief of Nawab's army) and the Jagat Seths (moneylenders of Bengal).
  • 1757–1760: Company received ₹22.5 million from Mir Jafar (new Nawab) → invested to propel the Industrial Revolution in Britain; India pushed to de-industrialisation. Plunder continued for another 190 years.

7.1 Peasant and Tribal Resistance

  • Rural elite, peasants and tribals revolted – aim: restoration of the pre-colonial order, not necessarily removal of the British. Nearly a hundred peasant uprisings during British rule.
  • Four categories of peasant uprisings:
    TypeMeaning
    Restorative rebellionsRestore old order and old social relations
    Religious movementsLed by religious leaders; restructure society on religious principles
    Social banditryLeaders called criminals by British/elite, but heroes to their people
    Mass insurrectionUsually leaderless, spontaneous
  • Changes in revenue system: EIC restructured the Mughal revenue system → heavier burden on peasants. No widespread private ownership of land in pre-British India.
  • Subletting of land: zamindar sublet to subordinate lords who collected fixed revenue → higher tax burden on peasants.

7.2 The Great Rebellion of 1857 (First War of Indian Independence)

  • Began as mutiny of Bengal presidency sepoys, spread to civilians, esp. peasants. Significance: (1) first major revolt of armed forces + civilian rebellion; (2) unprecedented violence by both sides; (3) ended EIC rule – governance taken over by the British Crown.

(a) Causes

  • Annexation policy (1840s–50s): Doctrine of Paramountcy (British supreme; annexed on grounds rulers were inept); Doctrine of Lapse (no male heir → state lapses to British) – Satara, Sambalpur, parts of Punjab, Jhansi, Nagpur.
  • Insensitivity to cultural sentiments: Vellore mutiny 1806 – new dress code banned religious marks on forehead and whiskers, round hat replacing turban (feared conversion). 1824 Barrackpur sepoys refused to go to Burma by sea (loss of caste).
  • Discrimination in salary and promotion; racial humiliation.

(b) The Revolt

  • Precursor: rumour that cartridges of the new Enfield rifle were greased with cow and pig fat (to be bitten before loading).
  • 29 March (1857): Mangal Pandey assaulted his European officer; fellow soldiers refused to arrest him; court-martialled and hanged. Arson at cantonments of Ambala, Lucknow, Meerut.
  • 11 May 1857: sepoys from Meerut marched to Red Fort, Delhi; Bahadur Shah II proclaimed Shahenshah-e-Hindustan (Emperor of Hindustan). By beginning of June British rule in North India gone except Punjab and Bengal.
  • Civil rebellion: sepoys = "peasants in uniform"; first civil rebellion in North-Western Provinces and Oudh (main recruiting regions); zamindars, taluqdars, artisans (ruined by British goods) joined.
  • Map – important centres: Delhi, Meerut, Ambala, Agra, Bareilly, Lucknow, Kanpur, Allahabad, Banaras, Jhansi, Gwalior, Barrackpore.

  • (e) India becomes a Crown colony: Government of India Act, November 1858 – directly governed by Parliament; cabinet member = Secretary of State for India.
  • Changes after 1857: cautious on social reform; Queen Victoria's proclamation – no interference in religion/traditions, Indians to be absorbed in government service; Indians in army reduced and kept from important ranks; British took control of artillery; recruitment shifted from Rajputs, Brahmins, N. Indian Muslims to Gorkhas, Sikhs, Pathans; "Divide and Rule" policy.

7.3 Peasant Revolts under the Crown

  • Indigo Revolt 1859–60: planters gave cash advance with interest, forced peasants to grow indigo instead of food crops, paid low prices → hereditary debt. Began 1859 as a strike in a village of Nadia district (Bengal); spread, turned violent; Hindus & Muslims, women with pots and pans. Nil Darpan ("Mirror of the Indigo", 1860 play) by Dina Bandhu Mitra drew attention in India and Europe.
  • Deccan Riots 1875: first riot against moneylenders in May 1875 at Supa (village near Poona); ~30 villages in Poona and Ahmadnagar; directed mostly at Gujarati moneylenders; new law let moneylenders attach and auction mortgaged land → land passed to non-cultivators.

  • Rise of nationalism: English-educated intelligentsia (lawyers, journalists, teachers, doctors); vernacular & English press.

  • Economic critique of colonialism (most significant contribution of early nationalists): Dadabhai Naoroji, Justice (M.G.) Ranade, Romesh Chandra Dutt – colonialism = main obstacle to India's economic development.
  • INC 1885: culmination of efforts in three presidencies (Bombay, Madras, Calcutta); A.O. Hume facilitated; first President – Womesh Chandra Bonnerjee (1885); first session 28 December 1885 [(extra) at Bombay, 72 delegates].
  • Early objectives: national unity but loyalty to Britain; methods – appeals, petitions, delegations, constitutional.
  • Key demands: legislative councils at provincial & central level; more elected members; separation of judiciary and executive; reduce military expenditure; reduce Home Charges; extend trial by jury; ICS exams in India as well as England; police reforms; reconsider forest laws; promote Indian industries, end unfair tariffs/excise.
  • (d) Militant nationalism: moderates' petitions failed → "extremists" stressed self-help.

7.5 Partition of Bengal (1905) and Swadeshi Movement

  • Partition by Lord Curzon, 1905 – the most unpopular measure; aim: curtail Bengali influence, weaken nationalism by a Hindu–Muslim divide; Bengali-speakers made a linguistic minority; Curzon promised Muslims unity in East Bengal unseen since the Mughals. Result: it united Bengalis; regional newspapers built Bengali pride.
  • Anti-partition movement: moderates turned to boycott of British goods; moderates wanted only annulment, militants wanted mass struggle beyond Bengal.
  • 16 October 1905 (partition day) = day of mourning; bath in the Ganga, marches singing Bande Mataram.
  • Boycott & Swadeshi Movement in Bengal (1905–1911) – four trends: Moderate trend, Constructive Swadeshi, Militant Nationalism, Revolutionary Nationalism.
  • Constructive Swadeshi: self-help, alternative institutions; Swadeshi shops (textiles, handlooms, soaps, earthenware, matches, leather).
  • Passive resistance (from 1906) – 4 points: boycott of foreign goods; boycott of government schools/colleges, courts, titles, services; Swadeshi industries & national schools; armed struggle if repression became unbearable.
  • Militant nationalism – Lal-Bal-Pal: Lala Lajpat Rai (Punjab), Bal Gangadhar Tilak (Maharashtra), Bipin Chandra Pal (Bengal). Hotbeds: Punjab, Bengal, Maharashtra.
  • South India: Tuticorin = most important Swadeshi centre – Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company by V.O. Chidambaranar.
  • Swaraj: common goal of extremists; for Tilak = complete autonomy, total freedom from foreign rule.

7.6 Home Rule Movement (1916–1918)

  • Led by Lokamanya Tilak and Annie Besant; background – World War I (Britain declared war on Germany 1914); moderates supported Britain hoping for self-government; British non-committal → "betrayal".
  • (extra) Tilak's League – April 1916 (Belgaum); Besant's League – Sept 1916 (Madras/Adyar).
  • Objectives: self-government within British Empire by constitutional means; dominion status (like Australia, Canada, South Africa, New Zealand); non-violent constitutional methods.
  • Lucknow Pact (1916): after reunion of moderates and militants; Congress + Muslim League agreed on self-government ASAP; Congress accepted separate electorates for Muslims.
  • British response: repression; 1919 Montagu–Chelmsford reforms (gradual self-government – disappointed nationalists); then Rowlatt Act – arbitrary arrest and strict punishment.

Glossary: orchestrated – organised for an effect; clandestine – secret; restorative – re-establishing; subletting – lease by one lessee to another; egalitarian – equal rights for all; coercive – forcible; extortion – taking by force; disgruntled – dissatisfied; abysmal – extremely bad.

Introduction

  • Gandhi returned to India in 1915 from South Africa after ~20 years of fighting for civil rights of Indians there; introduced satyagraha (perfected in South Africa) – could be practised by men and women, young and old.

8.1 Gandhi and Mass Nationalism

(a) Evolution of Gandhi

  • Born 2 October 1869, Porbandar (Gujarat). Father Kaba Gandhi – Diwan of Porbandar, later Diwan of Rajkot. Mother Putlibai influenced him.
  • Sailed to England 1888 to study law; became barrister June 1891; returned a believer in British justice. Practice in Bombay failed.
  • Left for South Africa April 1893 (law-suit for a Gujarati firm). Faced racial discrimination for the first time – thrown out of first-class compartment at Pietermaritzburg railway station (journey Durban → Pretoria).
  • Indians in Transvaal: poll tax of £3; could not own land except in marked areas; could not move outdoors after 9 p.m. without permit.
  • Influences: Tolstoy – The Kingdom of God is Within You; Ruskin – Unto this Last; Thoreau – Civil Disobedience. Blend of Indian and Western thought, yet critical of Western civilisation and industrialisation.
  • Inspired by Ruskin: Phoenix Settlement (1905) and Tolstoy Farm (1910) – equality, community living, dignity of labour; training grounds for satyagrahis.

Satyagraha as a strategy in South Africa

  • Satyagraha = devotion to truth, truth-force (அறப்போர்): peaceful marches, courting arrest against unjust laws; used against immigration rules and racial discrimination; registration offices picketed.
  • Smuts–Gandhi Agreement → poll tax on indentured labourers abolished.

8.2 Gandhi's Early Satyagrahas in India

  • Political guru: Gopal Krishna Gokhale; on his advice Gandhi toured the country first. On a journey through Tamil Nadu Gandhi decided to discard his robes and wear a simple dhoti.
  • (a) Champaran Satyagraha (Bihar): tinkathia system – peasants forced by European planters to grow indigo on three-twentieths (3/20) of their land. German synthetic dyes pushed indigo out of the market; planters raised rent and illegal dues as price for release. Rajkumar Shukla persuaded Gandhi to come. Gandhi refused to leave when ordered → trial; Gandhi: "The country thus had its first object-lesson in Civil Disobedience". Assisted by Brajkishore Prasad (lawyer) and Rajendra Prasad. Lieutenant Governor's committee (Gandhi a member) → tinkathia abolished.
  • Followed by Ahmedabad mill strike (1918) and Kheda Satyagraha (1918) → Gandhi established as leader of mass struggle.

(b) Rowlatt Satyagraha and Jallianwala Bagh Massacre

  • Government of India Act 1919 disappointed (no real power to Indians). Rowlatt Act – excessive police powers, arrest without warrant, detention without trial. Gandhi called it the "Black Act".
  • Nation-wide satyagraha 6 April 1919 (fasting and prayer) – earliest anti-colonial struggle spread across the country. Intense in Punjab – Amritsar and Lahore. Gandhi arrested, prevented from visiting Punjab.
  • 9 April 1919: Dr. Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr. Satyapal arrested in Amritsar.
  • 13 April 1919 – Jallianwala Bagh, Amritsar, Baisaki day (spring harvest festival of Sikhs). General Reginald Dyer blocked the only entrance; fired without warning for 10 minutes till ammunition ran out. Official: 379 killed, >1,000 injured; unofficial >1,000 dead. Martial law; people flogged and forced to crawl.
  • Protest: Rabindranath Tagore returned his knighthood; Gandhi surrendered his Kaiser-i-Hind medal.

(c) Khilafat Movement

  • After WWI (ended 1918) the Caliph of Turkey (head of Muslims of the world) treated harshly. Led by the Ali brothers – Maulana Mohamed Ali and Maulana Shaukat Ali.
  • Gandhi presided over the All India Khilafat Conference, Delhi, November 1919; supported Shaukat Ali's three slogans: Allaho Akbar, Bande Mataram, Hindu-Musalman ki Jai.
  • Khilafat Committee, Allahabad, 9 June 1920 adopted Gandhi's non-violent non-cooperation; Non-Cooperation began 1 August 1920.

8.3 Non-Cooperation Movement and its Fallout

  • Approved at special session, Calcutta, September 1920; passed at Nagpur session, December 1920, chaired by Salem C. Vijayaraghavachariar.
  • Programme (8 points): surrender titles & honorary offices; non-participation in government functions; lawyers suspend practice (private arbitration); boycott of government schools; boycott of legislatures under 1919 Act; non-participation in government parties/official functions; refuse civil or military posts; boycott foreign goods, spread Swadeshi.
  • (a) No-tax campaign at Bardoli – February 1922; bonfires of foreign cloth; boycott of the Prince of Wales' visit successful.
  • Chauri Chaura – 5 February 1922, village near Gorakhpur (present UP): procession provoked by police turned violent; mob burnt the police station – 22 policemen died → Gandhi withdrew the movement.
  • (b) Swarajists: Congress split – pro-changers (Motilal Nehru, C.R. Das – enter legislatures, wreck from within) vs no-changers (Vallabhbhai Patel, C. Rajaji – continue non-cooperation). Swaraj Party formed 1 January 1923 (later approved by a special Congress session); won many seats in Imperial Legislative Assembly and provincial councils; in Bengal refused to take charge of transferred subjects; declined after C.R. Das died in 1925.
  • Box – Dyarchy (dual government, GoI Act 1919): provincial subjects split into Reserved (finance, defence, police, justice, land revenue, irrigation – British) and Transferred (local self-government, education, public health, public works, agriculture, forests, fisheries – Indian ministers). Ended with provincial autonomy in 1935.
  • (c) Constructive Programme (after Chauri Chaura): Khadi, Hindu–Muslim unity, abolition of untouchability; khaddar compulsory for Congress members; All India Spinners' Association formed.
  • (d) Simon Commission: announced 8 November 1927 – Indian Statutory Commission, 7 members, headed by Sir John Simon; all-white, no Indian member → boycotted by Congress and Muslim League; slogan "Go Back Simon"; Lala Lajpat Rai injured in police assault at Lahore and died a few days later.
  • (e) Nehru Report (1928) – all-party conference; committee under Motilal Nehru. Recommended: dominion status; joint and mixed electorates; reservation of seats for Muslims at Centre and in provinces where in minority (and for Hindus in NWFP); fundamental rights and universal adult franchise.
  • Jinnah demanded one-third of Central Legislature seats for Muslims (supported by Tej Bahadur Sapru) – defeated; then Jinnah's Fourteen Points – rejected. Jinnah, once "Ambassador of Hindu–Muslim Unity", turned to a separate nation for Muslims.

8.4 Struggle for Poorna Swaraj and Civil Disobedience Movement

  • Lahore Congress, December 1929, President Jawaharlal Nehru: Poorna Swaraj declared goal; boycott Round Table Conference; launch Civil Disobedience; 26 January 1930 = Independence Day (pledge taken). Gandhi authorised to lead.
  • (a) Salt Satyagraha: charter of demands to Viceroy Lord Irwin, ultimatum 31 January 1930: cut army & civil service expenditure by 50%; total prohibition of liquor; release political prisoners; cut land revenue by 50%; abolish salt tax (a "brilliant tactical decision").
  • Dandi March: 12 March 1930 from Sabarmati Ashram with 78 inmates; Gandhi aged 61; 241 miles in 24 days; reached Dandi 5 April 1930; broke salt law next morning (6 April).
  • Provinces: Tamil Nadu – C. Rajaji, Tiruchirappalli → Vedaranyam; also Kerala, Andhra, Bengal; NWFP – Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan, Khudai Khidmatgar ("Red Shirts").
  • Gandhi arrested at midnight → Yeravada Jail; Nehru, Ghaffar Khan arrested. Boycott of foreign cloth, picketing liquor shops, non-payment of taxes, breaking forest laws – biggest mass movement India had witnessed.
  • Box – Forest Acts: first Forest Act 1865 (restricted access to firewood, fodder, minor produce); Indian Forest Act 1878 – original ownership of forests with the state; waste/fallow lands counted as forest; shifting cultivation prohibited. Alluri Sitarama Raju – struggle in Rampa (Rampa uprisings 1922–24); special Malabar Police team sent; Raju attained martyrdom.
  • (b) First Round Table Conference – London, November 1930; PM Ramsay MacDonald proposed federal government with provincial autonomy; Congress did not attend (leaders in jail).
  • (c) Gandhi–Irwin Pact – 5 March 1931: release of political prisoners not involved in violence; return of confiscated land; lenient treatment of resigned employees; coastal villagers allowed to make salt for consumption; non-violent picketing allowed. Congress suspended CDM and agreed to attend. Second RTC began 7 September 1931 – Gandhi attended; refused separate electorates for minorities → no result.
  • (d) Renewal of CDM: martial law; Gandhi arrested 4 January 1932. Third RTC: 17 November – 24 December 1932 – Congress did not participate.
  • (e) Communal Award – 16 August 1932 (Ramsay MacDonald): separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, women and the "depressed classes". Dr. B.R. Ambedkar supported separate electorates. Gandhi's fast unto death – 20 September 1932. Talks by Madan Mohan Malaviya, Rajendra Prasad with Ambedkar and M.C. Rajah.
  • Poona Pact (Gandhi–Ambedkar): separate electorates abandoned → joint electorate with reserved seats for depressed classes; reserved seats raised from 71 to 148; 18% of seats in Central Legislature reserved.
  • (f) Campaign against untouchability: base shifted to Satyagraha Ashram, Wardha; all-India Harijan Tour; Harijan Sevak Sangh; Temple Entry Movement – 8 January 1933 = "Temple Entry Day".

8.5 Beginnings of Socialist Movements

  • CPI founded at Tashkent (Uzbekistan), October 1920 (inspired by Russian Revolution 1917); founders M.N. Roy, Abani Mukherjee, M.P.T. Acharya.
  • Kanpur Conspiracy Case 1924: M.N. Roy, S.A. Dange, Muzaffar Ahmed, M. Singaravelar and others tried.
  • (a) All India Communist Conference, Kanpur 1925 – Singaravelar gave Presidential Address → founding of CPI on Indian soil; led to All India Workers' and Peasants' Party (1928).
  • (b) Revolutionary activities: Hindustan Republican Army (HRA), Kanpur, 1924. Kakori (near Lucknow) train robbery 1925 – Ram Prasad Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan; Kakori Conspiracy Case – four sentenced to death.
  • Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev reorganised HRA in Punjab → Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (1928). Killed Sanders (officer responsible for the lathi charge that killed Lajpat Rai). Bhagat Singh & B.K. Dutt threw a smoke bomb in the Central Legislative Assembly, 1929; slogans "Inquilab Zindabad", "Long Live the Proletariat". Bhagat Singh and Rajguru sentenced to death.
  • Chittagong Armoury Raid – April 1930, Surya Sen; provisional revolutionary government; survived 3 years; Surya Sen caught 1933, hanged after a year.
  • (c) Left in the 1930s: Great Depression – fall in trade, 50% fall in agricultural prices (land revenue doubled in real terms). CPI banned in 1934. Congress Socialist Party, 1934 – Jayaprakash Narayan, Acharya Narendra Dev, Minoo Masani.
  • Gandhi quote: "Real Swaraj will come not by acquisition of authority by a few, but by the acquisition of the capacity by all to resist authority, when abused."

8.6 First Congress Ministries under GoI Act 1935

  • GoI Act 1935 (positive outcome of CDM): provincial autonomy and dyarchy at the Centre; All-India Federation of 11 provinces, 6 Chief Commissioner's provinces and willing princely states; all provincial subjects to Indian ministers; property-based franchise – only ~10% could vote; Burma separated from India.
  • (a) 1937 elections: Congress won 7 of 11 provinces; formed ministries in 8 – Madras, Bombay, Central Provinces, Orissa, Bihar, United Provinces, NWFP, and Assam (coalition with Assam Valley Muslim Party of Sir Muhammad Sadullah).
  • Work: ministers' salaries cut Rs 2,000 → Rs 500/month; emergency-power Acts repealed; bans lifted (except Communist Party); press restrictions removed; CID reporting of speeches stopped; laws to reduce peasant debt, improve labour conditions; temple entry legislation; focus on education and public health.
  • (b) Resignation – 1939: WWII began; Government entered the war for the Allies without consulting Congress ministries → ministries resigned. By 1940 Jinnah was demanding a separate state for Muslims.
  • (c) 1939: Subhas Chandra Bose became Congress President defeating Pattabhi Sitaramayya (Gandhi's candidate); resigned when Gandhi refused to cooperate → started Forward Bloc.

8.7 Developments leading to Quit India Movement

  • (a) Individual Satyagraha: after Viceroy Linlithgow's August 1940 offer; Vinoba Bhave first satyagrahi, 17 October 1940; >25,000 arrested.
  • (b) Cripps Mission – 22 March 1942 (Sir Stafford Cripps): dominion status after the war; princes could sign separate agreement (implied Pakistan); British control of defence during war. Rejected by Congress and League. Gandhi: "a post-dated cheque on a crashing bank".
  • (c) Quit India Resolution – AICC, Bombay, 8 August 1942; Gandhi's call "Do or Die" ("We shall either free India or die in the attempt…"). 9 August 1942 – Gandhi and whole Congress leadership arrested.
  • (d) Socialists led: Jayaprakash Narayan and Ramanand Misra escaped prison, organised underground movement; Aruna Asaf Ali; Usha Mehta – underground Congress Radio (till November 1942).
  • (e) People's response: hartals, strikes, attacks on railway stations, telegraph lines – particularly widespread in Madras; parallel governments in Satara, Orissa, Bihar, United Provinces, Bengal.
  • (f) Bose and INA: escaped house arrest March 1941 → Afghanistan → Germany; February 1943 to Japan by submarine. INA (Azad Hind Fauj) earlier organised by Gen. Mohan Singh with Indian POWs in Malaya & Burma (Japanese support). Bose reorganised into 3 brigades: Gandhi, Nehru, and women's Rani of Jhansi brigade (headed by Captain Lakshmi Sahgal). Provisional Government of Free India at Singapore; slogan "Dilli Chalo". Japan's defeat stopped INA; Bose died in a plane crash.
  • INA trials – Red Fort; Congress defence committee: Nehru, Tej Bahadur Sapru, Bhulabhai Desai, Asaf Ali; convicted but released under public pressure.

8.8 Towards Freedom

  • (a) Royal Indian Navy Revolt – Bombay, February 1946; >20,000 ratings; strikes also in Indian Air Force and Signal Corps at Jabalpur.
  • (b) Wavell Plan – 14 June 1945: interim government; equal Hindus and Muslims in Viceroy's Executive Council; all portfolios except war to Indians.   • Discovery of Harappa: ruins first described by EIC soldier-explorer Charles Masson ("ruined brick castle with very high walls and towers") – earliest historical record of Harappa. 1856 – engineers laying Lahore–Karachi railway used the burnt bricks for the rail road. 1920s excavation of Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro; 1924 Sir John Marshall (DG of ASI) concluded they were part of one large civilisation. Harappa older than Mohenjo-Daro (pottery differences).
  • ASI started 1861 with Alexander Cunningham as Surveyor; HQ New Delhi.
  • Sites: within India – Lothal, Dholavira (Gujarat), Kalibangan (Rajasthan), Rakhigarhi, Mitathal (Haryana), Manda (J&K); in Pakistan – Harappa, Mohenjo-Daro, Amri, Kot Diji, Ganweriwala. (State names – extra.)
  • How archaeologists explore: bricks, stones, potsherds; literary sources; aerial photographs; magnetic scanner; RADAR and remote sensing.
  • Mehergarh – precursor; Neolithic site near Bolan basin, Balochistan (Pakistan); farming & herding as early as 7000 BCE.
  • Fact box: range South Asia; Bronze Age; 3300–1900 BCE (by radiocarbon dating); area 13 lakh sq. km; 6 big cities; more than 200 villages.
  • Urban civilisation because: well-conceived town planning; masonry & architecture; hygiene & public health; standardised weights & measures; solid agricultural and artisanal base.
  • Town planning – two parts: Upper town / citadel (west, higher; administrators; Great Bath and granaries) and Lower town (east, lower but larger; common people).
  • Drainage: covered drains (slabs/bricks), gentle slope, holes at intervals for cleaning; house drains → main drains; every house had a soak pit.
  • Streets & houses: grid pattern, streets N–S and E–W crossing at right angles; wide roads, rounded corners; houses 1–2 storeys with courtyard, well, toilets, bathrooms; baked bricks and mortar (also sun-dried), uniform size; flat roofs; no conclusive evidence of palaces or places of worship. Burnt bricks – strong, fire- and water-resistant.
  • Great Bath (Mohenjo-Daro): large rectangular tank – maybe earliest water-proof structure; bricks + plaster + layers of natural bitumen; steps on north and south; rooms on three sides; water from a well.
  • Great Granary (Harappa): massive, solid brick foundation; remains of wheat, barley, millets, sesame, pulses. Mud-brick granary also at Rakhigarhi (Haryana) – Mature Harappan phase.
  • Assembly Hall (Mohenjo-Daro): multi-pillared – 20 pillars in 4 rows.
  • Trade: great traders; standardised weights; marked sticks to measure length. Leader/"priest-king" statue (Mohenjo-Daro): head band, armlet, combed hair, trimmed beard, shawl with flower/ring designs.
  • Technology: ivory scale at Lothal (book prints "1704 mm"; correct: smallest division 1.704 mm – the smallest recorded among contemporary civilisations); carts with spokeless solid wheels; maritime trade with Mesopotamia (Sumer) – Indus seals found there; Naram-Sin of Akkad bought jewellery from Melukha (Indus region); cylindrical seals of Persian Gulf type found in Indus area.
  • Lothal dockyard (naval) – on a tributary of Sabarmati, Gujarat. "Civilisation" from Latin civis = city.
  • Dancing girl statuette – Mohenjo-Daro (John Marshall's remark on its modelling).
  • KVT Complex (Korkai–Vanji–Thondi): place names in Pakistan/Afghanistan found in Sangam literature – Korkai, Vanji, Tondi, Matrai, Urai, Kudalgarh (Pakistan); Gurkay, Pumpuhar (Afghanistan); rivers Kawri, Poruns (Afghanistan), Kaweri Wala, Phornai (Pakistan).
  • Indus script not yet deciphered. Apparel – cotton common, clay spindles, wool. Ornaments of gold, silver, ivory, shell, copper, terracotta, precious stones; Carnelian (red quartz) for jewellery.
  • Peaceful – few weapons, no evidence of an army; settlements on platforms. Horse and iron unknown. Copper – first metal used by humans.
  • Religion: no specific deity evidence; possibly Mother Goddess (fertility) – female figurines. Governance: a central authority believed.
  • Occupation: agriculture, handicrafts, pottery, jewellery, weaving, carpentry, trade; cattle rearing; potter's wheel. Pottery red with black designs.
  • Radiocarbon (C14) dating – uses carbon-14 to date objects. Earliest writing – Sumerians.
  • Decline by 1900 BCE – floods, ecological change, invasions, natural calamity, climate change, deforestation, epidemic.
  • Toys: carts, cows with movable heads, clay monkey, terracotta squirrels, etc. General facts: among oldest; largest of the four ancient civilisations; world's first planned cities; Mohenjo-Daro – UNESCO World Heritage Site.
  • Extent: Makran coast of Baluchistan (W) – Ghaggar-Hakra valley (E) – Afghanistan (NE) – Maharashtra (S). Great Bath = one of the earliest public tanks.
  • Glossary: steatite – soft talc stone; bitumen – water-proof tar; pictograph – pictorial record; Mohenjo-Daro = "mound of the dead"; citadel = raised platform; bronze = alloy.
  • Elsewhere: Great Pyramid of Giza – King Khufu, 2500 BCE; Ur Ziggurat – Ur Nammu (Moon god Sin); Abu Simbel – Ramesses II.
  • Exercise answers: metals known – copper, bronze, silver, gold, not iron; Indus = metal (Bronze) age; odd one – horses; wrong pair – Harappan civilisation–River Cauvery; earliest writing by Sumerians (not Chinese).

B. Vedic Culture in North India & Megalithic Culture in South India (Class 6 T2, Unit 1)

  • Vedic Age: 1500–600 BCE; named after the 4 Vedas – Rig, Yajur, Sama, Atharva. Began with arrival of Aryans – Indo-Aryan-speaking semi-nomadic pastoralists from Central Asia via the Khyber Pass (Hindu Kush); cattle rearing + slash-and-burn agriculture.
  • Fact box: North India; Iron Age; sources – Vedic literature; nature – rural.
  • Homeland: Rig Vedic – Punjab = Sapta Sindhu (land of seven rivers); c. 1000 BCE moved east to Indo-Gangetic plain; iron axes and ploughs.
  • Two phases: Early (Rig) Vedic 1500–1000 BCE; Later Vedic 1000–c.600 BCE.
  • Shrutis ("listening"; oral) – 4 Vedas, Brahmanas, Aranyakas, Upanishads – sacred, eternal. Smritis ("definite, written") – Ithihasas (Ramayana, Mahabharata), Puranas (Shiva, Vishnu), Tantras, Agamas, Dharmasutras, Manu Smriti, Narada Smriti – revised constantly.
  • National Motto "Satyameva Jayate" – from Mundaka Upanishad.
  • Archaeological sources: iron implements, pottery in Punjab, UP, Rajasthan. Many texts but inadequate material evidence.

Vardhana (Pushyabhuti) dynasty

  • Founder Pushyabhuti (Gupta general), capital Thaneswar; strong under Prabhakaravardhana. Son Rajavardhana killed by Sasanka (Gauda ruler of Bengal) while rescuing sister Rajyashri (whose husband, Raja of Kanauj, Sasanka had killed) → Harshavardhana king; capital shifted Thaneswar → Kanauj.
  • Harsha: ruled 41 years; feudatories Jalandhar, Kashmir, Nepal, Valabhi; unified most of N. India; checked in the south by Chalukya Pulikesin II; died 648 CE (book); cordial with Iran and China; first met Hiuen Tsang at Kajangala near Rajmahal (Jharkhand).

  • Religion: first Shiva worshipper → Buddhism (Mahayana) under Rajyashri & Hiuen Tsang; last Buddhist sovereign in India; stopped killing animals for food; tolerant – worshipped Buddha, Shiva, Sun. Assemblies at Kanauj (20 kings; golden Buddha; 3-ft statue procession) and Prayag (distributed wealth; also attended Kumbhamela).
  • Literature: Harsha wrote Ratnavali, Nagananda, Priyadharshika; court – Banabhatta, Mayura, Hardatta, Jayasena. Hiuen Tsang – "prince of pilgrims", Si-Yu-Ki. Harsha's time = early Tang dynasty (capital Xi'an).

Sack of Chittor 1303 – jauhar. Reforms: agrarian survey, fixed revenue, collection by military officers; forced grain procurement at fixed prices, state granaries; spies.
  • Ghiyas-ud-din Tughluq (1320–1324): son Jauna Khan defeated Prataparudra of Warangal; founded Tughluqabad near Delhi. Jauna Khan said to have killed his father → Muhammad-bin-Tughluq (1325).
  • Muhammad-bin-Tughluq (1325–1351): learned but cruel; capital Delhi → Devagiri, renamed Daulatabad, then back; Ibn Battuta (Moroccan) found Delhi "empty, abandoned". Revenue in money instead of grain (disastrous in famines); copper token currency → counterfeiting → withdrawn, bankrupt; tax raised in Doab → peasant revolts, famines. Revolts – Awadh, Multan, Sind; Bahmani sultanate (Daulatabad region); Madurai sultanate 1335; Bengal independent 1346; died 23 March 1351 (ruled 25 years).

  • Sayyids (1414–1451): Khizr Khan, Timur's representative (Delhi, Meerut, Punjab); last – Ala-ud-din Alam Shah abdicated 1451. Sultanate survived 114 more years after Timur till the Mughals.

G. Rise of Marathas and Peshwas (Class 7 T2, Unit 3)

  • Shahji Bhonsle (Shivaji's father) – officer of Ahmednagar, later Bijapur. Mughals had to recognise Maratha right to collect Chauth over the Deccan. Peshwas held real power from Shahu's time till 1761.
  • Factors for rise: rocky, mountainous terrain (guerrilla warfare); service in Bijapur & Ahmednagar cavalry; Bhakti movement – saints Eknath, Tukaram, Ramdas (Tukaram & Ramdas influenced Shivaji); Marathi hymns.
  • Shivaji: born 1627; mother Jijabai; teacher-guardian Dadaji Kondadev. 1645 (age 18) captured Kondana (near Poona); next year Torna; rebuilt Raigarh. Independent after Kondadev's death (1649); army strength – Mavali foot soldiers; took Purandhar from Mughals; Bijapur held Shahji captive.
  • Consolidation: Javali (1656) from Chandrarao More; 1659 killed Afzal Khan (Bijapur general); 1663 wounded Shaista Khan (Aurangzeb's uncle); plundered Surat 1664 and again 1670. Raja Jai Singh sent → Shivaji yielded forts, became mansabdar; humiliated at Agra, escaped hidden in a basket. 1674 coronation at Raigarh – "Chhatrapati" (chhatra = parasol, pati = lord); Jijabai died days later. Died 1680 (fever & dysentery).
  • Administration – three circles: (1) swaraj (direct rule); (2) suzerainty areas paying Chauth (¼ of revenue, protection money) and Sardeshmukhi (extra 1/10, chieftain's due); (3) plunder zone. Deshmukhs – 20 to 100 villages; village headman Patil, accountant Kulkarni.
  • Army: infantry the early backbone, later cavalry; soldiers chosen personally (on a serving soldier's surety); retired captains guarded forts. Revenue: on actual yield – 3/5 to cultivator, 2/5 to government. Civil cases – panchayat; criminal law – shastras.

Origin of the Palayam system

  • Plassey (1757) → British dominance; English claimed the right to collect annual tribute (kist) from Palayakkarars. First resistance – Puli Thevar; later Kattabomman, Oomathurai, Marudu brothers, Dheeran Chinnamalai.
  • Vijayanagar rulers appointed Nayaks; Nayak of Madurai appointed Palayakkarars. Viswanatha Nayak (Nayak of Madurai from 1529) with minister Ariyanatha Mudaliyar instituted the Palayakkarar system in 1529 – 72 Palayams, held on military tenure.
  • Taxes: 1/3 to the Nayak, 1/3 for the army, 1/3 kept by the Palayakkarar.
  • Two blocs: Eastern Palayams (Nayaks) under Kattabomman; Western Palayams (Maravas) under Puli Thevar – both refused kist. Carnatic Treaty 1792 gave the English the right to collect taxes → Palayakkarar revolt.

Puli Thevar

  • Pioneer of protest against English rule in Tamil Nadu; Palayakkarar of Nerkattumseval near Tirunelveli; refused tribute to Mohammed Ali (Nawab of Arcot) and the English.
  • Defeated the combined Nawab–English forces at Tirunelveli – first Palayakkarar to fight and defeat the British in India; tried to form a league of Palayakkarars.
  • 1759 Nawab's forces under Yusuf Khan attacked; defeated at Anthanallur; Nerkattumseval captured 1761; recaptured 1764; defeated by Captain Campbell 1767; died in exile.

Veera Pandiya Kattabomman

  • Father Jagaveerapandiya Kattabomman ruled Veerapandiyapuram; capital Panchalankurichi. Wife Jakkammal; brothers Oomathurai and Sevathaiah; minister Sivasubramaniam.
  • Panchalankurichi paid tribute to the Nawab of Arcot; after Carnatic Treaty 1792 the Company collected taxes – tribute = main cause of rivalry.
  • 1798 – Colin Jackson (Collector of Ramanathapuram) demanded arrears; famine excuse; meeting at Ramanathapuram – only 1080 pagodas balance; Kattabomman & minister made to stand 3 hours; attempted arrest; Oomathurai helped escape; Sivasubramaniam captured.
  • Edward Clive (Governor, Madras) – committee found Kattabomman not guilty; Jackson dismissed; S.R. Lushington new collector; Sivasubramaniam released.
  • Confederacy of Marudu Pandyas (Tiruchirappalli Proclamation) – Kattabomman interested; marched on Sivagiri (Company tributary) → Company acted.
  • Fall of Panchalankurichi: Major Bannerman, 5 September (1799); clash at Kolarpatti – Sivasubramaniam captured; Kattabomman fled to Pudukottai; captured in Kalapore jungles by Vijaya Ragunatha Tondaiman (Raja of Pudukottai). Sivasubramaniam executed at Nagalapuram; Kattabomman hanged at Kayathar on 16 October 1799.

Velu Nachiyar

  • Queen of Sivagangai; married at 16 to Muthu Vaduganathar, killed in Kalaiyar Koil battle (1772) by Nawab & British.
  • Escaped with daughter Vellachi Nachiyar; protected by Gopala Nayakar at Virupachi near Dindigul; organised army; Kuyili (commander) – suicide attack on British ammunition store; recaptured Sivagangai with Marudu brothers.
  • First queen to fight British colonial power in India; "Veeramangai"; "Jhansi Rani of South India".

Marudu Brothers & South Indian Rebellion (1800–1801)

  • Sons of Mookiah Palaniappan and Ponnathal; Periya Marudu (Vella Marudu) and Chinna Marudu ("Marudu Pandiyan"); served Muthu Vaduganatha Peria Udaya Devar (1750–1772); recaptured Sivaganga – Periya Marudu ruler, Chinna Marudu adviser; called "Lion of Sivaganga".
  • Causes: sheltered Oomathurai and others after Kattabomman's execution; Company interference in Sivaganga.
  • Feb 1801 Oomathurai & Sevathaiah escaped Palayamkottai prison → Kamudhi → Chinna Marudu's capital Siruvayal; rebuilt Panchalankurichi fort; retaken by Colin Macaulay (April); Col. Agnew and Col. Innes marched on Sivaganga.
  • Confederacy: Marudu Pandiar (Sivagangai), Gopala Nayak (Dindigul), Kerala Varma (Malabar), Krishnappa Nayak and Dhoondaji (Mysore).
  • Tiruchirappalli Proclamation – June 1801 (proclamation of independence) – first call to Indians to unite against the British; pasted on the Nawab's palace (Tiruchi fort) and the Srirangam temple wall. Chinna Marudu raised ~20,000 men. British reinforcements from Bengal, Ceylon, Malaya; rajas of Pudukkottai, Ettayapuram, Thanjavur sided with the British.
  • Rebels defeated at Piranmalai and Kalayarkoil; Sivagangai annexed 1801; Marudu brothers executed at Tirupathur fort (now Sivagangai dt.) on 24 October 1801; Oomathurai & Sevathaiah beheaded at Panchalankurichi 16 November 1801; 73 rebels exiled to Penang (Prince of Wales Island).
  • British records: Second Palayakkarar War. Carnatic Treaty, 31 July 1801 – direct British control over Tamil Nadu; Palayakkarar system abolished.

Dheeran Chinnamalai

  • Born at Melapalayam, Chennimalai near Erode; original name Theerthagiri; Palayakkarar of Kongu country (Salem, Coimbatore, Karur, Dindigul – earlier Madurai Nayaks → Wodayars → Mysore Sultans → English after 3rd & 4th Mysore wars).
  • Trained by the French; fought alongside Tipu Sultan; after Tipu's death built a fort at Odanilai; sought help of Marathas & Marudu Pandyas to attack Coimbatore (1800) – failed.
  • Guerrilla victories at Cauvery, Odanilai, Arachalur; betrayed by his cook Nallapan; hanged at Sankagiri fort, 1805.

Vellore Revolt (1806)

  • Tipu's family imprisoned at Vellore Fort after the 4th Mysore war; ~3,000 ex-servants of Hyder & Tipu nearby; many troops recruited from Tirunelveli after the 1800 rising.
  • William Cavendish Bentinck – Governor of Madras from 1803; military regulations 1805–06 enforced by C-in-C Sir John Cradock.
  • Causes: new discipline, weapons, uniforms; shave beard, trim moustache; ban on religious marks and ear-rings; racial prejudice. Immediate cause: June 1806 – "Agnew's turban" (European-hat-like, with a cross badge).
  • 10 July 1806 (early morning) – sepoys of 1st and 23rd Regiments; Colonel Fancourt first victim; Fateh Hyder (Tipu's elder son) proclaimed ruler; Tipu's tiger-striped flag hoisted.
  • Suppression: Major Cootes informed Colonel Gillespie at Ranipet; 113 Europeans and ~350 sepoys killed.
  • Effects: new regulations withdrawn; Tipu's family sent to Calcutta; Bentinck removed. Failure – no leadership, poorly organised, divide and rule.
  • V.D. Savarkar: Vellore revolt = prelude to the First War of Indian Independence (1857).

The Revolt of 1857

  • Causes: economic exploitation (high revenue); doctrine of lapse, subsidiary alliance, effective control; annexation of Awadh; missionary conversion fears; abolition of sati/infanticide, widow remarriage, women's education seen as interference; sepoys paid less, no promotion.
  • Immediate cause: Enfield rifle cartridges greased with cow and pig fat, bitten before loading.
  • 29 March 1857, Barrackpur (near Kolkata): Mangal Pandey (Bengal Regiment) refused, shot his sergeant; executed.
  • 10 May 1857, Meerut – 3rd Cavalry freed comrades, joined by 11th & 20th Native Infantries; reached Delhi 11 May, proclaimed Bahadur Shah II Emperor of India. Spread to Lucknow, Kanpur, Jhansi, Bareilly, Bihar, Faizabad.
  • Suppression: Governor-General Lord Canning – forces from Madras, Bombay, Sri Lanka, Burma; diverted China-bound army; loyal Sikh army. Delhi recaptured by John Nicholson, 20 September 1857; Bahadur Shah II deported to Rangoon, died 1862. Colin Campbell took Kanpur; Nana Saheb fled to Nepal; Tantia Tope captured & executed; Begum of Awadh hid in Nepal; revolt fully suppressed by end of 1859.
  • Central India: Sir Hugh Rose occupied Jhansi; Lakshmi Bai joined Tantia Tope at Gwalior; British captured Gwalior June 1858; Rani killed – "best and bravest" leader (British historians).
